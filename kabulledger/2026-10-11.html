Four Iranian delegations came to Kabul this week. None of them came to discuss the 492,000 Afghans Iran has deported this year.

Iran is now Afghanistan’s largest trading partner in both directions. It supplies a third of what the country buys and takes a third of what it sells. That happened while Iran was at war, while its main shipping lane sat blocked, and while its police were putting Afghans on buses to the border.

Those facts are not in tension. They are one relationship seen from both ends. Afghanistan needs Iranian fuel, flour and electricity far more than Iran needs anything Afghanistan grows or digs up, and both governments can read the numbers. This week the weaker party was offered land at a port and a promise of power. It was also handed back half a million of its citizens.

I

The Week

Trade & transit

Tehran sent four delegations to Kabul in five days

Iran spent the week shopping, selling and surveying in Afghanistan. Four separate missions arrived, each with a different brief.

  1. Mon 5A parliamentary delegation led by Mohsen Zanganeh, who chairs Iran’s commission on supporting production.
  2. Tue 6About 180 traders and investors, led by Mahmoud Siadat of the Iran–Afghanistan Joint Chamber, for the Imam Abu Hanifa exhibition in Kabul.
  3. Wed 7Energy Minister Abbas Aliabadi, to talk about selling Afghanistan around 2,000 megawatts of electricity, plus generating and water-treatment equipment.
  4. Fri 9Zanganeh again, this time with the governors of Herat, Farah and Nimroz. Kabul promised to keep the Islam Qala, Abu Nasr Farahi and Nimroz crossings open around the clock.

The same day brought the week’s one concrete offer. Iran said it would set aside about 110 hectares at Chabahar for Afghan business: roughly 10 inside the port, 100 in the adjoining free zone. Afghan officials had asked for more space in the port itself and were told the coast is short of land. Both sides talked about reaching $10bn in annual trade, which would be roughly triple what they do now.

Chabahar matters for a reason neither side advertised. It sits on the Gulf of Oman, east of the Strait of Hormuz, and is the one Iranian deep-water port a ship can reach without entering the strait. Hormuz has been largely blocked since the end of February. Iran’s main container port, Bandar Abbas, sits behind the blockage. Afghanistan, meanwhile, has had Pakistan’s crossings shut to its goods for almost a year. Two countries with matching problems have found each other.

Nothing was signed. The electricity remains an offer, as it was in September. What changed is the traffic: four delegations in five days is not a courtesy call.

Sources: Pajhwok Afghan News, 6, 8 and 9 October 2026; Ariana News, 9 October 2026; Al Jazeera, 4 October 2026; World Bank, Afghanistan Economic Monitor, August 2026.

Follow-up · Returnees & labour

Iran has deported 492,300 Afghans so far this year

The figure came out on 8 October, from the UN refugee agency. Between 1 January and 3 October, 581,600 Afghans crossed back from Iran. Of those, 492,300 were forcibly removed — 85 of every hundred. The rest went home by choice, or by something close enough to it.

Three weeks ago we reported 59,000 deportations from Iran in August alone, and the open question was what September would add. The answer is a continuing grind rather than a spike. Most of the year’s returns — 475,400 of them — have come since 28 February, the day the war with Iran began. Last year the pace was far higher: more than 1.9 million people came back from Iran in 2025, about 1.26 million of them deported. Slower is not the same as stopping.

The economic consequence is quiet and large. A worker in Tehran or Mashhad was sending money home; now he is standing in a labour market in Herat. The World Bank puts remittances at 15.7% of national output last fiscal year, down from 17.8% the year before, and attributes most of the fall to exactly this. Each deportation subtracts a wage earner abroad and adds a jobseeker at home, in a country where a labourer already finds work two days a week.

Sources: UNHCR report of 8 October 2026, via Pajhwok Afghan News and KabulNow, 9 October 2026; World Bank, Afghanistan Development Update, May 2026. Earlier: Kabul Ledger, 20 September.

Water & climate

Kabul now buys by the barrel what it used to pull from the ground

Asadullah Ahmadi pays at least 150 afghanis a day, about $2.35, to keep seven people in water. He has been doing it for two years. He is not unusual; he is simply counted.

Beneath Kabul, the water table has dropped nearly 100 feet in a decade. Roughly half the city’s boreholes are dry. One resident of Taimani has followed the water down past 700 feet. The arithmetic underneath all this is simple and unforgiving: the city pulls out about 44 million cubic metres a year more than rain and snowmelt put back, which is close to twice the sustainable rate. About one in five residents has a piped connection. Everyone else buys, digs, or carries.

That turns a shortage into a household expense, and the expense is the story. Poorer families spend up to 30% of what they earn on water. The UN treats 3% as the limit of affordability. Set Ahmadi’s bill against the going wage and the numbers stop making sense: an unskilled labourer earns 308 afghanis a day and finds work 2.1 days a week, which comes to about 2,800 afghanis a month. Ahmadi spends 4,500 on water.

Both engineering answers are stalled. The Shahtoot dam, which India agreed to pay for and Pakistan has always opposed, has never broken ground. A 124-mile pipeline from the Panjshir river, meant to serve about two million people, is still being surveyed. Kabul had under a million residents in 2001 and has roughly six million now. Prosper Global, the research group formerly part of Mercy Corps, thinks the aquifers could fail as early as 2030.

Sources: CNN, 6 October 2026; Prosper Global (formerly Mercy Corps), Kabul water analysis, April 2025; OCHA; WFP market monitor, August 2026. Figures on groundwater decline and the recharge deficit appear in both the Prosper Global analysis and UN reporting.

Money & banking

Trade with Russia is up by half again, and barely any of it comes back the other way

Figures released on 7 October put Afghan–Russian trade 150% higher in the first seven months of 2026 than in the same months of 2025. Russian deputy prime minister Alexei Overchuk and Afghan foreign minister Amir Khan Muttaqi spent the week talking about doing more.

The two governments cannot agree on the starting point. Russian customs recorded about $326m of trade in 2025; Afghan figures said $590m. Take the Russian number and this year lands near $815m. Either way the direction is the same, and so is the composition: Russia sends natural gas, petrol, diesel, wheat, flour and sunflower oil. Afghanistan sends back about $4m of raisins, dried apricots, pomegranates, cotton and melons. For every hundred dollars that moves, roughly one is Afghan.

This is the shape of nearly every partnership Kabul has announced this year. Belarus, Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Russia, Iran: the new friends are sellers. Afghanistan’s problem is not finding people willing to ship it things. It is paying for them, which is the subject of the next section.

Sources: Ariana News, 8 October 2026, reporting figures released 7 October; World Bank, Afghanistan Economic Monitor, August 2026. The two governments’ own 2025 trade figures differ by about 80%; both are given here.

II

How It Works

Who pays for the imports

Afghanistan buys about seven and a half dollars of goods for every dollar it sells abroad. Its banks cannot move money across a border. So how does the bill get settled?

Start with the size of the hole. In the last fiscal year Afghanistan imported $13.2bn of goods and services and exported $1.77bn. The trade deficit came to roughly 70% of everything the country produces — a ratio almost no other economy on earth posts. Every month, somebody has to hand foreign suppliers around a billion dollars.

The banking system cannot do it. Foreign banks have been reluctant to touch Afghan payments since 2021, less because of any specific prohibition than because the compliance risk is not worth the fee. Nearly $9.5bn of central bank reserves sits frozen abroad. The whole Afghan banking sector holds about $3.5bn in assets, which is less than a single month’s import bill. Lending to private business comes to 35bn afghanis, or 2.5% of national output. The World Bank counted 446 bank branches and 274 cash machines in the entire country.

So the money moves through hawala, the money-transfer network that predates all of this by several centuries. The mechanism is worth following step by step, because it is not a workaround bolted onto banking. It is a different way of settling debts, and it is doing the job of a national payments system.

  1. A Kabul importer wants a container of Russian flour. He walks into Sarai Shahzada, the money market, and hands a hawaladar afghanis in cash.
  2. The hawaladar telephones his counterparty in Dubai or Mashhad. That man pays the flour supplier out of dollars he already holds there.
  3. Nothing has crossed a border. What exists now is a debt between two money changers, recorded in two ledgers.
  4. They clear it against the next flow going the other way: a construction worker in Tehran sending wages to his mother in Herat, an aid agency funding a clinic, an exporter’s receipts for raisins. Each of those needs dollars turned into afghanis — the exact opposite of the importer.

Netting only works when the two directions are roughly equal. Afghanistan’s are not.

The fees tell you how efficient this is at home and how expensive it is at the border. A domestic transfer costs 200 to 300 afghanis per 100,000 — two or three parts in a thousand, better than most banks manage. Money arriving from abroad costs 4 to 6%, up from 3 to 5% before 2021. The gap between those two numbers is the price of being cut off.

Hawaladars also supply the credit that Afghan banks do not. Under an arrangement called ograyee, the hawaladar pays the foreign supplier up front and the trader repays in instalments as the goods sell. That is a trade finance line, offered on a handshake and a reputation, at a scale no Afghan bank could underwrite.

Now the strain. Offsetting claims works only if money flows both ways in similar amounts. With imports running seven and a half times exports, the Dubai and Mashhad side of the ledger steadily runs out of dollars. They have to be physically replaced — by remittances, worth about one dollar in six of everything Afghanistan produces; by aid; by UN cash flown into Kabul; by banknotes carried across borders in bags. The afghani’s stubborn strength, which this Ledger examined in September, rests on that same supply.

Which is why deportation and trade are the same story. When Iran sends half a million Afghans home, it does not only create unemployment. It thins the stream of dollars that pays for the flour, and it does so at the moment the import bill is unchanged. And it is why “transparent banking mechanisms” sat on the wish list Afghan traders published this week about Chabahar, alongside warehouses and visas. Every memorandum signed in Kabul over the past five days has to pass through a money changer’s ledger before it becomes a shipment.

What nobody knows. The volume. Afghanistan’s published accounts do not close: a goods gap of roughly $11bn against remittances and grants worth perhaps $5bn. The remainder is unrecorded — smuggled exports, re-exports booked elsewhere, customs valuations that bear little relation to invoices. Hawala keeps meticulous ledgers. It does not publish them.

By the Numbers

8.2 : 1

Imports to exports, August

Afghanistan bought $1,194.5m of goods in August and sold $145.6m. For every dollar it earned abroad, it spent eight.

Exports actually jumped 75% on July — grapes, pomegranates and melons moving in the autumn season, which is when this country earns most of its foreign currency. They were still 18% below last August, because the Pakistani crossings that used to carry the fruit are shut.

308

Afghanis for a day’s labour

About $4.70, and unchanged since July. The catch is how rarely the day comes: an unskilled labourer found work 2.1 days a week in August, 14% less often than a year before.

A month’s food for a family costs roughly 6,036 afghanis. On those wages the average labourer can buy half of it. In Nuristan, 17%.

31%

Of the UN appeal, funded

The 2026 humanitarian appeal for Afghanistan asks $1.71bn and has raised under a third of it, with 21.9 million people counted as needing help.

On 6 October the UN’s central emergency fund released another $18m. At the appeal’s own daily run rate, that is not quite four days.

IV

What to Watch

i

Whether this week’s talk produces a way to pay

The thing to look for is not another memorandum. It is a named banking arrangement: a correspondent relationship, a joint bank, a rial–afghani settlement window. Also worth checking in a month is whether Islam Qala and Nimroz really do run around the clock, or whether the promise lasted as long as the delegation. Without a payment channel, $10bn is a figure in a press release.

ii

The pine nut season, and whether a second buyer shows up

Last year Afghanistan exported about 7,500 tonnes of pine nuts for $95m, nearly all to Chinese buyers who, traders in Khost say, set the price and the terms. This year’s harvest is larger after the rain. Watch for two things: a buyer outside China, and any sign that the nuts are shelled and packed inside the country rather than shipped raw. The second is where the money is.

iii

Snow, not rain

Forecasters expect above-average precipitation in October and again in January, which is good news for winter wheat. Kabul’s aquifer needs something different: snow in the Hindu Kush that melts slowly and soaks in, rather than downpours that run off the hillsides and leave. Watch the snowpack through December, and treat a wet autumn as a harvest story rather than a water-table one.

About these numbers

Afghanistan has not held a census since 1979. Almost every national figure in this edition rests on a sample survey, a satellite image, or an administrative return that nobody independent has audited. Population totals vary by millions between sources. Trade data come from customs declarations whose valuations are widely thought to understate reality, and the figures published by two governments about the same shipments can differ by 80%, as Russia’s and Afghanistan’s do above.

Where sources conflict, both are given. Where a number is an estimate, it is called one. Prices and wages are monitored monthly in a fraction of markets and extrapolated; groundwater depths come from scattered monitoring wells; deportation counts record crossings observed at official points and miss the rest. The honest position is that the direction of travel in this economy is usually clear and the magnitude rarely is.
