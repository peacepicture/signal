Afghan farmers have just had a good year. Nineteen million Afghans will still go short this winter.

The harvest was strong. The rain came back, the wheat came in, and the shops have food in them. Then on Tuesday the United Nations published its new hunger forecast, and the numbers went the wrong way. More people are short of food than a year ago, and twice as many are in the category below that.

Both things are true because food in the market is not the binding problem. Money is. The average labourer found two days of work last week and earned 295 afghanis a day, about $4.50. That buys a quarter less than it did last October.

Meanwhile the government spent the week selling the one asset it cannot exhaust: the ground between other people’s countries. A power line, a railway study, a port plot. All of it is rent on geography, and almost none of it pays before 2028.

I

The Week

Prices & livelihoods

The harvest came in strong. The hunger forecast got worse.

On 13 October the IPC, the inter-agency body that grades food crises on a five-point scale, published its reading of Afghanistan. It found 15.9 million people — 32% of the country — in crisis or worse. Of those, 3.4 million are one step further down, at what the scale calls emergency.

The winter forecast is the part that matters. Between November and April, the IPC expects 19.2 million people in crisis or worse, 38% of the population. Five million of them would be at emergency level. That is nearly double the figure projected for this summer, and about half a million more than last winter.

Seven provinces are expected to tip into emergency as a whole: Daykundi, rural Ghazni, Badakhshan, Samangan, Sar-e-Pul, Badghis and Ghor. The malnutrition numbers move with them. Just over four million children under five will need treatment next year, 9% more than this year’s estimate, and the severe cases rise faster, from 942,000 to about 1.1 million.

None of this is a crop failure. FAO’s representative in Kabul, Richard Trenchard, called it a strong harvest and said it shows “what Afghan farmers can achieve”. The cereals are in the country. What is missing is the wage that buys them.

WFP’s market monitors, who collect prices in all 34 provincial capitals, put the problem in numbers. Unskilled workers found work two days a week in the first week of October, 14% less often than a year ago. The daily wage fell 5%, to 295 afghanis. Rice costs 31% more than last October, cooking oil 12% more, flour 10% more. Put days, wages and flour together and a labourer’s buying power is down 25% in a year.

Aid is not filling the gap and is not expected to. The IPC reckons humanitarian food assistance will reach about 1% of the population through the winter. Roughly a third of nutrition treatment sites have closed or been suspended. “This crisis is increasingly being defined by the gap between growing needs and shrinking funding,” said John Aylieff, WFP’s country director.

FEWS NET, the American famine early-warning service, published its own outlook the same week and reads the map slightly differently. It expects crisis conditions to persist in six named provinces and the big cities rather than nationwide, and it emphasises the good winter planting season ahead. The two agencies use the same scale. They disagree about how much the harvest offsets the wage.

Sources: IPC Afghanistan acute food insecurity analysis, 13 October; WFP and FAO joint statement, 15 October; WFP weekly market report, via Amu, 12 October; FEWS NET Afghanistan key message update.

Follow-up · Trade & transit

A year with the border shut, and the stand-in only works one way

On 14 October the government’s spokesman, Zabihullah Mujahid, said Afghanistan has made up the losses from the closed Pakistani crossings by trading more with Central Asia. The claim is testable, and it is half right.

Pakistan shut nearly a dozen crossings on 12 October last year after a round of border fighting. Five of the big ones — Torkham, Chaman, Ghulam Khan, Kharlachi and Angoor Adda — are still closed to goods. We last reported on this when Islamabad promised a reopening and then exchanged fire at Spin Boldak instead (27 September).

Start with what has genuinely been replaced. Kazakhstan is now Afghanistan’s granary. In the first seven months of this year the two countries traded $581.1m, and Kazakh grain and flour shipments rose by roughly half in volume. On 13 October ten Afghan companies signed five more contracts in Kazakhstan, worth $55m: $40m of wheat and other farm goods, $15m of flour.

Now look at the direction of travel.

A supplier is not a market. Central Asia has replaced the shop Afghanistan bought from. It has not replaced the customer Afghanistan sold to, and it has not replaced the road to the customers beyond.

The export side of the ledger is where the year shows. Afghan goods worth $6.7bn transited Pakistan in 2023; this fiscal year the figure is $367m. Container traffic fell from 42,959 in 2024–25 to 11,592. Fruit traders describe the detour in cents. A consignment to India used to cost about $0.25 a unit through Pakistan and take ten to fifteen days. Routed through Iran it costs $0.70 to $0.80 and takes two months. One Kabul trader is $350,000 down on this season’s apples and grapes, and owes money to orchards and money changers.

The state feels it too, in a line that is rarely quoted. Customs duties brought in 24.7% less this fiscal year to June than in the same months last year.

Sources: Afghanistan International, 14 October (Mujahid; Kazakh trade and contract figures); RFE/RL, 4 October (transit values, container counts, trader accounts); FreshPlaza, 15 October (freight costs); World Bank Afghanistan Economic Monitor, June 2026 (customs revenue).

Resources & energy

The transit line is nearly built. The buyer at the far end is Pakistan.

Afghanistan’s electricity utility said this week that the Afghan section of CASA-1000 is 70% complete. It expects to finish by the end of the Afghan year 1406, which is March 2028.

CASA-1000 is a high-voltage line that carries summer hydropower from Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan south through Kabul to Nowshera in Pakistan. It costs about $1.2bn and is designed for 1,300 megawatts: 1,000 for Pakistan, up to 300 for Afghanistan. Kabul also expects $60m to $65m a year for letting the power across, a payment known as a wheeling fee.

The participating countries met in Bishkek from 7 to 9 October and agreed, again, to hurry. Kyrgyzstan has finished its own stretch — 456 kilometres and 1,243 towers, done in November 2024. The Afghan line is the last big piece.

Two details complicate the announcement. The first is arithmetic. A year ago the Afghan section was publicly put at about 75% complete; this week’s figure is 70%. Nobody has explained whether the earlier number was optimistic or the job was re-measured. The World Bank’s January update expected the whole project finished by the end of 2027, which is three months earlier than the utility now says.

The second is the customer. The 1,000 megawatts and most of the fee come from Pakistan, whose crossings have been shut to Afghan goods for a year and whose air force bombed Kunar and Helmand on 1 October. Nobody has published a payment mechanism for a wheeling fee between the two.

Three days before the Bishkek meeting, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and the United Arab Emirates signed a memorandum to study a 900-kilometre railway from Central Asia to Pakistan’s ports. The route runs through Afghanistan. Afghanistan did not sign it.

Sources: TOLOnews, DABS statement (70%, March 2028, $60–65m); Trend, 14 October (Bishkek working group, Kyrgyz section, 1,300 MW split, World Bank timeline); Kun.uz and Ariana News, 3 October (rail memorandum).

Water & climate

The money for weather stations sits behind a recognition problem

Mullah Abdul Ghani Baradar, the deputy prime minister for economic affairs, spoke in Kabul on 13 October and asked for something modest: meteorological equipment, early-warning systems and rescue gear. He argued that Afghanistan emits almost nothing and suffers a great deal, and called the mismatch “completely inconsistent with the principles of international climate justice”.

The request is small and the obstacle is structural. The large climate funds do not hand money to countries. They accredit national institutions, and accreditation runs through a government that other governments recognise. Afghanistan’s entire book at the Green Climate Fund is $17.2m across one project, approved in August 2020 and run by the UN Development Programme, plus $1.6m of preparatory support. Nothing new has been approved since 2021.

The workaround is to route around the state. UN agencies apply, hold the money and do the work. FAO sought $10m and UNDP $8.9m from the Global Environment Facility on that basis. At the last public account, both were still waiting on its council. That design keeps funds away from the authorities, which is the point. It also means the ministry that would run a weather station does not get one.

Donors have their own reasons, and the clearest of them appears in the IPC report itself, which lists restrictions on women working as a driver of hunger. The standoff is real on both sides. What it produces, this winter, is a country facing an unusually wet El Niño season with very little ability to forecast it.

Sources: Pajhwok, 13 October; Green Climate Fund country page; Reuters via Arab News (the GEF route).

II

How It Works

Afghanistan has little to sell and a great deal to cross. This is how a country charges rent on its own map — and why the rent is harder to collect than it looks.

Afghanistan does not have much to sell. In June it imported goods worth $1.06bn and exported $78m. That gap is the central fact of the economy, and no harvest closes it.

So the government sells something it does not have to grow or dig up. It sells passage.

A transit fee is a charge for letting someone else’s cargo cross your country. The electricity version has its own name: a wheeling fee, paid per unit of power that travels down your wires on its way somewhere else. In both cases the seller is foreign, the buyer is foreign, and you are the road.

The appeal is obvious once you say it plainly. A toll needs no factory, no crop and no export standard to meet. It is paid in hard currency by people outside the country. And it grows with other economies rather than your own, which is useful when your own is shrinking per head.

Four things have to be in place before a single dollar arrives.

  1. A finished link. A line that is 70% built carries nothing at all; there is no partial credit in a circuit.
  2. A buyer who takes delivery. The fee follows the flow. No flow, no fee.
  3. A contract and a way to be paid under it. A banking system that cannot receive a wire cannot receive a toll.
  4. A government the counterparties will sign with. Three countries signed a memorandum this month about a railway through Afghanistan without Afghanistan in the room.

A toll needs no factory. It does need a customer who turns up.

Then there is the size of it. Sixty-five million dollars is about four billion afghanis a year. Set against the population the IPC assumes, that is roughly $1.30 a head annually. It is real money for a finance ministry and it is not a solution to anything.

Which brings us to the toll Afghanistan already collects, and which is much larger. Customs duties and fees at the land borders brought in 2.8 billion afghanis in June alone, and 12.4 billion in the fiscal year to that month — about a fifth of all government revenue. The state’s most reliable income is a charge on goods crossing a line.

That has an odd consequence. In most countries tax revenue rises when people at home produce more. Here it rises when the country buys more from abroad, and falls when a border shuts. Customs receipts are down 24.7% on last year. That is what a closed Torkham looks like in the accounts.

So the week’s announcements are one business in two forms. A customs duty and a wheeling fee are the same trick: charge for crossing. The difference is who pays. Customs is paid by Afghan importers and handed on to Afghan shoppers in the price of flour. A wheeling fee is paid by foreigners. That is exactly why Kabul wants the second kind, and exactly why the second kind is so hard to get.

And then the timing. On the utility’s own schedule the first wheeling fee arrives in 2028. The hunger forecast at the top of this edition runs to April.

It explains why the northern route has solved the import problem and not the export one. Kabul has found a shop, not a customer.

income per head, 2025

The economy grew about 4.8% that year. Income per person fell 5.6%, because the population grew faster and more than a million deported Afghans arrived to share it.

Growth and poverty are rising together. Any Afghan figure quoted as a national total needs dividing before it means anything.

IV

What to Watch

i

Whether the winter money appears

The IPC expects aid to reach about 1% of the population through the lean season. The 2026 appeal was roughly 31% funded in early October, after an $18m release from the UN’s emergency fund. Watch the next funding figure and whether WFP revises the five million it expects at emergency level.

ii

Which CASA-1000 gate moves first

The interesting thing is not the percentage. It is whether anyone publishes a payment arrangement for the wheeling fee while the Pakistani crossings stay shut, and whether the 70% figure gets reconciled with last year’s 75%.

iii

Anything moving north

Kazakh flour coming south is settled business. The test is traffic in the other direction: a real Afghan export contract into Central Asia, of any size, and whether the CASA railway study adds Kabul as a party rather than a corridor.

A note on the numbers

Afghanistan has not held a census since 1979. Every percentage of “the population” in this edition rests on a projection, and the projections disagree with each other by millions. The IPC’s 32% and 38% imply a country of just under 50 million; other estimates run several million lower.

The hunger figures come from household surveys of limited coverage, combined with price series, satellite imagery and expert judgement. They are estimates, carefully built, and they are not measurements. The wage and price series are collected by third-party monitors in the 34 provincial capitals, which means they describe towns rather than villages.

Trade figures from Kabul and from partner governments routinely disagree, sometimes by a factor of two. Where they do, this page gives the figure and names whose it is. The exchange rate quoted is the market rate WFP’s monitors observed, which runs close to but not exactly on Da Afghanistan Bank’s published rate.
