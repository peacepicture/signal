Afghanistan’s finance ministry began designing a stock market this week. The money that actually holds the country up arrives by hand, from men who are being deported.

A capital market is the top floor of a financial system. Afghan banks finance about a quarter of one percent of what Afghan firms invest, so the foundations are still in dispute. Meanwhile Afghans working abroad send home something near three billion dollars a year. That is roughly six times what this year’s United Nations appeal has actually raised. The flow is shrinking, for the simplest of reasons: the people sending it keep being put on buses home. Pakistan’s arrest tally for 2026 passed 86,000 this week.

Since last time

The winter money has not appeared. The 2026 humanitarian appeal still stands at 31.4 percent of the $1.71 billion asked, and shelter agencies this week requested a further $12.8 million to reach March. Nothing moved north, either. No Afghan export contract into Central Asia surfaced to set against the $8.2 million Kazakhstan bought in seven months. Goods did move west, by rail, which is a different story and appears below.

I

The Week

Four items
Money and banking

Kabul starts drawing up a stock market

The finance ministry held its first meeting on building a capital market on Thursday. Minister Mullah Mohammad Nasir Akhund chaired it. The commerce minister, Nooruddin Azizi, and the central bank governor, Haji Noor Ahmad Agha, sat in. A treasury official presented the design. The ministry said the Taliban leader had ordered the work.

A capital market is where companies raise money by selling shares and bonds to savers. The stated aim is to turn what the ministry called idle capital into investment in industry and infrastructure. No law, no regulator and no timetable were announced.

Here is the problem underneath. The World Bank’s 2025 survey of Afghan firms found that banks financed 0.28 percent of what those firms invested. Across the Middle East and North Africa the figure is 9.8 percent. In Afghanistan in 2014 it was 1.5 percent, so the number has fallen by four fifths. Firms named access to finance their single biggest obstacle. Women-led firms fare worse: 32 percent are fully credit-constrained, against 16 percent of firms led by men.

An exchange needs audited accounts, a securities law, a supervisor and savers willing to hold paper. It also needs banks that lend, because a company that cannot borrow rarely grows large enough to list. The meeting produced none of that. It produced a committee.

Sources: Pajhwok Afghan News, 22 October 2026; World Bank Enterprise Survey 2025, reported March 2026.

Trade and transit

Two trains, and a headline that needs correcting

A freight train left Linyi, in China’s Shandong province, on 21 October. It carried 8 million yuan of goods, a little over a million dollars: textiles, chemical feedstock, hand tools, construction machinery. The route runs through the Khorgos crossing into Kazakhstan, on through Uzbekistan, and over the bridge at Termez into Hairatan. Twenty days, if it keeps time.

Much of the coverage called it the first direct freight train from China to Afghanistan. It is not. A Chongqing service ran its first train to Hairatan in February 2025, fifty-five forty-foot containers, twelve to fifteen days. This is the first from Shandong. The distinction matters, because a route that already exists tells you something a brand-new one does not: the rail link works, and traders are using it.

Goods also went the other way. On 22 October the public works ministry said 600 tonnes of cotton had left for Türkiye on the Khaf–Herat railway, through Iran. It gave no value and named no exporter.

And Herat’s chamber of commerce said exports through Islam Qala reached about $124 million in the first half of 2026, up 72 percent on the same months last year. About a thousand lorries cross daily, the port commissioner said. Two cautions. A chamber of commerce is not a statistical agency. And the prior-year figure in the same announcement does not reconcile with the growth rate quoted, as Pajhwok itself noted. Take the direction as the finding and the number as a claim.

Sources: Pajhwok Afghan News, 21–23 October 2026 (citing Xinhua); Khaama Press, 23 October 2026; Belt and Road Portal, February 2025; DID Press, 21 October 2026.

Returnees and labour

Pakistan arrested 86,644 Afghans this year, then sent most of them home

The figure comes from UNHCR and the International Organization for Migration, published on 24 October and covering 1 January to 17 October. Of those arrested or detained, 82,526 held an Afghan Citizen Card or no papers at all. Another 4,118 were registered refugees or asylum seekers, who are not supposed to be picked up.

Balochistan accounted for 55 percent of cases. A single district, Chaghi, recorded 35,740 of them.

Returns ran to 812,131 from Pakistan this year, of whom 85,821 were formally deported. In the week to 17 October, 3,883 people crossed at Torkham, Chaman and Bahramcha. Arrests that week fell 47 percent and deportations 48 percent, which is a lull rather than a policy change. Since April 2025 nearly 1.9 million have returned; since September 2023, more than 2.74 million.

The mechanism is worth seeing plainly. Only about a tenth of this year’s returns were deportations. In an IOM survey in July, 96 percent of returnees from Pakistan gave fear of arrest as their reason for leaving. Arrest does the work; the bus is voluntary. The agencies add that their own arrest numbers are probably undercounts.

Sources: UNHCR–IOM joint flash update, 24 October 2026, reported by Amu TV; UNHCR Afghanistan returns data, 5 October 2026; IOM returnee monitoring, July 2026.

Aid and the state

Donors met in Doha to discuss life after aid

The Afghanistan Coordination Group met on 20 and 21 October. Qatar’s minister of state for international cooperation, Maryam Al Misnad, urged donors to shift from emergency relief towards economic recovery and private-sector work, and to coordinate better. The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation set out its own priorities: girls’ education, food security, counterterrorism. Qatar noted it has given or pledged about $150 million over a decade.

Nothing was decided and nothing new was pledged.

Recovery is the right destination. The timing is awkward. Three days later, the agencies that supply blankets and heating asked for $12.8 million to cover 102 priority districts from October to March, including cash for 198,832 people. The appeal they sit inside is 31.38 percent funded of $1.71 billion, with the hardest four months ahead.

Sources: Amu TV and Ariana News, 21 October 2026; OIC statement, 20 October 2026; Shelter and Non-Food Items Cluster, reported by KabulNow, 24 October 2026.

II

How It Works

The explainer

Wages earned somewhere else

Afghanistan’s largest dependable source of foreign currency is not aid. It is not mining, and it is certainly not exports. It is wages earned in another country and sent home.

The World Bank puts remittances at 15.7 percent of GDP in the 2025 fiscal year, down from 17.8 percent the year before. Nominal GDP was $20.3 billion. Multiply the two and you get roughly $3 billion. That is an estimate resting on an estimate, and we will come back to why.

For scale: the 2026 United Nations appeal asked for $1.71 billion and has raised about $537 million. Afghans working abroad send home something like six times what the international relief system has delivered this year. No donor conference arranges it. No one announces it.

It does not travel through banks. Afghan banks have almost no working relationships with banks abroad, so the wire transfer that moves money in most of the world is not available here. Instead a labourer in Tehran or Karachi walks into a shop, hands over cash and a name, and is given a code. The dealer telephones a counterpart in Herat or Jalalabad. The family collects afghanis, often the same day. The two dealers settle up later, against goods they are financing anyway. The fee is usually one to three percent. The IOM counts more than 900 firms and individuals doing this.

Now the part that matters this month. Roughly 15 percent of rural households receive remittances, and the money covers about a fifth of what they spend. For about 5 percent of the population it is the main income.

Consider one man on an Iranian building site. He earns Iranian wages and his family spends at Afghan prices. The gap between the two is the remittance. One worker abroad can keep a household of seven in flour.

Deport him and both halves of that arithmetic vanish at once. The wage stops. The household gains a consumer. And he lands in a labour market where the World Food Programme found casual labourers getting two days of work a week at 300 afghanis a day.

Two days at 300 afghanis is 2,400 afghanis a month, about $37. A 49-kilo sack of flour in Kabul costs 1,700. A month of his new work buys his family a sack and a half.

This is why the national accounts show remittances falling 2.1 points of GDP in a single year while 1.2 million people came home. Those are not two facts. They are one fact, counted twice.

It also explains why the loss is so hard to reverse. A donor can restart aid with a decision. A remittance cannot be restarted by anybody in Kabul, because it requires a job in a country that has just expelled the worker.

Aid can be switched back on by a decision. A remittance needs a job in a country that has just expelled the worker.

III

By the Numbers

Three figures
0.28% of firms’ investment, bank-financed

Almost every machine, lorry and warehouse in Afghanistan is bought with the owner’s own money or a relative’s. The regional average is 9.8 percent. This is the floor the proposed stock market would be built on.

World Bank Enterprise Survey 2025.

47% of the food basket a labourer can buy

A casual worker with full-time hours could afford less than half the standard food basket in September. He does not get full-time hours. Affordability ran from 16 percent in Nuristan to 62 percent in Baghlan.

WFP Countrywide Monthly Market Report, September 2026 (Issue 76).

80.3 afghanis a litre, diesel, September

Up 4.7 percent in a month, 14 percent on the year and 19 percent above the three-year average. Diesel runs the tube wells and the lorries, so the war against Iran reaches an Afghan wheat field as a fuel bill. Brent averaged about $102 a barrel.

WFP, September 2026.

IV

What to Watch

Next
  1. 01

    Whether the Shandong train arrives, and whether a second follows

    Due at Hairatan around 10 November. One train is a press release; a timetable is a route. Watch for a second departure and a published freight rate, which is what a trader actually needs to switch corridor.

  2. 02

    Whether the capital market acquires a law

    A securities market needs a statute, a supervisor and companies with audited accounts. Watch for any of the three, and for who would be permitted to list. A second meeting is not progress.

  3. 03

    What the Kunduz gold contract actually pays

    A contractor began work on 23 October at Sorkh Betel and Takht-e Qabad, in Qala-e-Zal district, under a five-year contract worth $22 million, with $500,000 pledged for local roads. The company has not been named and no royalty rate has been published. Both are worth asking for.

On the numbers in this edition

Afghanistan has not held a census since 1979. The population behind every per-head figure here — 47.4 million, the World Bank’s working estimate — is a projection, not a count. Prices come from market surveys in a sample of districts. Wages come from interviews with labourers. Returnee and arrest counts come from border monitoring that the agencies themselves call incomplete. GDP is modelled, and satellite imagery stands in for ground survey in much of the agricultural data. Where we have multiplied one estimate by another, we have said so. Where credible sources disagree, we give both and leave the disagreement visible.
