A Dubai property developer now holds thirty years of Afghan gas.

The contracts were signed in Kabul on Wednesday. Nobody has said when the first cubic metre will burn, or when the first dollar reaches the treasury. That gap — between a number announced at a ceremony and a number entered in the accounts — is the most useful thing to understand about Afghanistan's economy right now. This week it appeared four times. A thirty-year energy deal. A Pakistani bank that spent twenty years in Kabul and left without explaining why. One export line up by two thirds. And a salt plant in Ghazni that now opens three days in ten.

Since last time

We asked three things a week ago, and all three are still unanswered. The UN drugs office has not published the satellite dates, coordinates or error margins that Afghanistan's interior ministry demanded over its poppy survey, and the ministry has published no eradication data of its own. Delta International Energy, the Saudi company holding a $200m oil and gas contract in Herat and Badghis, has not been reported starting field work.

Nor has the finance ministry published half-year revenue figures. Its only announcement this week was a deputy minister visiting Kandahar customs to stress the importance of hitting the revenue target.

I

The Week

Resources and energy

A property developer takes thirty years of Afghan gas

Afghanistan's mines ministry signed two long contracts on Wednesday. Both went to the Azizi Group, a Dubai conglomerate best known for building apartment towers.

One covers the Jangal-e-Kalan gas field in Jawzjan, more than 3,000 square kilometres of the Amu Darya basin. The other covers 111 square kilometres of coal around Pul-e-Khumri in Baghlan. Each runs for thirty years. Mines minister Hedayatullah Badri put the investment at $275m and said the state would take 75% of the gas extracted, plus a 15% royalty on top. He expects the contractor to burn the gas for 1,000 megawatts of power, and about 2,000 jobs.

Those terms come from one outlet. Khaama reported the same ceremony and said the financial value was not disclosed; it also put the jobs at around 5,000 across both projects. Amu's summary of the official briefing carried the $275m but no revenue share at all. Three days later the deputy prime minister's office described the Baghlan side separately: five coal mines, 69.4 million tonnes in the ground, annual revenues it estimated at about $60m. It did not mention Azizi, or a signing.

Azizi is becoming the largest single name in Afghan energy on paper. In February it signed five generation contracts with the state electricity company — 845 megawatts, about $463m. In August it announced a deal with Shanghai Electric for 3,000 megawatts over four years. Its stated ambition is 10,000 megawatts. Afghanistan currently imports close to 80% of its electricity.

None of it is generating yet. Solar plants are reported under construction at Ghazni, Naghlu and Barikab. No Azizi plant has been commissioned, and no Azizi well is producing gas.

Ministry of Mines and Petroleum via The Kabul Tribune, Amu TV and Khaama, 4 Nov · Office of the Deputy Prime Minister for Economic Affairs via Bakhtar, 7 Nov · Bakhtar, 4 Nov
Money and banking

Bank Alfalah sells up and goes home

A Pakistani bank has left Afghanistan after twenty years. Bank Alfalah told its shareholders on Friday that it had sold its Afghan banking business to Ghazanfar Bank, a Kabul lender, and completed the transaction on 5 November.

The paperwork went through both central banks, the State Bank of Pakistan and Da Afghanistan Bank. Customers of the Kabul and Herat branches were told to move their balances or withdraw them by 2 November. Alfalah opened in Kabul in 2005 and offered both conventional and Islamic accounts.

No price was disclosed. Neither was a reason. The bank's notice does not mention sanctions, and nor does any of the coverage — but the exit was not sudden. A non-binding offer arrived in December 2025, and regulators gave preliminary approval in January. Nearly a year of quiet paperwork, and then a line to shareholders on a Friday afternoon.

What leaves with it is a connection. A foreign-owned bank inside Afghanistan is one of the few institutions that can see both sides of a border at once. Ghazanfar is an Afghan bank buying an Afghan branch network, which keeps the deposits in the country and moves the ownership entirely inside it.

Bank Alfalah notice to shareholders via Dawn, Ariana News and Khaama, 7 Nov · Pajhwok, 8 Nov
Trade

Pine nuts are selling. Almost nothing else is.

Afghan pine nut exports rose 69% over seven months, the chamber of commerce said on Monday. The crop earned $72m, against $42m in the same period last year. Most of it went to China, with smaller volumes to Europe and India.

That is an unusually good number in an unusually bad year. Last week the statistics office reported that total exports for the first six months of the Afghan fiscal year fell 17.4%, to $598.5m. Fruit and medicinal plants carried almost two thirds of what was left. The periods are not identical, so the figures do not net against each other cleanly — but a single tree crop adding $30m while the overall column drops $126m tells you how narrow the base has become.

The other trade number of the week came from London. Britain's business department published its annual factsheet on Friday: trade with Afghanistan fell 16.7% over the twelve months to the end of June, to £70m. Britain sold £57m and bought £13m. Of that £13m, vegetables and fruit accounted for £1.7m. Afghanistan is the United Kingdom's 166th-largest trading partner, below a tenth of one per cent of British trade.

Prices and livelihoods

In Ghazni, the salt plant runs three days in ten

A salt factory in Ghazni used to fill 200 to 300 sacks a day. It now fills 20 to 30.

Bashir Ahmad, one of its managers, says the workers are called in once every three or four days. Down the road a mineral water plant has cut its staff from about twenty-five to seven. A paint factory manager, Hamidullah Munib, wants the government to raise tariffs on imported paint. A carpet manager, Hussaindad Rezaei, says his weavers cannot find buyers for handmade wool.

None of them are asking for aid. They are asking for electricity, industrial land and customers — which is to say, for a domestic market large enough to buy what they make. Afghanistan imported $6.66bn of goods in the first half of the fiscal year and exported $598.5m. Somewhere inside that ratio is a paint factory that cannot compete with a container.

Amu TV, 3 Nov, interviewing plant managers in Ghazni · NSIA six-month trade figures, 27 Oct
II

How It Works

The mechanism

What a thirty-year contract pays this year

A mining contract is not a payment. It is a promise to spend money, and a timetable for spending it. The two are easy to confuse, because both are announced in the same sentence, by the same minister, on the same day.

Start with the arithmetic. $275m spread across thirty years is about $9m a year. Afghanistan has roughly 46 million people. That is twenty cents each, annually, and only if every dollar arrives on schedule.

What the state is actually buying into is a royalty. A royalty is a slice of what comes out of the ground — charged on output, not on profit, so the government gets paid whether or not the company does well. On the Jangal-e-Kalan gas field the reported terms are unusually generous to Kabul: three quarters of the gas extracted, and a 15% royalty besides. Both are shares of something that does not exist yet.

Because between a signature and an afghani in the treasury sit five separate thresholds, and a contract has to clear all of them.

Capital has to arrive — not pledged, spent, on roads and rigs and transformers. Something has to come out of the ground. It has to be measured by someone the state trusts. A buyer has to pay for it, in money, through a channel the state can see. And a collector has to bank the government's share instead of pocketing it.

Miss one, and the other four are worth nothing.

Coal was the one mineral that genuinely paid. Then its only customer shut the gate.

Coal is the worked example, and the warning. It was the single mineral that reliably earned hard currency: $9.9m of exports in June 2025 alone, almost all of it crossing into Pakistan. Taxes on coal exports may at one point have been the government's largest income line, according to a figure the Afghanistan Analysts Network cites with care.

Then Pakistan closed its crossings. Coal export earnings were zero in every single month from November 2025 through June 2026, the World Bank reports. The mines kept working, the royalty rate did not change, and the revenue went to nothing — because coal failed at threshold four. There was no buyer on the other side of the gate.

Now look at where mining sits in the state's own books. The World Bank's revenue tables have three lines: tax, customs, and non-tax. Mining does not get a line. When the Bank lists what drives non-tax revenue, it names road and transport tolls, passport and administrative fees, overflight charges, and railway and telecom receipts. Rocks are not on the list.

The last figure the mines ministry published for its own annual collection was 13.2 billion afghanis, announced in August 2022. For scale, the state collected 227.8 billion afghanis in ten months of the following fiscal year. Mining has been, on the only numbers anyone has published, a rounding error beside the customs post.

The announcement record explains why. In 2023 the government reported seven contracts with domestic companies worth $6.5bn. In November 2024, three major contracts worth an estimated $1.2bn. In January 2025, nine worth about $2.9bn. And eighteen small-scale contracts with Afghan firms across 2025, worth about $24m in total.

The small ones are the ones that became holes in the ground. The Afghanistan Analysts Network, reviewing the sequence, concluded that the real impact and timeline of the promised investments "are however difficult to ascertain." Afghanistan has been here before: Aynak's copper and Hajigak's iron were both signed for billions, more than a decade ago, and neither became a mine.

There is a deeper problem underneath all of it, and it is measurement. A royalty is a percentage of a number, and somebody has to count that number. Afghanistan's last proper national mineral survey was carried out by Soviet geologists in 1977. Production figures from working mines are patchy, and the coal trade in particular has long been reckoned in estimates rather than records.

Which brings us to the uncomfortable part. In the current fiscal year the revenue line that collapsed was customs, down 24.7% with the Pakistani border shut. The line that grew fastest was non-tax revenue, up 29%, driven by fees for administrative services. Afghanistan's state is funded, increasingly, by charging its own citizens for paperwork.

A thirty-year gas contract might change that. It would change it in year fifteen or year twenty, after the capital, the wells, the pipeline, the power plant, the meter and the buyer. This week it changed nothing, and that is not a criticism of the contract. It is just what a contract is.

III

By the Numbers

$512.9m
Raised against a $1.71bn appeal

The UN's monitoring report, reported on Tuesday, covers the first eight months of the year. For every three dollars the UN said it needed, it has one. Shelter is the worst funded at 6% of its $160m ask, with winter arriving; water and sanitation stands at 12%. The one line that is over-subscribed is multipurpose cash, at 149% of a small target — donors prefer handing out money to building things. A caution: the UN's own country page displays the appeal as about 9% funded, with a $1.6bn gap. Both figures cannot be right, and neither carries a clear as-of date.

OCHA monitoring report to 31 Aug 2026, via Amu TV, 3 Nov · unocha.org/afghanistan, accessed 8 Nov
619,030
People deported to Afghanistan in 2026

Against 1,366,140 returns in total, so about two in five people who came back this year were made to. Iran did 547,100 of the deporting — 88% of the total — and Pakistan 70,500. The split matters because the two countries are doing different things. Pakistan mostly frightens people into leaving; Iran puts them on buses. UNHCR's latest count for Pakistan, to the end of October, is 724,600 returns with 71,300 formal deportations, roughly one in ten.

UNHCR and IOM, Afghanistan situation data to 24 Oct 2026 · UNHCR Returns Emergency Response #57, 6 Nov 2026
Zero
Months of coal export earnings, Nov 2025 to Jun 2026

Not low. Zero, in each of eight consecutive months, on the World Bank's reading of the trade data. Coal earned $9.9m in the single month of June 2025, when the Pakistani crossings were open. It is the clearest number in the Afghan economy, and the one that explains why this week's thirty-year coal contract should be read as a wager on Pakistan rather than on geology.

IV

What to Watch

  • Whether Azizi switches anything on

    The February contracts gave the solar plants a two-year build, which puts the first of them in early 2028. Nothing in the portfolio is generating now. The thing to watch is narrower than a ribbon-cutting: whether a published contract anywhere confirms the 15% gas royalty and the 75% share, since only one outlet has reported them.

    Check by 7 February 2027

  • Whether Pul-e-Khumri coal finds a buyer

    The deputy prime minister's office estimates $60m a year from 69.4 million tonnes. Spread over the thirty-year term that implies around 2.3 million tonnes a year at roughly $26 a tonne — our arithmetic on their figures, not theirs. It also implies a customer. Pakistan was the customer, and the crossings are shut.

    Check by 10 January 2027

  • What Afghan savers get from the Alfalah sale

    Ghazanfar Bank has bought a branch network and a book of deposits. Whether that produces any new lending is the question worth asking, in a banking system where commercial banks financed 0.28% of firms' investment. Also worth knowing: whether any bank with a foreign parent is still operating in Afghanistan.

    Check by 13 December 2026

On the numbers in this edition

Afghanistan has not held a census since 1979. Its population is an estimate, and published estimates this week ranged from 46 million to 48.6 million depending on which UN agency produced them. Almost every figure above rests on something other than a count: household surveys of a few thousand people, satellite imagery of poppy fields and cropland, border tallies collected at crossings by agencies that say themselves the counts are low, and ministry announcements that are not always reconciled with the ministry's own earlier announcements.

Three specific cautions for this edition. The World Bank's fiscal-year revenue totals do not reconcile with its own monthly table in the same document, a discrepancy it does not explain; the fiscal year's start was moved forward a month, which may account for some of it. The terms of the Azizi gas contract — the 75% share and the 15% royalty — come from a single outlet, and two other outlets covering the same ceremony report the figures differently or not at all. And the 13.2bn afghanis of mining revenue in the chart above is from August 2022, because it is the most recent such figure the ministry has published; comparing it to anything from 2026 requires some charity.

We say "estimated" when a number is estimated and "reported" when a single source carries it. Where credible sources conflict, both are given. Nothing here is a measurement unless it was measured.
