Afghanistan has been rerouted. The country now buys its flour, its fuel and its electricity through doors that did not matter five years ago.

Two of Afghanistan's three ways to the outside world are jammed. The southern route through Pakistan has been shut by war since October, and this week brought gunfire at Spin Boldak rather than the reopening Islamabad promised in August. The western route through Iran still works on land, but the sea at the end of it has been closed for 209 days. So goods come down from the north instead — by rail from Uzbekistan, by road from Turkmenistan, by wire and, soon, by pipe.

That is the week's real news, and it explains the rest of it. Kabul spent the week talking to Tehran about ports, to Ashgabat about gas, and to nobody much about Karachi.

I

The Week

21–27 Sept

Trade & transitPakistan promised to reopen the border. Then it shot at Spin Boldak.

In August, Pakistan said it would reopen all six main crossings with Afghanistan after roughly 300 days shut. No date was ever set. On 25 September, Taliban and Pakistani forces exchanged fire near Spin Boldak instead, each blaming the other.

The numbers behind the silence are startling. Afghan cargo transiting Pakistan fell to 11,592 containers worth $367 million in Pakistan's 2026 fiscal year. Three years earlier it was 102,886 containers worth $6.7 billion. Afghan exports moving the other way through Pakistan fell from $454 million to $7 million — not a decline so much as a stop.

The border closure of October 2025 finished the job, but it did not start it. Volumes had already halved twice before the shooting began, because Kabul was deliberately moving its business elsewhere.

Sources: Dawn (19 Jul 2026), citing World Bank Afghanistan Economic Monitor 2026; Amu TV (25 Sep 2026); KabulNow and Afghan Studies Center (Aug 2026).

Energy & diplomacyKabul spent the week shopping in every direction but south.

On 24 September the head of Afghanistan's Chamber of Commerce, Sayed Karim Hashimi, met Iran's ambassador Alireza Bikdeli to talk about rail links, port facilities and transit. Two days earlier, Turkmenistan's ambassador to Pakistan said gas would start flowing into Afghanistan through the TAPI pipeline in 2027. The Afghan stretch from Serhetabat to Herat is 153 kilometres long and about 80 per cent welded.

And on 19 September, Iran's energy minister offered Afghanistan up to 2,000 megawatts of electricity. That figure deserves a moment. Afghanistan generates roughly 250 megawatts of its own and imports about 800. Iran is offering, on paper, twice everything the country currently uses.

Notice what these deals have in common. Gas in a pipe and power on a wire need no ship, no port and no customs queue.

Sources: Pajhwok Afghan News (19 and 24 Sep 2026); Khaama Press (22 Sep 2026); Times of Central Asia on installed capacity and imports.

The stateThe books balance. Nearly half of what they hold goes to men with guns.

The World Bank's August monitor put Taliban spending at 113.3 billion afghani so far this fiscal year, about $1.74 billion. Security took 51.3 billion of that — 45.2 per cent. Education got 16.1 per cent. Health got 2.1 per cent. Agriculture, in a country where most people farm, got 1 per cent.

Domestic revenue came to 111.5 billion afghani, roughly 7 per cent of GDP, leaving a deficit of just 2.1 billion. That is the unusual part. This government balances its books. It simply collects too little to run a country of some 45 million people, and spends what it has on holding it.

Source: World Bank Afghanistan Economic Monitor, August 2026, reported by Amu TV, 24 Sep 2026. Dollar conversions at about 65 afghani.

AidThe money that used to fill the gap is two-thirds missing.

The UN asked for $1.71 billion for Afghanistan this year. By mid-September it had about 31 per cent of it. Aid agencies count 21.9 million people needing help and have narrowed their target to 17.5 million, because the money will not stretch. The World Food Programme can treat roughly one in seven malnourished women and children it identifies.

Norway added $1.63 million to the Afghanistan Humanitarian Fund on 21 September. It is a real contribution, and it is about one-thousandth of the gap.

Sources: OCHA via Afghanistan International (23 Sep 2026); KabulNow (5 Aug 2026); Pajhwok (21 Sep 2026).

II

How It Works

Trade routes

How a sack of flour reaches Kabul

A landlocked country buys nothing without a neighbour's permission. This year, two of the three neighbours who matter stopped giving it.

Start with the geography

Afghanistan has no coastline. Everything it buys abroad arrives overland, which means every import is also a transit deal with a neighbour. Understanding that one fact explains most of this year's economics.

There are three doors.

The southern door

This runs from Karachi up through Pakistan to Torkham or Spin Boldak. It is the shortest and cheapest way to a container port, which is why it carried most Afghan trade for decades. It is also a door with a lock on Pakistan's side, and Pakistan has used it. Since October 2025 it has been mostly shut.

The western door

This runs from Bandar Abbas or Chabahar on Iran's coast, inland to Islam Qala or Milak. On paper it replaced Pakistan. By May the World Bank found Iran supplying about 31.6 per cent of Afghan imports outright, with 56 per cent of all imports crossing Iranian soil.

Then the sea failed. US and Israeli strikes closed the Strait of Hormuz at the end of February, and Iran declared it shut on 2 March. Roughly one commercial ship a day now passes, against about 85 before, and war-risk insurance runs at forty times normal. A door on land is no use when the dock behind it is empty.

The northern door

This is the one now doing the work. Freight comes by rail from Termez in Uzbekistan to Hairatan, and by road from Turkmenistan through Torghundi. Afghan imports from Central Asia rose 43 per cent in 2025 to $2.4 billion. Exports the other way rose 77 per cent, though from a tiny base of $122 million. Uzbekistan ratified a preferential trade agreement in March 2026 that drops tariffs on fourteen categories of goods. Central Asia now supplies 80 to 85 per cent of Afghanistan's imported electricity.

What the switch costs

The northern route is long and has no ocean at the end of it. Goods that once sailed to Karachi and drove 200 kilometres now travel thousands of kilometres overland, or come by sea to a port that ships are afraid to enter. Every extra kilometre is diesel, drivers, tolls and time. All of it lands in the retail price.

Walk into a Kabul market this week and the flour on sale is Kazakh: 1,650 afghani for a 49-kilogram sack, about 34 afghani a kilo. The rice is Indian. The cooking oil is Malaysian, and it went up again this week.

A strait Afghanistan does not touch, closed by a war it is not in, sets the price of a sack of flour in Kabul.

Now put a wage beside it

A casual labourer in Afghanistan earned about 302 afghani a day in early September — roughly $4.70 — and found work about two days a week. A day's pay buys 10.2 kilograms of the cheapest flour, 14 per cent less than a year ago. A month of that work covers about half the cost of feeding a family.

So the chain runs like this. A strait is closed by a war. That raises the price of a sack of flour in Kabul. A man who works two days a week pays the difference. That is what "trade route disruption" means by the time it reaches a kitchen.

III

By the Numbers

What they mean
10.2 kg of flour per day's wage

Not a price and not a wage, but the ratio between them — the only figure that tells you whether a household eats. It is down 14 per cent in a year. The wage barely moved; the flour got dearer and the work got scarcer.

WFP market monitor, first week of September 2026
64.90 afghani to the dollar

The currency has strengthened about 5 per cent in twelve months, which sounds like good news and mostly is not. A currency rises when dollars are plentiful or when demand for them is weak. Afghanistan has some of the first, from aid and remittances, and a great deal of the second, because closed borders throttle imports and people are too poor to buy much. A strong afghani here is partly the symptom of an economy that cannot spend.

Da Afghanistan Bank and Kabul money market, 26 September 2026

The value of Afghan cargo that crossed Pakistan in its 2026 fiscal year, against $6.7 billion three years earlier. A 95 per cent fall in the cheapest link the country had to a seaport — and most of that fall happened before the border was shut.

IV

What to Watch

Next 90 days

October – JanuaryA wet winter, after four dry ones

FEWS NET expects above-average rain from October through January, after four straight drought years. Winter wheat goes in now. It is the best agricultural news Afghanistan has had in years, and it feeds nobody until next summer. The same forecast still has Crisis-level hunger running through January, spreading to more provinces from October.

Before winterWhether the southern door actually opens

Pakistan said in August it would reopen all six crossings. A month later there was shooting at Spin Boldak. Watch for a date, not an announcement. And watch whether Afghan importers go back if it opens — many have already rebuilt their supply chains northward, and rebuilding costs money twice.

End of DecemberTAPI's first Afghan section

The Serhetabat-to-Herat stretch is due to be finished by the end of the year, with gas flowing in 2027. If it runs, Afghanistan collects transit fees — the first substantial foreign-currency revenue in years that is neither customs duty nor aid. Turkmen gas has been promised since the 1990s. This is the closest it has come.

A note on the figures

Afghanistan has not held a census since 1979, so every per-capita number in this edition rests on a population estimate, and those estimates differ by millions. Household surveys are irregular and rarely national. Harvest and drought figures lean on satellite imagery. Wage and price data come from market monitors sampling a few dozen sites. Trade figures are often reported by the counterparty, because Afghan customs data is thin, and the two accounts rarely match. Afghan and Iranian officials put bilateral trade near $5 billion this year; World Bank data implies something smaller, and separates goods bought from Iran from goods merely passing through it. Where sources conflict we say so. Where a number is an estimate we call it one. Nothing here is a measurement unless we say it is.
