Poppy pays in cash, at the farm gate, on the day. Very little else in Afghanistan settles that easily.

The UN said on Thursday that Afghan opium production rose roughly fivefold this year, four years after the Taliban banned the crop. Two days earlier, the government's own statisticians reported that legal exports had fallen by a sixth. In between, in an exhibition hall in Kabul, the mines ministry showed a portfolio of some 350 mining contracts to visiting buyers from China, India and Iran.

Put the three together and a pattern appears. Afghanistan has things to sell and no shortage of people willing to sign for them. What it lacks is a way to be paid. That is this week's lesson, and it is the subject of the explainer below.

Since last time

The freight train that left Linyi, in Shandong, on 21 October is still crossing Central Asia. It is due at Hairatan around 10 November, and no freight rate for the route has been published.

The finance ministry has not reported a second meeting on its proposed capital market. One meeting, so far, remains one meeting.

Work began at the Qala-e-Zal gold concession in Kunduz on 25 October. The contractor is still unnamed and no royalty rate has been published. Accounts of the company's local development fund differ. Ariana News puts it at $200,000, against the $500,000 reported when the contract was signed.

I

The Week

Agriculture · 29 October

Four years after the ban, the poppy is back

Afghan farmers planted 43,300 hectares of opium poppy this year. Last year they planted about 10,200. The UN Office on Drugs and Crime published the figures on Thursday, and they describe the collapse of the most effective drug-control measure of the century.

Production rose further than the area did, because the weather was kind. A yield of 33.8 kilograms a hectare, up from 29, lifted the harvest from 296 tonnes to roughly 1,466. That is a rise of about 400% in a single season.

The geography has moved. Before the ban, poppy was a southern crop, grown in the irrigated belt of Helmand and Kandahar. This year three-quarters of it grew in the north-east, and most of that in Badakhshan. There the area under poppy went from about 6,600 hectares to nearly 29,500. Takhar rose from 274 hectares to about 3,400. Badakhshan is where the government's writ is thinnest. It is also where villagers have fought eradication teams, and where ACLED has recorded repeated armed clashes over mining sites. Helmand and Kandahar did grow again, to 3,500 and 1,560 hectares. But they are no longer the story.

The reason is arithmetic, not ideology. Dried opium sold for about $500 a kilogram this year, down from $570, but still five times the pre-ban price of under $100. At this year's yield, a hectare of poppy grossed something like $16,900. A hectare of wheat, at the 28 afghanis a kilo the World Food Programme recorded in October and an ordinary irrigated yield, grosses perhaps a thousand dollars. No agricultural extension programme closes a gap of that size.

The interior ministry rejected the survey. Its spokesman, Mufti Abdul Mateen Qani, told Pajhwok that cultivation was close to zero and that more than 26,000 hectares had been cleared. He asked UNODC to publish its satellite dates, coordinates and error margins. Other accounts of the ministry's own position put cultivation near 24,000 hectares and eradication at 22,000 or 23,000. The official numbers have been reported three ways in a week. That is part of the problem. UNODC says it cannot verify the eradication claim.

Trade · 27 October

The state counted its exports, and a sixth were missing

Afghanistan sold $598.5 million of goods abroad in the first six months of its current year, which ran from late March to late September. In the same six months a year earlier it sold $724.2 million. The National Statistics and Information Authority published the comparison on Tuesday: a fall of $125.7 million, or 17.4%.

Imports went the other way, up $116.3 million to $6.66 billion. The gap between the two is about $6.06 billion in half a year — roughly eleven dollars of imports for every dollar exported.

The composition explains why the gap is so stubborn. Afghanistan's exports are farm produce. Fresh and dried fruit came to $223.5 million, more than a third of the total, and medicinal plants to $136.9 million. Minerals, despite the contracts, earned $32.1 million. The imports are what an economy cannot make for itself quickly: $1.4 billion of fuel, $765 million of machinery and vehicles, $691 million of metal, and $554 million of wheat and flour.

The immediate cause of the export fall sits on the Pakistani border, shut to goods since October last year. Pakistan's own trade authority, cited by Dawn, puts bilateral trade down 74% in the last fiscal year, from $1.3 billion to $358 million. Afghan transit through Pakistan came to 11,592 containers worth $367 million. Before 2021 it was roughly 89,000 containers worth some $5 billion.

The government's answer is to make more at home. On Thursday the deputy prime minister for economic affairs, Abdul Ghani Baradar, opened the first phase of the Shorandam cement plant outside Kandahar. It makes 1,500 tonnes a day and is meant to reach 5,000, on an investment the mines ministry put at $113 million in September. Baradar described a shift from a consuming country to a producing one. Cement is a good place to start, since it is heavy, cheap and absurd to import. It is also a small fraction of $6.66 billion.

Mining · 28–31 October

A hundred thousand visitors, and 184 million afghanis

Afghanistan held its first mining exhibition this week. A hundred and fifty stands went up at the international exhibition centre in Kabul, and companies came from China, India and Iran. The ministry's spokesman, Humayun Afghan, said more than 100,000 people walked through over four days.

The minister of mines and petroleum, Hedayatullah Badri, used the opening to set out the size of the sector he runs. About 350 mining contracts are active, 44 of them large-scale. Roughly 180,000 people work in mining. The ministry has issued 1,510 licences for precious stones. Of those, 1,287 are in Kunduz, Takhar and Badakhshan — the same north-eastern provinces now growing most of the poppy.

The concrete result of the week was a gemstone auction held on the sidelines. Emeralds from Panjshir, rubies from Kabul, tourmaline from Kunar and kunzite from Nuristan sold for 184 million afghanis, about $2.8 million. Four memoranda of understanding were signed, all between domestic companies. None was named and no values were given. Iran's acting chargé d'affaires in Kabul, Ali Reza Bikdeli, attended. One exhibitor told TOLOnews he expected to sign a large talc contract with Iranian buyers.

Hold that against the pipeline on display. In September the ministry signed a $200 million, 25-year contract with Delta International Energy of Saudi Arabia. It covers seven blocks of the Kushk–Tirpul basin, 22,000 square kilometres across Herat and Badghis. Last Saturday it signed a $38.74 million, fifteen-year talc contract for four square kilometres at Woch Bandar in Nangarhar, with about 400 jobs promised. Minerals, meanwhile, earned the country $32.1 million of exports in six months.

The gap between a portfolio and a payment is the subject of this edition's explainer. For now, note which transaction actually cleared this week: the one settled in cash, in a hall, over four days.

Follow-up · Pakistan border

Karachi begins selling the cargo it would not let through

Pakistani customs have started auctioning Afghan traders' containers at Karachi port. Pajhwok reported it on Sunday, and traders confirmed it to the agency. The reports say hundreds of containers; there is no official statement, no count and no published value.

The background, briefly. Pakistan closed its crossings to Afghan goods in October last year, after border clashes. We reported on 18 October that Central Asia had replaced the imports but not the exports.

What has changed is the status of the cargo stranded by that closure. A container left at a port accrues storage and demurrage charges every day it sits. Once the charges and unpaid duties exceed what the goods are worth, customs authorities in most countries may sell them to recover the debt. A year of closure has quietly turned Afghan traders' stock into a liability that the port can liquidate.

The Afghanistan–Pakistan Joint Chamber of Commerce says more than 60% of the stranded goods have already moved. They went back to their country of origin, or on to the Iranian ports of Chabahar and Bandar Abbas. The remainder are tangled in fee and paperwork disputes. Afghan chamber officials called the auctions contrary to regional and international trade law. Earlier estimates put as many as 15,000 Afghan containers and shipments at Pakistani ports. Neither customs service has ever confirmed that figure.

Treat the detail with care. The reporting is secondhand, carried on agency feeds, and neither Pakistani customs nor the port authority has published anything.

Sources: Free Press Journal on Pajhwok reporting, 26 Oct · IANS, 26 Oct · Ariana News on Pakistani export losses, 23 Oct

II

How It Works — The Missing Correspondent

A payment from one country to another is not a journey. Nothing physically travels. What happens is that two banks change their records, and a third bank — one they both trust and both hold an account with — makes the change stick. That third bank is called the correspondent. Afghanistan, for practical purposes, does not have one.

The country's problem is not that its trade is illegal. It is that almost no large bank will keep the account through which the money would move.

What a correspondent bank does

Suppose an Iranian trader agrees to buy talc from a company in Nangarhar. His bank in Tehran has no relationship with the seller's bank in Jalalabad, and no reason to trust it. So his bank uses an account it holds at a large bank in a financial centre — Frankfurt, Dubai, New York. The Afghan bank holds an account at the same large bank. The correspondent debits one account, credits the other, and the payment is done. SWIFT is only the messaging system that tells the banks to make those entries. People often mistake it for the money.

Everything about cross-border trade rests on this arrangement. Letters of credit let a seller ship before being paid. Trade finance lends against the cargo. Bank guarantees are what a government demands before awarding a mining contract. All three need a bank in the middle that both sides accept.

Why Afghanistan lost it

After August 2021 the large banks withdrew. They were not ordered to. Most Afghan trade is lawful, and the United States has issued broad general licences permitting trade and aid. But a correspondent bank chooses its customers, and the arithmetic is lopsided. The fee on a payment is a few dollars. The fine for processing one payment that turns out to touch a sanctioned person runs into the hundreds of millions. So banks exit. The industry calls it de-risking.

The cost is measurable. The International Crisis Group found that one European bank needed forty to fifty staff to clear the legal paperwork for a single Afghan transaction. Some payments took months. It also records an Afghan bank that spent years trying to pay a United States software supplier $3 million, failed, and in the end moved the money as smuggled cash. Large banks went further and refused regional banks that kept Afghan business. So a bank in Dubai or Almaty is often no easier a route than one in London.

Why the central bank cannot fix it

In a normal country the central bank vouches for its banks. It supervises them, publishes the rules, and submits to outside assessment so that foreign banks can see the standard. Da Afghanistan Bank cannot do any of that convincingly. Its governor, Noor Ahmad Agha, appears on a UN sanctions list. About $7 billion of the country's reserves were frozen in 2021. Half were moved into a Swiss trust in 2022, which has grown to roughly $4.2 billion. Nothing has ever been disbursed from it. Afghanistan's membership of the Asia/Pacific Group on Money Laundering went inactive in 2022. So there is no current external review of its anti-money-laundering regime for a foreign compliance officer to read. The Taliban say they will follow the rules and have asked for auditors to return. Donors tie that question to the restrictions on women, and so it sits.

What a banking system this thin can do

The result is a financial sector that holds the country's cash but barely lends it. Total banking assets came to $3.5 billion in December 2025, less than a mid-sized regional bank. Credit to the private sector was 35 billion afghanis in February 2026 — 2.5% of GDP, among the lowest ratios anywhere. The World Bank's 2025 enterprise survey found banks financing 0.28% of Afghan firms' investment, against 9.8% across the Middle East and North Africa. The World Bank's own summary is blunt: the banking sector intermediates only a small share of the payments in the economy.

So business is done the three ways the diagram shows. Cash arrives physically. The UN alone has bought and flown in more than $2.9 billion of banknotes since December 2021, which is why the afghani has stayed strong while the economy has not. Hawala settles claims between dealers and nets the difference later, cheaply and quickly, but only up to the volume that real trade flows can absorb. And a trading company in a third country buys the goods, resells them, and charges for carrying the risk a bank will not.

What this explains

All three substitutes share a shape. They suit transactions that are small, physical, repeated and settled close to the moment of exchange. That describes a lorry of pomegranates, a parcel of emeralds, a remittance from a building site in Tehran. It does not describe an eight-year exploration programme. That needs monthly payroll, imported drilling rigs, insurance and a performance bond. It needs a bank that will hold money in escrow and a lender that will advance against the asset. Those are exactly the services Afghanistan cannot buy.

Which is why the country's deal pipeline and its investment figures read like two different nations. It can sign a $200 million oil contract and auction $2.8 million of gemstones in the same autumn, and only the second will be completed on time. Until a correspondent relationship exists, the contracts will keep being signed and the money will keep arriving in suitcases.

III

By the Numbers

$598.5mSix months of exports

Everything Afghanistan sold abroad between late March and late September, according to its own statistics authority. Spread across a population the World Bank now puts above 51 million, that is about twelve dollars a head for half a year.

Imports over the same six months were eleven times larger. A country can run a gap like that only while something else pays for it — in Afghanistan's case remittances, aid and physically imported dollars.

$525mWhat the opium was worth, inside Afghanistan

UNODC's estimate of what this year's crop earned the Afghans who grew and traded it. That is about 2.7% of GDP, and 8.5% of everything the country's agriculture produces. It is not the street value of the heroin made from it abroad, which is many times larger. It is the money that stays.

Treat it as an estimate, and a soft one. At the $500 a kilogram UNODC quotes, 1,466 tonnes would gross about $730 million. The lower figure implies prices realised below the quoted average, or a narrower definition of who counts as inside the trade. Nobody audits this market.

$3.5bnThe whole banking system

Total assets of every bank in Afghanistan as of December 2025, on the World Bank's reading — loans, cash, buildings, all of it. For a country of more than fifty million people, that is roughly seventy dollars of banking per person.

The lending figure is starker. Credit to private borrowers was 2.5% of GDP in February 2026. In most middle-income countries the equivalent is between a third and all of GDP. Afghan firms finance themselves from retained earnings, family, and the hawala dealer.

IV

What to Watch

Late November

Whether either side shows its working on the poppy figures

The interior ministry has asked UNODC for its satellite dates, coordinates, methodology and error margins. UNODC says it cannot verify the 22,000 to 26,000 hectares officials claim to have cleared. Both demands are reasonable and neither has been met. It matters now because next year's planting decisions are being made this month, and the only unambiguous signal reaching a farmer in Badakhshan is a price of $500 a kilogram.

By February

Whether anything in the mining portfolio produces a bank payment

Two specific tests. Does Delta International Energy begin field work on its seven blocks in Herat and Badghis, eight weeks after the exhibition that advertised the sector? And is any foreign buyer ever reported paying an Afghan mining company through a bank rather than in cash or through an intermediary? The second would be the more significant event, and it has not happened yet.

December

Whether the state publishes half-year revenue to match the trade figures

NSIA has now given six months of trade. The matching number is government income, and customs duty supplies about a fifth of it. Duties fell 24.7% in the year to June with the Pakistani crossings shut. The half-year revenue figure is the one that shows whether a year of closed borders has reached the budget, or whether tariff rises and Central Asian imports have held the line.

How much to trust any of this. Afghanistan has not held a census since 1979. The population figure used above is a World Bank projection, and estimates in current use differ by several million. Household surveys are irregular and often cannot reach the places that matter most. Opium area is measured from satellite imagery and extrapolated through ground samples, with error margins the survey itself publishes and news reports usually drop. Trade data comes from customs posts with incomplete coverage and an informal economy running alongside. Banking figures, in the World Bank's own words, should be read with caution because the sources contradict one another.

Where sources disagree, we give both readings and say so. Where a number is an estimate, we call it one. Nothing here is a measurement unless it says it is.
