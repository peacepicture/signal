The afghani is one of the world’s strongest currencies this year. Afghans are hungrier than they were last winter.

Both things are true, and they are connected. Afghanistan buys about thirteen dollars of goods abroad for every dollar it sells. A gap that wide normally wrecks a currency. The afghani has instead gained around eleven per cent in a year.

The reason is that Afghanistan’s dollars are not earned. They are flown in, wired in and carried in — and money that arrives as aid behaves differently from money that arrives as payment. That is this week’s subject. Meanwhile Iran deported another 59,000 people home last month, and the fund that feeds them is a quarter full.

I

The Week

14–20 Sept
Returnees

Iran sent home 59,000 people in August alone

UNHCR published fresh numbers this week. Counted to 12 September, 522,200 Afghans have come back from Iran in 2026. More than 439,000 of them were deported rather than choosing to leave. August accounted for roughly 59,000 removals, up from about 53,000 in July.

Add Pakistan and the year’s total returns passed 1.18 million by the end of August. Some 86 per cent of those coming from Iran held no papers at all.

This is an economic story before it is anything else. Afghanistan’s output grew 4.8 per cent last year, which sounds healthy. Income per person fell 5.6 per cent over the same stretch. More people arrived than the economy could absorb. Most land in Nangarhar, Kabul and Herat, and join the queue for daily-wage work — construction, portering, brick kilns — that pays a few hundred afghanis when it pays at all.

UNHCR Afghanistan situation portal, data to 29 Aug 2026; UNHCR figures reported 18 Sept 2026; World Bank, Afghanistan Development Update, 26 May 2026.
Trade and transit

Pakistan has stopped being a trading partner

The crossings at Torkham and Spin Boldak closed on 12 October 2025, after fighting along the border. They have opened since — for aid convoys, and for deportees walking home. Commercial traffic has not come back.

The redirection is close to total. By June 2026, Central Asian routes carried 48 per cent of Afghanistan’s imports and Iranian routes 46 per cent. Pakistan’s share was near zero. India has meanwhile become the largest buyer of Afghan goods, taking $755 million in 2025 against Pakistan’s $505 million, on the Afghan commerce ministry’s count. Almonds, raisins and saffron now reach Mumbai by sea through Iran’s Chabahar port instead of by truck through Peshawar.

Torkham used to clear 500 to 700 cargo vehicles and 10,000 people a day. Pakistani traders put their own losses near $2 million a day. Islamabad’s interior minister said in August that the routes would reopen. No official confirmation followed. Kabul now says it wants guarantees the border will not simply shut again.

Figures for 2026 trade with Pakistan are disputed between Afghan and Pakistani sources; both accounts agree the volume is small.
Aid and hunger

The relief appeal is a quarter funded, and it is nearly October

The UN asked donors for $1.71 billion to cover Afghanistan this year. It has received 26 per cent of it. The World Food Programme says it needs $540 million to reach January, and can currently feed about two million people a month. Last winter it fed four million.

Twelve provinces have hit critical levels of child wasting, the sharpest and deadliest form of malnutrition. That is the highest number of provinces ever recorded in Afghanistan. Four in five of the severe cases are children under two.

The cruel part is that the harvest was good. The 2026 wheat crop was the first decent one after four dry years, and it did lift food supplies. But a harvest feeds the household that owns the land. Most Afghan families buy their flour rather than grow it, and for them a good year in the fields is mainly a slightly cheaper sack. FEWS NET still expects Crisis conditions — the stage at which households sell off assets to eat — across the central highlands, the east, the north-east, the south-west and the cities through January.

OCHA via IPS, 4 Aug 2026; WFP Afghanistan emergency page, Sept 2026; FEWS NET Afghanistan outlook, Aug 2026–Jan 2027.
Resources

Kabul offers its mines to Washington; Washington declines

Foreign minister Amir Khan Muttaqi spent this month inviting American money into Afghan mining, infrastructure and farming. The State Department replied that it has no plans to work with the Taliban on critical minerals.

The pitch leans on one famous number: a trillion dollars of minerals underground. It comes from US Geological Survey work published in 2010, and it estimates what is in the rock — not what anyone can dig up, haul out and sell at a profit. The copper at Mes Aynak, south of Kabul, has been about to open since 2008.

The obstacle is not only foreign. Mining revenue is contested inside the government itself, between the leadership in Kandahar and the Haqqani network in the south-east. Investors reading the geology should also read the org chart.

Asia Times, 5 Sept 2026 (updated 9 Sept); US Geological Survey mineral assessment, 2010.
II

How It Works

The plumbing

Why the afghani is strong and Afghanistan is not

A country that buys far more than it sells should watch its money lose value. Afghanistan’s has gained. Here is the machinery behind that.

Start with the puzzle in its plainest form. In March 2026, Afghanistan exported $78 million of goods and imported $1.04 billion. The gap was $961 million in a single month. In the same month the afghani closed at 64.2 to the dollar, almost eleven per cent stronger than a year earlier.

Normally that combination is impossible. A country short of foreign currency has to bid for it, and the price of dollars goes up. In Afghanistan the price of dollars has gone down.

The answer is that Afghanistan’s dollars do not come from selling things.

Where the dollars come from

Three pipes feed the country, and the biggest is aid — delivered, remarkably, as physical banknotes. Since December 2021 the UN has flown shrink-wrapped US cash into Kabul airport and deposited it with a private Afghan bank. The US inspector general counted more than $2.9 billion of it by January 2024. At the peak the flights ran near $40 million a week.

That cash cannot stay in dollars. The people the UN pays — clinic nurses, wheat suppliers, drivers, day labourers on cash-for-work schemes — are paid in afghanis. So the dollars get sold.

The second pipe is remittances, and they move by hawala. An Afghan working in Dubai hands cash to a broker there. A broker in Kandahar pays the equivalent to his family the same afternoon. The two brokers settle up between themselves later, often by netting off other trades. Nothing crosses the border. No bank is involved. It is the oldest money-transfer system still running at scale, and sanctions barely touch it.

The third pipe is the finance behind imports, which runs largely the same way.

Why the currency rises

Every aid dollar that lands has to be converted. That makes the UN a seller of dollars with no choice in the matter, week after week, regardless of the price.

On the other side, ordinary demand for dollars has collapsed. Wanting dollars means wanting to save, or to import. A country this poor does little of either. Households are spending what they have on flour and fuel.

Then the central bank leans on the scale. Da Afghanistan Bank auctions dollars most weeks, selling into the market to set the rate. It has banned foreign currency in domestic trade, so bread in Jalalabad is bought in afghanis, not Pakistani rupees. It restricts moving dollars abroad, and police seize cash at the border. Afghanistan has assembled a closed capital account out of decrees and roadblocks.

The result is a currency braced from both ends: a guaranteed supply of dollars arriving from outside, and a floor under demand for afghanis at home.

Why this is not good news

A strong afghani makes Afghan raisins, pistachios and saffron more expensive for the Indian buyers who have just become the country’s biggest market — exactly when those exporters need to win shelf space.

It makes imported wheat cheaper, which helps the family buying a sack of flour and hurts the farmer trying to sell one. And it is borrowed strength. Remove the aid and the arithmetic reasserts itself fast.

That is the thing to hold on to. The exchange rate is not measuring Afghan productivity. It is measuring the volume of foreign cash landing at Kabul airport, and the grip of a central bank that cannot use most of the ordinary tools of one — its reserves are still frozen abroad, and its banks are largely cut off from the international system.

Watch the afghani the way you would watch a pulse in an induced coma. Steady is not the same as well.

What to Watch

Next weeks
This month

The Security Council’s quarterly briefing on Afghanistan

Rabab Fatima, appointed head of the UN mission in July, delivers her first quarterly report. China and Russia keep pressing for Afghanistan’s frozen central bank reserves to be unfrozen, and the United States keeps declining. Read the language on banking channels rather than the language on politics. Whether Afghan banks can clear a payment abroad matters more to a Herat trader than any communiqué.

October to January

Winter wheat goes into the ground

Forecasters expect above-average rain and snow this winter, the first properly favourable season after the drought years of 2021 to 2024. Planting starts now. It will not pay much — sowing needs far fewer hands than harvesting, so the labour market stays slack through the cold months. But a wet winter is what decides next summer’s bread, and after four dry years the margin for another failure is gone.

Unscheduled

Whether the Pakistani border actually opens

Islamabad announced a reopening in August and then confirmed nothing. Kabul wants written guarantees against the next closure. Watch for loaded trucks clearing Torkham, not for announcements about trucks clearing Torkham. The two have diverged all year. If freight does resume, the interesting question is whether Afghan importers bother to switch back, having spent a year building routes through Iran and Uzbekistan.

A note on the numbers

Afghanistan has not held a census since 1979. Every population figure here is a projection built on older projections. Trade data comes from customs posts that keep closing. Food-security and price numbers rest on small household surveys, market visits and satellite readings of how green the fields look. Exchange rates come from a central bank that no external auditor inspects.

So we give the source and the date for every figure, name both sides where credible sources disagree, and label an estimate as an estimate. Read the numbers in this publication as the best available reading of a dial — not as a measurement.
