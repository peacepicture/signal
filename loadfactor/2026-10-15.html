Every autumn the Civil Aviation Authority goes through the books of a thousand British holiday firms. Its figures this year account for 993 of the 1,042 licences that fell due.

Start with the thing that makes this trade unlike most others. A touring company takes your money in November and delivers your holiday in June. For those seven months it is holding cash that is not yet its own, and spending some of it on next year's brochure. Every rule, every regulator and almost every collapse in this business comes back to that one arrangement.

The fortnight gave three views of it. The CAA counted which firms are still considered fit to hold customers' deposits. A private equity house sold another piece of its tour operator collection, nine years after buying the lot. And a Surrey operator's filed accounts showed £20.7m sitting in the bank against £1.48m of the company's own money. That gap is not a scandal. It is the business model, and Section II explains it.

I The Fortnight What moved, and why it matters
Regulation · CAA

The annual check on who may hold your money

On 7 October the Civil Aviation Authority published the results of its September licensing round. 1,624 travel businesses now hold an ATOL licence. Of the 1,042 licences that expired on 30 September, 951 were renewed. Another 42 were still in the application process, or had not yet met the conditions attached to them.

ATOL stands for Air Travel Organisers' Licensing. It is the permission a company needs before it can sell a flight-inclusive package holiday in the UK, and it is issued by the CAA. Renewal is not a formality. The regulator asks for audited accounts, management figures and forward projections, and it decides whether the firm has enough money behind it to be trusted with customers' deposits. It can refuse. More often it grants the licence with conditions, such as a bond or a cap on how many passengers the firm may carry.

Forty-nine licences are missing from the total. The CAA does not say what became of them, and the plain explanations are undramatic: a firm merged into another licence, stopped selling packages with flights in them, or simply did not apply. Some of the gap may be a counting artefact, since the stock of 1,624 holders and the flow of 1,042 renewals were not necessarily measured on the same day. What the number is not is a list of failures. It is a reminder that this register thins every autumn, quietly, and nobody publishes the names.

Two other figures are worth keeping. About 20 million passengers took an ATOL-protected holiday in the first eight months of 2026, flat to slightly up on last year. And the total number of licence holders has barely moved in three years: 1,607 in October 2023, 1,616 in April 2025, 1,624 now. Seventeen more businesses, across a period in which inflation, airfares and demand all swung hard. For a sector that is supposedly consolidating, the population of licensed sellers is remarkably stable.

One caveat for touring readers. Most coach and rail tours do not include a flight, so the companies selling them need no ATOL at all. The register is a window on one slice of the market, not a census of British tour operators.

Ownership · KKR

Nine years in, KKR is selling Travelopia a piece at a time

On 14 October Travelopia agreed to sell Experience Education, its British school travel business, to Rockaway Capital. The price was not disclosed. Completion is expected in the final quarter of this year, subject to regulatory approval.

The business being sold is substantial. Six brands go with it: Travelbound in study tours, SkiBound in school skiing, Edwin Doran and Masterclass in sports tours, World Challenge in expeditions, and JCA in outdoor residentials. Together they work with around 1,400 schools a year and take close to 100,000 pupils away.

Travelopia used to be TUI's specialist division. In 2017 TUI sold it to KKR, an American private equity firm, for £325m; the deal completed that June. A private equity firm buys companies using money raised from pension funds and other investors, reshapes them, and sells them on. The usual holding period it promises those investors is three to five years. KKR has now held Travelopia for nine years and four months.

It is not selling the whole thing. It is selling the parts. MyPlanet went to Kilroy Group. Gullivers Sports Travel went to Keith Prowse. In July 2025 Sovereign, Citalia and American Holidays went to TravCorp, and Travelopia said at the time that Experience Education, YMT Vacations and Sawadee were also for sale, so it could concentrate on its core. Earlier still, management had said it wanted to go from roughly fifty businesses to about ten.

Look at what the core turns out to be: Exodus Group, Quark Expeditions, TCS World Travel, Le Boat, Sunsail, The Moorings, Enchanting Travels, Europe Express and Kalos Golf. Expedition ships, charter yacht fleets, private-jet itineraries and bespoke long-haul. Those businesses own hard assets, or sell at prices high enough to carry a real margin.

The ones leaving are different in kind. School travel runs on volume, coaches, term dates and tight margins, and it is bought by committees rather than consumers. Both sorts can be good businesses. They are not the same business, and after nine years KKR has concluded it does not want to own both. Rockaway, for its part, says it intends to build “Europe's leading educational travel group” from a fragmented market, which usually means more purchases to come.

Accounts · Newmarket Holidays

Turnover up by nearly a third, and £1.5m of its own money at the end of it

Newmarket Holidays is an escorted touring and cruise operator based in Surrey, founded in 1983 and one of the larger British independents. Its accounts at Companies House for the year to 31 December 2024 show turnover of about £75.8m, up roughly £17.5m on the year before. That is growth of close to 30% in twelve months.

Two other lines matter more. The company ended the year with £20.7m of cash in the bank, up £4.4m. And its net assets — everything it owns minus everything it owes — stood at £1.48m, up £1.43m from almost nothing.

Put those side by side and the shape of a tour operator appears. There is fourteen times more cash in the account than there is company. The cash is not the company's. It is money paid by customers for holidays that have not happened, and most of it will leave again to pay hotels, airlines and coach firms. Net assets are what would be left for the owners if everything stopped on the last day of December and every bill were settled. At Newmarket, that was £1.48m on a £75.8m business.

This is normal. It is also the reason a regulator looks at net assets rather than turnover, and the reason an operator can grow fast and still be fragile. Section II works through why.

The ownership has changed recently too. In October 2024 Soho Square Capital, a London investment firm, backed what its own website describes as a minority buyout for existing shareholders. That phrasing matters: part of the money went to the founders rather than into the business. Jeremy Griffin, Toni Frei, Simon Hibbs and chief executive Niel Alobaidi remain the majority owners, and a chain of holding companies now sits above the trading company, which is how such deals are usually built.

Note the lag, because it is the permanent drawback of reading filings. These are 2024 figures, filed in June 2025. A further set covering 2025 reached the register on 14 July 2026 and runs to 26 pages, but the document could not be retrieved for this edition. Accounts are always history. They are also the only numbers in this trade that nobody has polished for a press release.

Demand · ABTA

Confidence slipped, and more holidaymakers went to a human being

ABTA presented its annual consumer research at the convention on 13 October. Its travel confidence index fell three points to 50, which is still the second-highest reading since the survey began. Some 88% of Britons took at least one holiday between August 2025 and July 2026, close to pre-pandemic levels.

The commercially interesting number sits elsewhere. 38% said they booked through a travel agent or tour operator in the past twelve months, up from 34%. Among 18 to 24 year-olds the share jumped six points, to 35%. The young are supposed to be the ones who never speak to anybody.

Their stated reasons are worth reading carefully. Ease of booking was cited by 60%, up from 56%. Value for money was cited by 44%, up five points. Value for money is not the same as cheapness: a packaged, protected, pre-arranged holiday costs more than assembling the parts yourself, and more people are now describing that premium as good value. They are buying certainty, and in a nervous year certainty sells.

The nervousness is in the same data. 15% intend to spend less on holidays next year, and 52% of them blame the cost of living, up from 47%. 32% intend to spend more, down from 34%. Meanwhile 63% of those going abroad booked a package, up two points.

For an operator this shift changes where the money goes rather than how much of it there is. A booking that arrives through an agent carries commission, a cost the operator can calculate exactly. A booking that arrives through its own website carries advertising, a cost that is far harder to pin to any single sale. The agent also keeps the relationship with the customer, which is the part operators mind about most.

Two cautions. This is a survey of what people say they did, not a count of transactions. And “agent or tour operator” lumps the two together, so a booking made directly on an operator's own site counts the same as one made in a shop. The figure tells you people are using intermediaries more. It does not tell you the high street's share.

II How It Works One mechanism, explained from scratch

Other people's money

A touring company spends most of the year holding cash it has not yet earned. How it is regulated, and how it fails, both follow from that.

Follow one booking. In November a couple reserve a river cruise leaving next June and pay a deposit of £300. The balance falls due about ten weeks before departure, so it arrives in March or April. The operator pays the cruise line on its own contracted schedule, the hotel after the night is used, the coach company monthly in arrears. For most of those seven months the couple's money sits in the operator's bank account.

Multiply that by every departure on sale and you get the central fact of the business. Most companies need working capital: cash to cover the gap between paying their suppliers and being paid by their customers. Touring has the gap the other way round. Customers pay first. Accountants call this negative working capital, and it is the nearest thing to free money in commerce.

The rule that follows

A growing operator takes in cash even while it loses money, because each new month brings more deposits than departures. A shrinking one runs out of cash even while it still looks profitable, because the departures it already sold must be paid for out of deposits that are no longer arriving.

That is why the balance sheets look strange from outside. Newmarket's £20.7m of cash against £1.48m of net assets is not a danger signal on its own. It is what the model produces. The question a regulator asks is narrower and harder: if the bookings stopped tomorrow, whose money would be missing?

Because the money is held, Britain makes operators protect it. There are four ways to do that, and which one applies depends on what was sold rather than who sold it.

ATOL is the cheapest by far. The operator pays the ATOL Protection Contribution, or APC, of £2.50 for every protected passenger aged two or over, into a pot called the Air Travel Trust. The CAA's own rules are strict about the detail: the charge falls due when the booking is made, is still payable if the booking is later cancelled, must reach the Trust within six weeks, and must never appear on the customer's invoice as a separate line. If a licence holder fails, the Trust refunds forward bookings and brings stranded passengers home.

A bond is a guarantee the operator buys from a bank or insurer and lodges with a trade body, which can call on it if the firm collapses. Coach tours and rail tours with no flight in them sit here. A trust account is the strictest arrangement: customers' money goes to an independent trustee and is released to the operator only as the holiday is delivered. It is the safest for the traveller and the most expensive for the company, because it takes away precisely the free working capital the model runs on.

Why operators argue about this

Every form of protection moves cash out of the operator's reach. That is the point of it, and it is also why proposals to tighten the rules meet such resistance. Protection and cash flow are the same argument seen from two ends.

So what does the CAA actually examine each September? Audited accounts, current management figures, forward booking forecasts, and whether the company holds enough of its own money behind the customers'. Net assets, in other words, which is why that unglamorous line in a set of accounts carries more weight than turnover. Where the regulator is unconvinced it does not usually refuse outright. It attaches conditions: a bond, a cap on passenger numbers, more frequent reporting.

The hole is exactly the money sitting with suppliers. Whether customers are made whole depends on how much of it comes back, and how fast. Today that is a negotiation with each hotel and airline, conducted by an administrator. From 6 April 2027 it becomes a right: a new regulation requires a supplier that cancels or fails to perform to refund the organiser within fourteen days. Section IV returns to it.

Finally, what failure looks like when it is not a catastrophe. Tripsmiths, a Woking firm selling escorted tours and unusual experiences, had its parent company put into administration in May. Its ATOL licence ceased on 6 August. The Travel Network Group bought the brand and the assets, and existing bookings went ahead under a Travel Trust Association arrangement. Nobody's holiday was cancelled. That is worth separating out: protection is a mechanism for getting money back, while continuity is a commercial decision taken by whoever buys the wreckage. The two are often confused, including by operators advertising how safe they are.

It is not a risk-weighted premium. A cautious operator and a reckless one pay the same per head, so the scheme's cost bears no relation to the danger each firm poses. Making that charge reflect risk is one of the things ATOL reform was meant to do, and has not. It is also not a charge on the traveller: the CAA forbids showing it as a separate line.

IV What to Watch Coming, and why it matters
6 April 2027

The package travel rules change, and almost nobody has noticed

Regulations made on 22 April this year come into force that day. Linked travel arrangements disappear as a separate category and fold into the definition of a package. A supplier that cancels must refund the organiser within fourteen days. And a company can no longer sidestep the rules by declaring itself a mere agent. The new terms apply only to contracts signed on or after that date, so two sets of rules will run side by side through the 2027 season.

November to January

Whether a three-point dip in confidence shows up in deposits

Peak booking season decides next summer. Two things to compare against ABTA's survey: how Trafalgar's new three-tier range sells over Black Friday and the new year, now that Insight and Costsaver have been folded into it, and whether operators report deposits taken rather than enquiries received. Confidence is a survey answer. A deposit is a fact.

Next six months

Whether KKR keeps selling, and Rockaway keeps buying

Rockaway says it wants to build the leading educational travel group in Europe, which implies more acquisitions in a fragmented market. The other half of the question is Travelopia's. YMT Vacations and Sawadee were put up for sale alongside Experience Education in 2025. If the brands KKR called its core start moving too, the nine-year hold is ending in earnest.

How to check this yourself

An ABTA membership. ABTA is the main British travel trade association, and its logo appears on most brochures. Go to abta.com, use the member search, and type either the company name or the membership number printed in the brochure and usually in a website's footer. The number takes the form of a letter and four digits, such as P8662. The search returns the member's legal name and the trading names its membership covers.

Three things to understand once you have found it. ABTA is a trade body, not a government agency: membership means a firm signs up to its Code of Conduct and arranges financial protection for the bookings that require it. For a coach tour, a rail tour or a cruise joined without a flight, that protection comes through bonding or insurance arranged under ABTA's rules, not through ATOL. And membership is not a quality guarantee. ABTA members do fail.

So use it as one of two checks rather than the only one. If a flight is included, look the company up on the CAA's ATOL register as well, and make sure the name taking your card is the name on one of those two lists.
