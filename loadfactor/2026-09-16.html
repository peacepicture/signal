A tour operator sells a seat on a coach that is leaving whether or not anyone buys it. Everything else in this business follows from that one fact.

This fortnight gave us a clean illustration. The Travel Corporation has now guaranteed that more than 2,000 of its 2026 departures will run regardless of how many people book them. That sounds like a marketing promise. It is actually a transfer of risk, worth real money, from the customer to the operator — and it tells you a great deal about who currently has the upper hand in this trade.

Elsewhere: Intrepid posts its biggest year ever, the adventure operators keep creeping up the age range towards the traditional escorted-tour customer, and a British regulatory reform that has been pending since 2021 continues, quietly, to not happen.

I The Fortnight What moved, and why it matters
Risk · Guaranteed departures

TTC has promised 2,000 tours will run. That promise costs money

The Travel Corporation has confirmed more than 2,000 definite departures for 2026 across Trafalgar, Insight Vacations, Brendan Vacations and Costsaver. A definite departure runs even if it never fills. Deputy chief executive Melissa DaSilva framed it as a service to travel agents, which it is.

It is also a bet. Traditionally, an operator publishes a departure and cancels it if too few people book, usually six to eight weeks out. That protects the operator and shifts the uncertainty onto the customer, who may have booked flights and time off. Guaranteeing the departure reverses that. The operator now eats the cost of running half-empty coaches, and in exchange gets bookings earlier from people who would otherwise have waited.

Operators do this when they are confident of demand, or when competition forces their hand. Watch which brands follow. If guaranteed departures spread across the sector, it means agents have enough leverage to demand them — and margins get thinner for everyone.

Results · Intrepid

Intrepid's biggest year, and a quiet move into owning things

Intrepid Travel reported revenue of $575m, up 29% year on year — the strongest year in its 37-year history, achieved against falling inbound travel to the United States and conflict in the Middle East.

The number worth noticing is not the revenue. It is that Intrepid has started buying property: a place on Tasmania's Edge of the Bay, a riad in Marrakech. Tour operators have historically owned almost nothing. They buy hotel beds and coach seats from other people, which keeps them light and lets them scale fast, but means every supplier takes a margin before they do.

Buying accommodation is the opposite trade. It locks up capital and adds fixed costs that must be paid in a bad year. Operators do it when they cannot get the beds they want at the price they want — which tells you something about how tight supply has become in the destinations that sell best.

Regulation · The one to watch

Britain's biggest unfinished travel reform is still unfinished

ATOL reform has been under way since 2021. The Civil Aviation Authority received 293 responses to its request for further information. A consultation on final proposals was promised for the end of 2023. It has not happened. The CAA now says the next consultation will be a joint one with the Department for Transport, and gives no date.

This is the least covered and most consequential item in the trade. At issue is whether UK operators may keep spending customers' deposits before those customers travel. Today, broadly, they can. Money taken in January for a September tour funds January's costs. That is the cash-flow engine of the entire British package industry, and it is why a sudden drop in bookings can kill an operator that looked solvent a month earlier.

The reform under discussion would push operators towards segregating or protecting that money. If it lands, the smallest and the most leveraged operators will feel it first, and some will not survive the transition. Five years of delay is not inaction — it is a measure of how hard the industry has pushed back.

Demand · ABTA

September is becoming the new August, and touring wins from it

ABTA's 2026 trends work finds a quarter of UK travellers now holiday in September, up from 17% in 2023. Among the over-65s the shift is sharper still: 35%, up from 22%.

For a beach operator this is a mild scheduling change. For an escorted tour operator it is close to free money. Touring is a shoulder-season business by nature — nobody wants a walking tour of Andalusia in August — and the fixed cost of a departure is the same in September as in July while hotel and coach rates are lower. Every passenger who moves from peak to shoulder improves the margin on a departure that was running anyway.

The over-65 figure matters most, because that is the core escorted-touring customer and the one most able to travel midweek and off-peak.

II How It Works The mechanism behind the fortnight

What one departure actually earns

To understand why a guaranteed departure is a big decision, you need to see how the money inside a single coach tour is arranged. It is simpler than you would expect, and stranger.

A tour operator's costs split into two kinds, and almost everything about the business follows from the split.

Fixed costs are incurred the moment the departure runs, whoever is on board. The coach and its driver cost the same with four passengers or forty. So does the tour manager — the person who travels with the group for the whole trip, and who is the actual product most customers are paying for.

Variable costs scale with each passenger: the hotel room, the meals, the museum entrances, the airline seat. And if the booking came through a travel agent, the commission — typically around a tenth of the price — behaves as a variable cost too.

Here is an illustrative ten-night European coach tour on a 40-seat vehicle. The figures are plausible rather than real; the shape is what matters.

Subtract the per-passenger costs from the price and each booking contributes £955 towards the £11,500 of fixed cost. Divide one by the other and the departure breaks even at twelve passengers out of forty.

Twelve of forty. That is a load factor of 30%, and it explains almost every commercial behaviour you will see in this trade.

Above twelve passengers, each additional booking is worth £955 of almost pure margin, because the expensive part of the trip is already paid for. That is why operators discount hard for early bookings, why they will quietly sell the last few seats at prices the early bookers would be annoyed to learn about, and why a tour running at 35 of 40 is enormously more profitable than one running at 25 — not 40% more, but roughly double.

The core idea

An operator is not really selling holidays. It is selling the marginal seat on a cost base it has already committed to. The last ten passengers on a coach are where the profit lives.

Now return to TTC's guarantee. Promising a departure will run means promising to absorb the loss when it does not reach twelve. On a single tour that loss is small — a couple of thousand pounds. Across two thousand departures, if even a tenth underfill, it is a seven-figure commitment made a year in advance.

They will have judged that the earlier bookings it generates more than cover it. That judgement is only correct while demand is strong. The interesting test comes in a soft year.

One caution before you take the model too literally: the £955 contribution is not profit. Out of it come brochures, advertising, the cost of acquiring the customer, head office, the finance team, the reservations staff, and the bonding and licensing the next section deals with. Operators talk about contribution because it drives the daily decisions. Shareholders eventually want the other number.

IV What To Watch Next fortnight and beyond
Autumn

Whether rivals match the guarantee

If other brands start guaranteeing departures, it has become a cost of competing rather than a marketing choice. That is a margin story, and it will show up in next year's accounts.

Ongoing

Any date for the DfT and CAA consultation

The single most consequential thing that could happen to UK operator cash flow. It has been promised and postponed for five years, so treat any announced date sceptically.

Late 2026

UK accounts filing season

Riviera Tours Limited's accounts to November 2025 were due at Companies House by 31 August 2026. Filed accounts are the only unspun numbers this industry produces.

How to check any of this yourself

Every UK tour operator is a registered company, and its accounts are free to read. Search the operator at Companies House, open the filing history, and download the latest accounts. Small companies file little, but you will still see turnover trends, staff numbers and whether the auditors raised anything.

Two cautions. Accounts lag reality by up to a year — Riviera's most recent published set runs only to November 2024. And the trading company is often one of several in a group, so the numbers may not be the whole business.
