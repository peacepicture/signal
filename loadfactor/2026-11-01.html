On Budget day the Treasury asked British tour operators a question nobody had put to them in forty years. Should the tax rule built for them exist at all?

Everything a tour operator earns lives in one gap: the space between the price on the brochure and the money paid out to hotels, coach firms and airlines. That gap is called the margin, and in Britain it has its own VAT rule, written in 1977 and in force here since 1988. On 28 October, hours after the Chancellor sat down, HM Revenue and Customs opened a review of that rule and put its abolition on the list of options.

The same afternoon produced a second, duller pressure on the same gap. The wage floor for April 2027 is now fixed, and it lands on holidays that were priced months ago and are already selling. Add a private equity owner testing the market for Britain's largest river cruise seller, and a set of filings showing how £21m in the bank can sit beside £27m of net liabilities, and you have a fortnight about one thing: who gets to keep the margin.

Since last time

Nothing moved on ATOL reform, and nothing moved on the package travel rules due in April 2027. Both were on the last edition's watch list; both are still waiting.

I

The Fortnight

15 Oct – 1 Nov
Tax

A forty-year-old VAT rule for tour operators is now up for abolition

HM Revenue and Customs published a call for evidence on 28 October, the day of the Budget. It asks whether the Tour Operators' Margin Scheme should be kept, reformed, made optional, partly withdrawn or scrapped. Responses close at 9.30am on 31 January 2027.

Start with what the scheme does. Most businesses charge VAT on everything they sell. A tour operator using the margin scheme charges VAT only on its margin — what the customer pays, minus what the operator paid for the beds, the coach seats and the flights. It cannot reclaim the VAT on those bought-in services, so the supplier accounts for it instead. The scheme was compulsory across the old European Community from 1977 and has applied in Britain since 1988.

The rate on that margin depends on where the holiday happens. Twenty per cent if it is enjoyed in the United Kingdom. Zero if it is enjoyed outside — which, since Brexit, includes the European Union. So a Rhine cruise or a fortnight by coach through Tuscany, sold from an office in Derbyshire, carries no UK VAT on the operator's margin at all.

That is why the review matters, and why the government's own language is striking. Leaving the EU, HMRC says, removed the technical need for the scheme. It goes further: withdrawing the scheme and the concessions attached to it "may have minimal impact" on the net VAT collected. It then invites anyone who disagrees to bring numbers.

Two places in that table are where the argument will actually happen. The first is the in-house element. If an operator owns the coach or employs the tour manager, that part was not bought in from anyone, so it falls outside the margin and gets apportioned by cost under HMRC's own notice. Leger runs its own fleet. Every operator that owns the metal does this sum annually, and a new rule would rewrite it.

The second is inbound. HMRC says it has seen more UK-based travel sold by overseas suppliers in a way that leaves no UK VAT behind, even for a trip taken in Britain, and that it "will be legislating to correct this distortion". That is a separate measure from the review, and it arrives whatever the review decides. A parallel Budget change already requires overseas suppliers to register for UK VAT on services sold to UK businesses that are not VAT-registered from April 2027, a measure the Treasury expects to raise £70m to £75m.

The paper runs to thirty-one questions and asks respondents to quantify what they claim. Operators have spent years asking for bits of the scheme to be restored, after the European Commission forced their removal. They are about to find out what else is on the table.

Sources HMRC, Review of the VAT treatment of travel services including the Tour Operators' Margin Scheme, 28 October 2026; BKL, Autumn Budget 2026 VAT measures; VitalLaw.

Ownership

Riviera's owner calls the bankers back

Silverfleet Capital has appointed the investment bank Harris Williams to review its options for Riviera Travel, including a sale. The Times reported it on 18 October. Talks are early, and nothing is agreed.

Riviera is the largest British seller of river cruises, with annual revenue above £255m, about a dozen ships on Europe's rivers and a plan to reach twenty-six within five years. Silverfleet has held a majority stake since 2017, when it bought the business from another private equity firm, Phoenix Equity Partners. In 2024 it hired a different bank, Baird, for the same exercise, and then chose to keep growing instead.

Private equity funds usually hold a business for three to five years. Nine is a long time, and the length tells you something about how hard touring is to sell. A buyer is not buying ships and offices. It is buying next year's order book, most of which has been paid for in deposits by people who have not travelled yet, and whose money the law requires to be protected. Diligence in this trade means reading a forward booking position and deciding whether to believe it.

There is a particular reason to read Riviera's carefully. Europe's rivers ran low through the summer, and low water is the one risk a river cruise cannot engineer away: the ship cannot pass, passengers go by coach, and the operator pays for both. Any buyer will be looking at how much of 2026 ended up on that bill, and whether 2027 has been repriced for it.

Sources Travel Market Report, 19 October 2026, citing The Times; M&A Insights; Phoenix Equity Partners.

Costs

Next April's pay rise lands on holidays already sold

From 1 April 2027 the National Living Wage for workers aged 21 and over rises to £13.21 an hour, from £12.71. The rate for 18 to 20-year-olds goes to £11.27, and the under-18 and apprentice rates to £8.31. The rise is 3.9%, and the government accepted the Low Pay Commission's recommendations in full.

A supermarket can pass that through at the till next spring. A tour operator cannot, because of the order in which it does things. Prices for 2027 were set in the middle of 2026. Brochures went out. Deposits have been arriving since the autumn, and the balances come in sixty or ninety days before each departure. The reservations team, the drivers and the UK-based tour managers who deliver those holidays will be paid next year out of prices fixed before anyone knew the rate.

The floor also matters to operators who pay well above it. It lifts the bottom of the pay ladder, and the rungs above tend to follow, because the gap between a new starter and a supervisor has to mean something. Employer National Insurance and pension contributions are percentages of gross pay, so they rise with it.

Three other measures from the same Budget touch this trade. Small business rates relief in England now reaches properties with a rateable value up to £18,000, from £12,000, lifting about 70,000 retail, hospitality and leisure businesses out of rates altogether and freezing bills in real terms for two years — help for a high street travel agency with a shopfront, not for an operator in an office park. From 2028 mayors and other English authorities may charge a levy on overnight stays, which raises the cost of touring in Britain. And Air Passenger Duty rises again: the economy rate for short-haul Europe goes from £15.00 to £15.49 next April and £15.97 in April 2028, with the main long-haul band moving from £102 to £105.33 and then £108.60.

The trade bodies were polite and unimpressed. Julia Lo Bue-Said, speaking for the UK Outbound Travel Group, welcomed the rates change but said the Chancellor had "missed a clear opportunity to back travel". ABTA's Mark Tanzer called the duty rise disappointing in a country that already charges among the highest departure taxes anywhere, and said members report customers waiting for clarity before booking. Joss Croft of UKinbound called the visitor levy timetable a "significant red flag".

Sources GOV.UK, National Minimum Wage rates; Employee Benefits, 28 October 2026; Travel Gossip; APD rates, 29 October 2026.

Companies House

£21m in the bank and £27m of net liabilities, both true at once

G Touring Limited, company number 04956006, is the business behind Travelsphere and Just You — two well-known British escorted touring brands, one for groups and one for solo travellers. It is owned by Bruce Poon Tip, who founded the global adventure operator G Adventures, and it files from an office in Market Harborough.

Its most recent readable figures are group accounts to 31 July 2024, filed in April 2025. Turnover was £40.56m, up £8.55m or 27% on the year. Cash at bank was £21.13m, up £2.61m. Net liabilities stood at £27.41m. It employed 81 people, nine more than the year before.

Those two balance sheet numbers look like a contradiction and are not. Most of the cash is other people's: deposits and balances for holidays that have not happened yet. Until the coach pulls away, that money is a promise the company owes, so it appears as a liability as well as sitting in the account. A touring business with a healthy forward book and a thin balance sheet will often show exactly this shape, and it is not the same as being insolvent. It becomes dangerous only when departures stop — the cash stops arriving, and the promises stay.

The file shows the owner acting on that. In March 2025 the company allotted shares taking its capital to £15,030,000, which is a shareholder putting real money in. Two new charges were registered in January 2024 and three older ones discharged, the signature of a refinancing. Then in May 2026 the company confirmed redundancies, blaming disruption from the war in the Middle East; a spokesperson said it was "streamlining certain areas of our operations".

Note the lag, as always. Accounts to 31 July 2025 were filed on 9 March 2026, but the document could not be retrieved for this edition, so the newest figures above describe a year that ended fifteen months ago. Free filings are the only unspun numbers this industry produces. They are also old.

Sources Companies House, G Touring Limited (04956006), overview and filing history; Endole for the 2024 figures; Harborough FM, 14 May 2026.

II

How It Works

AI and the shop window

An assistant that answers the question before the brochure arrives has not taken the booking. It has taken the shop window.

A woman in Shrewsbury wants three weeks in Japan with somebody else doing the driving, the translating and the booking. For forty years she had three ways to find that holiday, and each one cost the operator a different amount of money to be found in.

What a customer costs

She could send for a brochure. That costs print, postage and a mailing list, and the trade's rule of thumb is that most brochures never produce a booking. She could search online, where the operator buys her attention by the click, whether or not she buys anything. Or she could walk into a travel agency, which costs nothing until she books and then costs commission — usually around a tenth of the price, paid to the agent for making the sale.

Add up what an operator spends to win one booking and you get its cost of acquisition. On a £1,295 tour with a £375 margin, spending £120 to find the customer is the difference between a good departure and an average one. This is why operators care so much about repeat customers: somebody booking their fourth tour costs almost nothing to acquire, and the margin on that booking is nearly all kept.

What changes

Now she asks an assistant instead. It reads, compares and comes back with three suggestions. The operator has not lost the booking. It has lost the place where the choice gets made.

That distinction is the whole argument, and it is easy to overstate in either direction. Start with how little is happening. Booking Holdings has told investors that large language models account for "well under 1%" of its room nights, and the analyst Richard Clarke of Bernstein puts AI referrals below 2% at both Booking and Marriott. WeTravel's 2026 trends report, drawing on 1.3 million travellers and more than 400 companies, found 14% of operators reporting more bookings from AI search tools, 8% reporting fewer, and 78% reporting no change at all.

Why the terms are being set anyway

Volume is not where the action is. The commercial plumbing is being laid now, and it is being laid by people who have not asked permission. Skift reported on 19 October that when it asked Meta's assistant for a New York hotel room, the assistant opened its own browser and chose between two booking sites without being told to. Google's agentic hotel booking keeps the hotel or the booking site as the merchant of record and passes along only a name, a payment, an occupancy and a property. The supplier gets the sale and almost nothing else: no email address, no reason the customer chose them, no way to sell them a second holiday.

For a hotel that is an annoyance. For an escorted tour operator it cuts closer, because of what the product is. A hotel night is a commodity that exists again tomorrow. A departure is dated, finite and perishable, with a minimum-numbers clause behind it, and its economics depend on knowing who booked and when — the booking curve this newsletter walked through a month ago. An intermediary with an incentive to keep shopping until the day of travel is corrosive to that. Hilton already expects assistants to reshop bookings until arrival. In a study by McKinsey and Skift Research, half of a thousand American travellers changed a confirmed stay after finding something better, and Skift Research found 55% would follow an AI tool to a cheaper deal.

Where it is actually earning money

Ask what operators have genuinely deployed and the answer is unglamorous: the back office, not the shop front. Intrepid's chief executive James Thornton told an aviation summit in July that the company runs an AI-powered round-the-clock chat service and is "getting really good results from it", and that its sales consultants use AI while looking things up for a customer. Club Med has a WhatsApp deployment that began in Brazil and now runs in 23 markets, where it says 47% of conversations are handled without a human, with a handover available.

Treat the published numbers carefully, because most of them come from people selling software. A panel at Travolution Connects in June graded travel's AI maturity a B−; one speaker, Sarah Ditton of the vendor Alyza, said the trade is "still measuring activity rather than impact" — and the same session heard that an unnamed river cruise operator lifted its conversion rate 89% in six months, a figure published with no baseline and no method. The scepticism and the claim arrived in the same panel.

What to actually watch

The useful number in the WeTravel survey is not the 14%. It is this: 34% of operators are already rewriting their content so AI tools can find and quote them, and 52% say they do not know how to begin. That gap is the distribution story. Being absent from the answer is not a slow decline in traffic; it is simply not being among the three suggestions.

Against that, an escorted tour operator holds things an assistant cannot supply. A guaranteed departure, which is a promise backed by the operator's own money. A tour manager who is a person. Customer money that is bonded or held in trust, which a software intermediary does not arrange. And the repeat customer, the cheapest booking in the business, who does not ask anybody for a shortlist because she already knows whose coach she wants to be on.

Sources Skift, 19 October 2026; Travolution on the WeTravel 2026 report; Travolution Connects 2026; CAPA, 28 July 2026.

III

By the Numbers

And what they don't mean
31 Jan 2027
When the margin scheme evidence closes

What it means The moment HMRC stops accepting answers to its thirty-one questions about the Tour Operators' Margin Scheme. Nine-thirty in the morning, to be precise.

What it does not mean It is not the date anything changes. There is no draft legislation, no start date and no stated preference. The government will "announce full proposals in due course". An operator pricing its 2028 programme next summer will still not know which tax rule it is pricing against.

£13.21
National Living Wage, from 1 April 2027

What it means The legal minimum hourly rate for a worker aged 21 or over, up 3.9% from £12.71. Roughly £910 more in gross annual pay for a full-time worker on the floor.

What it does not mean Not a 3.9% rise in any operator's wage bill. Most drivers and tour managers are paid above the floor, so across a whole payroll the effect is smaller than the headline. On the lowest-paid roles it is larger, because employer National Insurance and pension contributions are percentages of the gross and go up with it.

£255m
Riviera Travel's reported annual revenue

What it means Scale. It makes Riviera the largest British seller of river cruises, running about a dozen ships.

What it does not mean It is not profit, and it is nothing like a sale price. Revenue in this business is the full amount customers paid, including every hotel bed, ship berth and flight the company bought in and passed on. The margin is a slice of it, and a buyer pays a multiple of profit, not of turnover.

Whether Riviera's review turns into a sale

Silverfleet walked away from this in 2024. If a formal process starts, the interesting number is not the price but how the summer's low water on the Rhine and the Danube is presented to a buyer — as a one-off, or as a cost that now recurs.

Colophon

How to check this yourself · Reading the booking conditions

Every operator publishes its booking conditions, usually linked in small type at the foot of its website. It is the least read and most informative document in the trade, because it is where the company writes down who carries each risk. Three clauses are worth finding.

  1. The cancellation charge scale. A table of how much of your money the operator keeps if you pull out, by how many days before departure. This is the operator's protection against an empty seat, and it tells you how much of your payment stops being yours long before the coach moves.
  2. The minimum numbers clause. Most escorted tours reserve the right to cancel a departure that has not sold enough seats, with a notice period. An operator advertising guaranteed or definite departures has written that clause out, and has moved the risk of a half-empty coach onto itself.
  3. The surcharge clause. The circumstances in which the price can rise after you have booked, and the threshold above which you may cancel without penalty. Package travel law caps this; the brochure says how the operator applies it.

Read the same three clauses at two operators selling a similar tour, and you can see which one has priced risk into its brochure and which has left it sitting with you.
