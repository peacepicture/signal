ATOL reformstill no date

Three British touring companies published numbers this fortnight. Each one was really a statement about a year that has not happened yet.

September is when this trade finds out whether next summer is safe. The brochures are out, the deposits are arriving, and an operator can finally see how much of the coming year it has already sold. Saga put that figure in writing on 30 September. Leger Shearings described its version of it on 28 September. Great Rail Journeys filed accounts in the summer that show what a good one looks like after the fact.

Read together, they say something a single set of annual accounts never can. The money an operator reports this year was committed by customers last year. The number that decides next year is the forward book, and this fortnight it came with a warning attached: Europe's rivers ran dry in the summer, and the damage is showing up not in the accounts but in the bookings for 2027.

I The Fortnight What moved, and why it matters
Results · Saga

Saga publishes the number most operators keep to themselves

On 30 September Saga reported half-year profits that had roughly doubled. Underlying pre-tax profit reached £46.6m, up from £23.5m. Revenue rose 14% to £366.3m. Net debt fell by £70.4m to £429.1m, and the company raised its full-year guidance to £65–70m.

Those are the headlines. The useful part sits further down, in a table dated 27 September, showing how much of the full year was already sold on that day. Its ocean cruises were booked to a load factor — the share of available berths or seats that have been sold — of 91%, against 93% at the same point a year earlier. The per diem, meaning the average revenue earned per passenger per day, was £439 against £394. Fewer berths sold, each one worth a tenth more.

That trade is the whole story of Saga's year. In the six months to 31 July its ocean fleet carried 25.7 thousand passengers at 92% load factor and £440 a day, against 22.5 thousand at 94% and £391. Profit on that division rose 38% to £47.7m. Holidays, the escorted touring and hotel-stay business, more than doubled its profit to £6.6m on 29.4 thousand passengers.

Almost no British touring company tells you any of this. Saga does because it is listed, and the discipline is worth borrowing. A private operator can announce a record year in a press release and never say how much of the next one it has sold.

Operations · European rivers

The rivers ran dry in July. The bill arrives in 2027

Buried in the same Saga table is a seven-point hole. Its river cruises were booked to 81% for the full year on 27 September, against 88% a year earlier. Saga names the cause plainly: “disruption from the low river water levels in Europe”.

Europe's rivers fell through the summer, worst from mid-July to mid-August. On the Danube, Budapest came within a few centimetres of its 2018 record low. Viking later said more than half of its European river-cruise days were affected. The ships themselves are fine; they simply cannot pass under bridges or over shallows at those levels.

Operators had three options, and they chose differently. Viking ran pairs of identical sister ships on opposite itineraries and swapped passengers between them mid-route. AmaWaterways says it cancelled nothing. Riviera Travel took the hardest line: in early September it stopped selling Rhine and Danube departures altogether, in every market, until mid-September. It told customers what it would not do, which was “replace significant sections of your planned river journey with repeated, long-distance coach journeys simply to keep an itinerary running”.

That sentence is a commercial decision, not a kindness. Under the Package Travel Regulations — the UK rules that make the operator, not its suppliers, responsible for delivering the holiday it sold — a change big enough to count as a “significant alteration” gives the customer the right to accept it, take an alternative or cancel for a full refund. A coach is the cheapest way to keep a departure technically running. It is also the fastest way to turn a repeat customer into a claim. Riviera set its own thresholds instead: coaches capped at two hours each way, ship transfers at four, compensation for any complete day lost, pro-rata refunds if a cruise ended early.

Saga's figure shows what the summer cost the sector. Its river division's reported half-year was excellent — profit up 54% to £6.0m, load factor 92%, helped by a new ship. The damage is not in the accounts. It is in next year's book.

Filings · Leger and Great Rail Journeys

Two sets of accounts, two different kinds of good year

On 28 September Leger Shearings Group said 2025 turnover rose 3.1% to £79.8m while EBITDA — earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, a rough measure of cash the trading operation throws off — rose 21.3% to £6.1m. Turnover up a trickle, cash up a fifth. The Rotherham coach operator also reported 15 consecutive months of sales growth, its best-ever single sales day by more than 25%, and double-digit growth in forward bookings for both 2026 and 2027.

Growth like that does not come from carrying many more people. It comes from charging more, filling better, or cutting what each departure costs. Chief executive Liam Race is selling the first of those openly, pitching coach travel as “no flying, no airport drama, no hanging around wondering what's gone wrong” — a proposition that got more valuable on 8 September, when a NATS systems failure grounded British departures for an afternoon. A new brand, Lumina, arrives in 2027, the group's first since 2015.

Compare Great Rail Journeys, which filed accounts for the year to 31 October 2025 on 28 July. Turnover rose from £111m to £139.8m, up 26%, and pre-tax profit went from £7.1m to £18.5m. The York operator credited higher passenger volumes and higher prices together. Private equity firm Vitruvian Partners bought it in May 2025 from Duke Street, in a deal reported at around £200m.

One grew its margin on flat volume. The other grew both at once and got sold. Both are good years. They are not the same business model, and an operator's accounts will tell you which kind you are looking at long before a press release does.

A word on timing, because it matters every time we do this. Leger's group accounts, filed under company number 12346190, cover the year to 31 December 2025 and were filed on 15 July 2026. You are reading about them in October. Free UK filings are the only unspun numbers this trade produces, and they are routinely a year or more behind the business they describe.

The accounts are also not always the interesting page. Leger's register of people with significant control records that Kathleen and Ian Henry stopped being its controlling owners on 24 October 2024, replaced by Leger Shearings Group Topco Ltd, a company incorporated that August. On Topco's own register Ian Duncan Henry holds “more than 25% but not more than 50%”. A family that controlled the business outright now holds a minority of the entity above it. No press release mentions that. The filing index does.

Distribution · TTC

Trafalgar takes three names down to one

From 1 September The Travel Corporation began retiring Insight Vacations and Costsaver and moving everything they sold under the Trafalgar name. More than 600 itineraries across 75 countries are being reorganised into three price tiers — Saver, Classic and Premium — and go on sale for 2027 departures ahead of Black Friday. Existing itineraries, experiences and prices stay as they are. Deputy chief executive Melissa DaSilva described it as “removing layers”.

Insight Vacations is more than fifty years old. Companies do not scrap names like that for tidiness; they scrap them to stop paying for them three times over. Three brands means three brochure print runs, three websites, three sets of agent training, three advertising budgets chasing broadly the same traveller. Each one carries its own cost of acquisition — the total marketing and selling spend divided by the number of bookings it actually produced. Fold them together and that cost is paid once.

The saving is real. So is the risk. A brand is a promise a customer recognises before they read the price; a tier is a dropdown they meet after. Insight's buyers were not choosing “premium”, they were choosing Insight. TTC is betting that what those customers actually wanted was the hotels and the dinners, which survive the change, rather than the badge, which does not.

For agents it should be simpler, which is the stated point: one portal, one training path, one brand to pitch at three price points. It also removes a tactic. An agent who could previously move a hesitant client sideways from Insight to Costsaver now moves them down a tier inside the same brand, where the comparison is explicit and the discount is visible.

II How It Works One mechanism, explained from scratch

The booking curve

A touring company sells next summer this winter. The shape of that selling is the most closely watched line in the business, and almost nobody outside it has ever seen one.

Start with the calendar. A coach tour or a river cruise departing in July 2027 goes on sale in the autumn of 2026. The brochure lands, agents start quoting, and the first deposits arrive. Bookings then accumulate month by month until the coach pulls away, roughly nine or ten months later.

Plot that accumulation and you get a booking curve: the cumulative share of a departure's seats sold, measured against time remaining before it leaves. It rises slowly at first, steepens through the new year, and flattens in the final weeks when only late deciders are left.

Every operator watches its own curve obsessively, for one reason. The coach leaves on a fixed date. The curve tells you, today, whether it will leave full.

The rule that follows

A booking figure means nothing without a date attached. “80% sold” is excellent nine months out and a disaster nine days out. This is why Saga's forward numbers are stamped 27 September and compared with the same day last year, not with the end of last year.

Compare two curves and the diagnosis gets harder, because a gap has at least three possible causes and they demand opposite responses.

The curve may have shifted right. The same customers are coming, later, because the brochure went out a month behind schedule. Saga said exactly this about its Holidays division: bookings sit behind last year partly because the season launched later. Nothing is wrong, and discounting into a shifted curve gives money away for sales you would have made anyway.

The curve may have changed mix. Fewer bookings, each worth more. Saga's ocean book is two points lighter but a tenth dearer per passenger day, which on a cruise ship is a straightforwardly better outcome.

Or the curve may simply be lower, because demand went away. That is what low water did to European river cruising. Seven points behind on the same date, with the same launch timing and no offsetting price gain, is lost demand rather than late demand.

Who sells the departure changes the curve's shape as well as its height. Bookings taken through travel agents cluster early, around brochure launch, because an agent with a client in front of them closes the sale in the room. Direct and online bookings arrive later and respond to price. So an operator that sells mostly through the trade sees a steep early curve and pays commission — an agreed share of the sale price — on almost every seat. One that sells direct keeps the commission, carries the marketing cost itself, and waits longer to find out whether the coach will fill.

When a curve runs genuinely late, an operator has three moves. It can discount, which lifts the curve now and lowers where it finishes. It can guarantee the departure, as TTC did for more than 2,000 tours, which pulls hesitant bookers forward by removing their risk and taking it on itself. Or it can stop selling, as Riviera did on the Rhine, accepting an empty curve rather than a bad holiday.

There is a fourth consequence worth naming now and returning to another time. Every deposit on that rising curve is cash an operator holds months before it spends it. The curve is not only a sales forecast. It is the company's working capital.

III By the Numbers What each figure does, and does not, mean
81%
Saga river cruise, booked

The share of Saga's river-cruise berths sold for the full year as at 27 September, against 88% on the same day in 2025.

It is not a final load factor. Months of selling remain, and discounting can close part of the gap. It is also one company's book, not the sector's; Saga runs its own ships on a handful of rivers.

£6.1m
Leger EBITDA, 2025

Up 21.3% while turnover rose 3.1%. The gap between those two percentages is the year's real achievement: better prices and fuller coaches, not more of them.

EBITDA is not profit. It is struck before interest, tax and depreciation, and a coach fleet depreciates hard. Leger took on bank funding in 2024, so the interest line is real money the figure excludes.

£88.5bn
ABTA and UKinbound estimate

Travel's claimed annual contribution to the UK economy, up from £80bn, said to support 1.4 million jobs. Published 24 September.

It is not profit, not tax paid, and not a national-accounts measure. It is an impact study commissioned by the two bodies it flatters, it bundles inbound with outbound travel, and the headline arrived without its methodology. Treat it as advocacy with arithmetic attached.

On 31 July the group's holding company extended its accounting period from October 2025 to April 2026. A private-equity owner moving a year end usually signals a refinancing, a reporting alignment or a sale process. Whichever it is, the next accounts will cover eighteen months and compare with nothing.

Ongoing

Whether AI agents ever get tour inventory

At Skift's forum on 23 September Uber's Dara Khosrowshahi said he is negotiating with “basically every other agentic company” and that “no agentic company is going to replicate that content”. An operator that owns its coaches, guides and ground agents has the same leverage. One that resells other people's hotels has none. Note what is actually running: the largest deployment anyone will quantify is a hotel call centre, HotelPlanner's Reservations.ai, which claims about 500,000 calls a day at roughly 15% conversion — its own figures, in a sponsored article. No British escorted operator has published anything comparable.

How to check this yourself

The ATOL holder register. ATOL is the UK scheme that protects money paid for flight-inclusive package holidays. Every licensed firm appears on a public register: go to caa.co.uk, follow Check an ATOL, then Search ATOL holders, and type a company name or ATOL number.

The useful trick is comparing what you find with the name on the brochure. The register names the legal entity that holds the licence, its ATOL number and the trading names it covers. Operators routinely sell under names that are not the licence holder's, and some trade under a franchise or accredited body whose licence covers your money rather than their own. If the firm taking your card is not on the register, find out whose licence protects you before you pay.

Note its limits. ATOL covers flights. A coach tour to Europe with no flight in it is outside the scheme entirely, which is where bonding and trust arrangements come in — a mechanism for another edition.

About this edition

Load Factor is a fortnightly digest on the commercial machinery of escorted and guided touring: who makes money, how, and where the risk sits. UK-focused, with the global movements a British reader would otherwise miss.

Every technical term is explained the first time it appears. Figures are checked against a second source where one exists, and worked models are labelled illustrative rather than measured.
