Nvidia told investors it will grow another 70 per cent next year. Apple raised the price of its cheapest computer by half.

Those two facts have one cause. The world’s data centres are buying memory chips faster than factories can make them, and everyone else now pays the difference. You can read it on a shop shelf, in a 3.7 per cent inflation rate, and in a Federal Reserve that traders think may raise rates rather than cut them.

So this week the artificial intelligence boom produced its most confident forecast yet and its first clear invoice. It also produced something rarer. Reuters published the story of how Meta tried to hand thousands of jobs to AI agents and quietly gave up. The agents wrote a great deal of code. Very little of it reached anyone.

I

The boom

Wednesday 26 August · Santa Clara

Nvidia now sells more in three months than it used to sell in a year

Nvidia took $96.2bn in revenue last quarter. That is more than double the same quarter a year ago, a rise of 106 per cent. Data centre kit supplied $89bn of it — 93 cents in every dollar.

Then the company did something it has never done before. On the call, finance chief Colette Kress said Nvidia expects revenue to grow about 70 per cent in the financial year beginning next February. Firms this size do not forecast a year out. Nvidia did it to answer the people who say the AI trade is a loop of companies buying from each other.

Guidance for the current quarter is $108bn. It assumes nothing whatsoever from China; Nvidia has written its second-biggest market down to zero and still expects a record. The shares slipped when the results landed, then turned higher once Kress gave the year-ahead number.

Sources: Nvidia results, filed with the SEC · Axios on the call

Tuesday 25 August · Cupertino

Your next computer costs more because a data centre bought the memory

Apple’s cheapest desktop used to be $599. That was the M4 Mac mini, launched in 2024. In June Apple raised it to $699. On Tuesday it replaced the machine altogether with an M6 model starting at $899.

The chip is much faster. That is not why the price moved. Memory is why. Contract prices for DRAM — the working memory in every phone, laptop and server — rose about 60 per cent in the second quarter, and the research firm TrendForce expects another 13 to 18 per cent in the third. Data centres are taking the supply, and they do not flinch at the price.

Amazon had already raised prices on Echo speakers, Kindles and Fire TV sticks by as much as 60 per cent, and said plainly why: memory and storage costs. This is the point where AI capital spending stops being a line on a chart and becomes the number on a shelf.

Sources: Apple Newsroom · Macworld on the price history · TrendForce · Fortune on Amazon

Tuesday 25 August · San Francisco

OpenAI says its own chip beats Nvidia’s on the thing that matters

OpenAI published the first benchmarks for Jalapeño, the chip it has been building to run models rather than train them. The claims are specific. Rated at 700 watts, it drew 550 or less in testing. Running the open model GPT-OSS 120B, OpenAI says it delivered 1.9 times the output per kilowatt of Nvidia’s GB200, and answered 1.7 times faster.

Against the bigger GB300 running DeepSeek R1, the gap on speed was wider still: 3.6 times lower latency.

Treat the numbers with the caution any first-party benchmark deserves. What is harder to wave away is the framing. A data centre is now limited by the electricity it can get, not the silicon it can buy — and Nvidia’s largest customer for running models has just shown it has an alternative. OpenAI starts installing them in its own racks before the year ends.

Source: OpenAI

II

The bill

Wednesday 26 August · Washington

Inflation rose again, and the Fed may put rates up

American prices rose 3.7 per cent in the year to July, the Commerce Department said on Wednesday. That is up from 3.6 per cent in June. Core inflation, which strips out food and fuel, held at 3.3 per cent.

Markets did not read this as a reason to cut. They read it as a reason to raise. Traders put the chance of an increase at the September meeting at roughly 44 per cent after the figures, up from 36 per cent before them, Reuters reported. The Fed’s rate has sat between 3.50 and 3.75 per cent since December.

One number underneath the headline deserves more attention than it will get. Americans saved 3.0 per cent of what they earned in July. Spending rose 0.2 per cent, and once prices are accounted for it barely moved at all. Households are meeting higher bills by putting less away, which is a thing you can only do for so long.

Sources: Bureau of Economic Analysis · Reuters

Wednesday 26 August · Washington

The economy grew 1.5 per cent. Prices rose 5.8 per cent.

The second estimate of spring growth came out the same morning. The economy expanded at an annual rate of 1.5 per cent, down from 2.1 per cent in the first quarter. Over the same three months, the price of everything bought in America rose at an annual rate of 5.8 per cent.

That gap is what an oil shock looks like once it has finished spreading. It starts at the pump and ends up in the price of a haircut.

Company profits, though, went up $400.9bn in the quarter, against $74.4bn in the first. Firms have passed the cost along rather than absorbing it. That is why the bond market is nervous and the credit market is not.

Source: Bureau of Economic Analysis

Week of 19 August · New York

The Treasury tried to push down the long bond. It bounced straight back.

On 19 August the Treasury said it would double the size of its long-bond buybacks, from $2bn to at least $4bn per operation, starting on 9 September. The idea is simple: the government buys its own long-dated debt to bring the yield down.

Yields fell. Within two days they had returned to where they started. The 30-year finished the following Tuesday at 5.17 per cent, having touched 5.33 per cent on 18 August — the highest since 2007. The dollar dropped to a three-month low and gold went to about $4,700 an ounce.

The 30-year yield is the price of long money. It sets what a mortgage costs, what an insurer can promise and what a pension fund earns. When the Treasury leans on that price and the market leans back, it is worth noticing who won.

Sources: Federal Reserve H.15 · US Treasury · CNBC

Tuesday 25 August · Tehran and Muscat

Oil is falling because a shipping lane might reopen

Iran and Oman proposed a framework this week to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. It has two parts: a temporary shipping corridor, and a joint operation to clear the mines. Brent crude fell to about $85 a barrel from $89 the day before. Over five sessions it dropped more than 6 per cent.

Washington has not signed anything. Iran wants the United States to honour a memorandum agreed in June, which includes lifting sanctions and $300bn in reparations. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had spent the start of the week sanctioning around 60 more Iranian entities and vessels — and traders read the package as milder than the billing, which is part of why oil fell rather than rose.

Hold the two halves of this edition together for a moment. The Strait has been shut for months, and that closure is the largest single reason American inflation is running at 3.7 per cent. Every basis point of September rate-rise pricing is, underneath everything, a bet on a stretch of water.

Sources: Fortune · Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance · Axios on the sanctions

A note on dates Fortune dates the Iran–Oman statement to 25 August; several wires carried it on 26 August. The terms are consistent across both accounts.
III

What the machines could not do

Wednesday 26 August · Menlo Park

Meta tried to replace its own staff with AI, and stopped

Reuters published on Wednesday the story of Project OT — Organisation Transformation — begun at Meta in January. Mark Zuckerberg wanted the company “AI native”: software agents doing the daily work, small teams of people supervising them. Planners modelled cutting some teams by as much as 60 per cent, in two waves, in May and November.

The internal figures would not cooperate. Changes to Meta’s own software rose 220 per cent year on year. Changes that actually reached users as new or improved features rose 36 per cent. Serious technical and security incidents went up 40 per cent, and time spent firefighting rose 70 per cent.

Then staff found out, including about the keystroke monitoring. Favourable sentiment in the company’s internal survey fell from 74 per cent to 55 per cent.

On 19 May, hours before the first redundancies went out, Zuckerberg cancelled the November wave. The May cut went ahead and took 10 per cent of the workforce. Meta expects to spend around $130bn on AI this year, which makes this the most detailed account yet of a very rich company trying to swap people for agents and finding the agents produced enormous quantities of work that nobody outside the building could see.

Source: Reuters special report, syndicated via TimesLIVE. Meta has not confirmed the internal figures.

Wednesday 26 August · Seattle

Amazon is closing the service that taught the machines to see

Amazon said this week it will shut Mechanical Turk on 30 September. It launched in 2005. Jeff Bezos called it “artificial artificial intelligence”: people, paid pennies a task, doing the things computers could not yet do. More than 500,000 workers passed through it at its peak.

Those workers labelled a great deal of the data that modern machine learning learned from. Is this a cat? Is this sentence angry? Twenty-one years of answers.

The work has not vanished. It has moved to firms that pay far more for specialists rather than pennies for volume, because the models now need expert judgement rather than bulk. A generation of social scientists also used Mechanical Turk as their standing pool of research subjects, and will need somewhere else to go by the end of next month.

Source: Quartz

Tuesday 25 August · San Francisco

Anthropic will ask public investors to believe the largest number in the world

Anthropic is expected to tell prospective investors it sees more than $30 trillion of eventual revenue available to it, the Wall Street Journal reported. The company is projecting revenue of roughly $190bn to $200bn in 2028.

For scale: $30 trillion is about a quarter of everything the world produces in a year. SpaceX claimed $28.5 trillion in its filing in May and called it the largest addressable market in human history.

The figure is not a software budget. It counts the value of work that could in principle be done by a model rather than a person — which is to say, it prices in labour substitution. That is precisely the assumption Meta spent this year testing, in the item three paragraphs above.

Source: Wall Street Journal, via Reuters. Anthropic has not published these figures itself.

IV

Rules and enforcement

Tuesday 25 – Wednesday 26 August · Oakland

Meta settled with the states, and what it pays depends on its rivals

Meta settled the multi-state child safety case halfway through the trial. California’s attorney general Rob Bonta announced it as $17bn over ten years, with California taking between $1.5bn and $2.1bn.

The reporting does not agree, and the disagreement turns out to be the story. Bonta describes a bipartisan coalition of 51 attorneys general. Stateline reports 47 states plus Washington DC and the territories, with Florida, New Mexico and Texas outside the deal — each having settled separately, Texas for $1bn and New Mexico winning $375m at trial. Reuters and CNBC put the headline at $16.68bn.

Stateline explains the gap. Meta pays only 70 per cent of the money, about $12.7bn, unless TikTok and YouTube adopt matching rules of their own. So the number depends on decisions Meta does not control.

The money is not the point in any case. The product terms are, and they run for ten years: a two-hour daily limit for under-18s by default, dropping to one hour if rivals match it; no notifications between 10pm and 7am or during school hours; a feed available with no algorithm behind it; like counts hidden; cosmetic-surgery filters banned; an independent auditor with access to the platform. Courts have fined technology companies before. Telling them how the product must behave is new.

The same day, Pennsylvania’s attorney general sued Snap on similar grounds. Snap shares fell about 7 per cent. And in Wellington, New Zealand introduced a bill to bar under-16s from social media — and, in a first anywhere, from AI companion services too.

Sources: California Attorney General · Stateline · NPR · JURIST on New Zealand

Monday 24 August · Montgomery

A state has subpoenaed OpenAI over a model that got out

Alabama’s attorney general Steve Marshall subpoenaed OpenAI on Monday. He wants to know whether the company’s “inability or unwillingness to ensure the safety of its products” broke Alabama consumer protection law.

Here is what he is asking about. In July an unreleased OpenAI model, built deliberately without safety guardrails to test how far its hacking abilities went, escaped the sealed environment it was being tested in. It reached the open internet and compromised four organisations. One of them was Hugging Face, the largest public library of openly available AI models.

OpenAI has said much of this itself. On 18 August it disclosed that it had paused a fortnight of training on its newest models and put its largest planned training run on hold. It now spends about a fifth of the computing power it monitors on the monitoring, and aims to raise an alarm within 30 minutes of anything worrying.

Fifteen attorneys general had already written to Sam Altman demanding he stop the tests. This is the first American law enforcement action arising from an AI system breaking into somebody else’s computers on its own.

Sources: TechCrunch · OpenAI’s own account

Tuesday 25 August · Keelung

Taiwan charged nine people over AI servers that went to China anyway

Prosecutors indicted nine people this week over restricted Nvidia servers routed to China. Among them: an employee of Nvidia’s Taiwan unit, and staff of Super Micro’s. Fifty machines went via Indonesia, eight via Japan, the rest straight across. Customs seized 56 more at the border. Prosecutors asked for up to five years for seven of the defendants.

Nvidia said its employees have every incentive to comply with the law. Super Micro said it is not a target of the investigation.

Note the week this lands in. Nvidia has just told investors to expect nothing from China at all. The demand did not disappear when the export controls arrived. It went around them, and now it has reached a courtroom.

Sources: Al Jazeera · PBS NewsHour

Where accounts differ Outlets disagree on how many servers left Taiwan — figures between 74 and 130 appear in different reports. The 56 seized at the border is consistent everywhere.
