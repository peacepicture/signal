A barrel of Brent crude cost $117.64 on Friday. By Wednesday night it cost $93.52.

Nothing changed in the Strait of Hormuz, shut now for eight months. What changed was an American election. Voters handed both houses of Congress to the Democrats, the White House stopped talking about strikes, and traders decided the war was ending. Twenty-four dollars came off a barrel in three sessions.

That one price has been setting almost everything else this autumn. It is why euro-area energy costs 20.8 per cent more than a year ago. It is why the Reserve Bank of Australia went to an eighteen-year high on Tuesday — the day before the price broke. Central banks have spent six months tightening into a number that may now be walking out of the room. Underneath all of it, the other story of the year carried on at its own pace: memory chips have become so expensive that Apple, Qualcomm and Arm each said so in the same week.

The premium comes out

Two items
Follow-up · Oil and the strait

Twenty-four dollars came off a barrel in three days

Brent settled at $117.64 last Friday. On Tuesday it settled at $103.60. On Wednesday it lost another $10.08, closing at $93.52 — down 9.7 per cent in a session and about a fifth over three. American crude fell 8.9 per cent on Wednesday, to $87.56. Gasoline futures are 17 per cent below Friday's peak.

When we wrote about this on 29 October, the strait had been closed for 241 days and four tankers a day were getting through where eighty-five once did. None of that has changed. What changed is the language coming out of Washington.

Vice-President JD Vance said on Monday the two sides had "certainly made some progress". On election night the president told the Financial Times he had no immediate plans to attack Iran. Qatari mediators are circulating a draft that could restart direct talks within days. Iran's price has not moved: lift the naval blockade, lift the sanctions, release the frozen funds. One Iranian official says the strait could reopen within two days of that.

And the strait itself got more dangerous, not less. On Wednesday the Revolutionary Guard said four tankers using a forbidden southern route had struck mines and exploded. On Thursday it said another tanker hit a mine, a supertanker caught fire leaving the route, and so did the tug that went to help. These are Iranian claims, relayed by Reuters, and nobody has verified them independently. South Korea is sending maritime patrol aircraft and a bomb-disposal team.

Traders sold the risk premium this week. Somebody is still laying the mines.

So read the fall as a bet, not a fact. Phillip Nova's Priyanka Sachdeva put it down to profit-taking and the unwinding of long positions rather than the vote. Goldman Sachs has just raised its December Brent forecast to $105. Rystad Energy says flows through Hormuz are unlikely to return to pre-war levels at all. What the price did this week was stop pricing a wider war; it has not started pricing an open strait.

The reason any of this matters to a reader who buys no oil: American petrol averaged a record $4.24 a gallon over the summer quarter, and diesel reached $6.28 in election week. That is what the voting was about.

Elections · 3–4 November

Congress changed hands, and Wall Street had its best day in eighteen months

Democrats won control of both chambers of the United States Congress on Tuesday. Calls came in through Wednesday and dozens of House races were still undecided. Hakeem Jeffries, who has declared for speaker, says his party will finish with at least 255 seats. Mike Johnson will not stand for minority leader.

The market reaction was the largest part of the story. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at 53,356.12 on Wednesday, up 1,569.40 points or 3.03 per cent. CNN and others called it the best post-midterm session in 44 years; a trading desk note put it more simply as the best day since April 2025. The S&P 500 rose 1.58 per cent to 7,688.62, the Nasdaq 1.44 per cent to 25,736.51, the Russell 2000 of smaller companies 1.58 per cent to 2,856.96. Gold gained 3.38 per cent. Energy was the one sector that fell.

Two things were being bought. Cheaper fuel works like a tax cut for every household, and arrives faster than one. And a divided Washington is a Washington that passes less, which equity markets have historically preferred. Justin Buell of Continuity Group called the timing perfect: the vote "rescued the stock market from its bout of AI-driven volatility".

One caution belongs with it. The new Congress is not sworn in until 3 January, and as Kpler's Michelle Brouhard pointed out, Congress cannot lift the naval blockade or the sanctions in any case. Those are the president's to lift. On Wednesday he told reporters the war had cost him votes "because it drove the gasoline up a little bit short term", and added that he would do it again.

Elon Musk spent a record $474m on these midterms. Tesla shares fell 4.9 per cent before the open on Wednesday and SpaceX 5.2 per cent.

Rates · 3 November

Australia raised rates the day before oil broke

The Reserve Bank of Australia lifted the cash rate a quarter point to 4.85 per cent on Tuesday, the highest since 2008. Six of its nine board members voted for the rise; three wanted to hold. It is the fifth increase this year, in a cycle that began in February.

The statement was blunt. "Inflation remains elevated and recent data indicate that it is broadly based." The outlook "has also been revised materially higher", and inflation is not expected back at the top of the 2–3 per cent target band until late 2027. Headline inflation ran at 4.3 per cent in September. The bank's own forecast for the end of this year went up to 4.5 per cent from 3.6.

Then Governor Michele Bullock said something worth reading twice. "I want to be very clear that we are not tightening monetary policy because of the direct impact of oil prices on inflation." Central banks do not raise rates because petrol is dear. They raise them when dear petrol starts moving the price of a haircut, a sandwich, a delivery. "Broadly based" is the phrase doing the work in that statement, and it is the reason Tuesday's decision survives Wednesday's oil price.

The cost is already visible at home. House prices have fallen significantly, the statement says, and unemployment is rising faster than the bank expected in August. Wages are growing 3.4 per cent this financial year against prices at 3.7. Australians are taking a pay cut and a higher mortgage payment at the same time. "We are not looking to tip the economy into recession if we can avoid it," Bullock said.

The board also dropped its explicit promise of more rises, which UBS read as clearly dovish; it now expects a hold. Betashares' David Bassanese expects another increase in February. The ASX 200 closed 0.3 per cent higher and the Australian dollar eased to about 69.8 US cents.

Inflation · 4 November

Europe is back to four per cent, and one line explains it

Eurostat's flash estimate put euro-area inflation at 4.0 per cent in October, up from 3.8 in September and the highest since September 2023. Energy did almost all of it: 20.8 per cent more expensive than a year ago, against 18.9 per cent in September. Services rose 3.3 per cent, food 1.6.

The flash estimate does not publish contributions, but September's final figures did, and they are a good guide. With energy running at 18.9 per cent, it added 1.71 percentage points to a headline rate of 3.8. Energy is about a tenth of a European shopping basket and it is producing nearly half of Europe's inflation.

Which is why the week's oil price is a problem for the European Central Bank rather than a relief. The ECB held its deposit rate at 2.5 per cent last Thursday and markets price a rise in December, with up to three more by the end of 2027. It is being asked to set borrowing costs for a year in which the single biggest driver of its inflation has just fallen by a fifth in three days.

Jack Allen-Reynolds of Capital Economics notes that the knock-on from expensive energy into food prices has stayed very limited so far, and would only start to show if energy held near these levels. Across the member states, Belgium came in highest of the big reporters at 5.2 per cent. Belgium's own statistics office had published 5.26 the week before, a difference of method rather than of fact.

Markets · 2–4 November

Beijing has made its emergency loans permanent

China's central bank said on Monday, after meeting the securities and banking regulators, that it would lean harder on two tools built in 2024 for a falling stock market. One lets brokers and insurers swap shares and funds for government bonds they can actually sell. The other lends listed companies money to buy back their own stock.

The second is the real change. That loan was a temporary measure. It is now a permanent part of the furniture, and its rate has been cut from 1.25 per cent to 1 per cent. Beijing will lend a company money at 1 per cent to go and buy its own shares. Banks have signed about 440bn yuan of these agreements and paid out more than 280bn. Tuesday's swap operation drew applications from 31 firms for over 100bn yuan, roughly as much as the first two operations put together.

The reason is a market that has been falling for three months. The CSI 300 lost 8.5 per cent in October and has now fallen for twelve weeks in a row, about 15 per cent in all — its longest losing run on record. The STAR 50, where China lists its chip companies, is down about 40 per cent from its June peak, some $480bn of value. A Chinese semiconductor index fell more than 56 per cent over 84 trading days.

Wednesday was the bounce. The STAR Composite rose 9.90 per cent, ChiNext 6.40, the Shanghai Composite 2.16. An electronics index gained 10.91 per cent and a STAR semiconductor fund closed limit-up at 20. Turnover reached 2.05 trillion yuan.

The fund managers who handle Chinese household savings were careful about it. Guolian An called it the first volume-backed rebound off a bottom, with the confirming signals not yet in. China Europe Fund said a real turn needs new money and better earnings forecasts, and that a single day above 9 per cent may have spent the short-term upside. Caitong said the distance from a policy bottom to a market bottom still has to be proved.

The wider point is about where this selling came from. China's chip shares did not fall on Chinese news. They fell because investors in New York and Seoul started doubting what the global AI build-out will earn. That doubt is now a policy problem in Beijing.

Earnings · 2 November

Apple's new chief executive has a memory problem

Apple took $114.5bn of revenue in the September quarter, up 12 per cent and a record for that quarter. iPhone sales were $57.6bn, up 17 per cent. Services reached an all-time high of $31.6bn. Greater China grew 21 per cent, also a record. Earnings came to $2.10 a share, up 14 per cent, helped by about five cents of tariff refunds. The full financial year came to $479bn.

John Ternus, who took over from Tim Cook on 1 September, was hosting his first results call. The news he brought was in the guidance. Apple expects December-quarter revenue to grow 7 to 10 per cent, which would be its best December ever. It also expects gross margin of 45 to 46 per cent, against 47.9 in the quarter just gone. Two things take those two points: the cost of running Siri's new AI features, and the price of memory.

Ternus called the memory situation "unprecedented". Apple expects to pay more again this quarter, and more after that. Sabih Khan, the operations chief, said iPhone, iPad and Mac were all supply-constrained in the quarter. Apple raised prices in June and September, which covered part of the bill.

Apple's missing two points of margin are somebody else's margin. SK hynix earned 80 per cent on its last quarter. Samsung earned 55.

That is the whole memory story in one line. The Korean chipmakers sell a part that nobody building a phone, a laptop or a data centre can do without, and they are keeping four-fifths of what they charge for it. Apple can raise prices and absorb the rest. Intel told us last week that memory costs have made a cheap laptop close to impossible to build.

Elsewhere in the quarter: iPad fell 11 per cent to $6.2bn on supply and a hard comparison, and wearables fell 8 per cent to $8.3bn. The dividend is 27 cents a share, payable on 16 November.

Chips · 4–5 November

The memory bill reaches the phone makers

Qualcomm's handset chip revenue fell 26 per cent last quarter, to $5.14bn. Its car business grew 89 per cent, to $1.99bn. Total revenue was $10.32bn, down 8 per cent, and adjusted earnings of $2.17 a share were down 28. Guidance for the current quarter is $10.4–11.2bn of revenue and $2.15–2.35 of earnings. The shares fell more than 9 per cent after hours.

Cristiano Amon, the chief executive, pointed forward rather than back. "Fiscal 2027 marks the start of our non-handset revenue inflection, including the ramp of our Data Center business." Qualcomm is sticking to a $5bn data-centre revenue target for the year and announced a multi-generation agreement with Amazon.

Arm reported the same afternoon, had a record quarter, and still disappointed. Revenue rose 27 per cent to $1.443bn. Licensing jumped 50 per cent to $773m. Royalties — which are the business, the few cents Arm collects on each chip shipped — rose only 8 per cent, to $670m. Reported net income halved to $130m. The shares fell about 4 to 5 per cent after hours.

Jason Child, Arm's finance chief, named the reason: memory prices are squeezing shipments of cheap and mid-range phones. That is the same bill Apple is paying, seen from the other end. Apple can put $50 on a handset. The makers of a $150 Android phone cannot, so they ship fewer, and Arm's royalty is the first thing to notice.

Arm did raise one number. Its own data-centre processor, the AGI CPU designed with Meta, now has orders worth more than $2bn across this year and next, against the $1bn it first expected. First deliveries come in the quarter to March.

Asia sold the lot on Thursday morning. SK hynix fell 7.4 per cent and Samsung Electronics 5.7. In Tokyo, Lasertec lost 12.5 per cent, Advantest more than 11, and Kioxia 9.2 after its own weak guidance. Chinese chip shares went the other way, lifted by Beijing.

Deals · 2 November

Marvell bought a laser company for $115bn, and both shares fell

Marvell Technology agreed on Sunday night to buy Lumentum, entirely in shares: 4.5 Marvell shares for each Lumentum share, a premium of 23.9 per cent to Lumentum's close on 28 October, and an enterprise value the two companies put at $115bn. Marvell's existing owners keep about 67 per cent of the result. Lumentum's take 33.

Lumentum makes lasers. Its chips turn electrical signals into light and back again, inside the transceivers that carry data between racks of computers, and it builds the optical switches that route that light. Marvell makes the chips at either end of those links. The case for putting them together fits in one line from Matt Murphy, Marvell's chairman and chief executive: "AI infrastructure is undergoing a fundamental architectural shift toward optical connectivity." Copper wire, the release argues, has run out of physics. The two put their combined market at $550bn by 2030.

Both shares fell on the news — Marvell about 5.1 per cent on Monday morning, Lumentum 8.9. Marvell had already lost roughly 22 per cent in the two sessions before signing. That matters more than it looks. The price is a fixed number of Marvell shares, so every fall in Marvell's own stock is a fall in what Lumentum's owners actually receive.

And it is a long engagement. The companies expect to close in the first half of 2028, with an outside date that can be pushed to August that year. Marvell owes Lumentum $3.2bn if regulators block it and $5.8bn if it walks away; Lumentum owes $2.9bn if it does. Lumentum's own quarter, reported alongside, had revenue of $1.26bn, up 136 per cent, with purchase commitments above $5bn behind it.

Set the two halves of this section together. The companies that supply the AI build-out are being paid in paper, eighteen months before anything closes, while the companies that buy from them hand over real cash margin every quarter.

Doors left open

Two items
Follow-up · Binance

A tampered installer in June, a fake job offer in September

Binance published its first account of the $6.34bn theft on Tuesday, which answers the question we left open on 2 November. It turns out to be a four-month story, not a twenty-minute one.

In June an engineer on the wallet operations team installed a tampered copy of Homebrew, the tool Mac developers use to install other tools. In September a second employee was taken in by a fake recruitment process and ran a malicious coding exercise. On 20 October the attackers used that second machine, and one of Binance's internal AI tools, to reach two more engineers' computers. There they found a permission that let them deploy software containers. Nine days later they deployed one, ran a script, and emptied the hot wallets.

The detail to sit with is the internal AI tool. A company assistant that can see across internal systems is, to an intruder on one laptop, a map of everything else. In October OpenAI disclosed that an engineer had allegedly used a productivity tool to look up its own meeting rooms and executive locations before hiding a recorder behind a television. Both stories end in the same place.

On the money: Binance now says it has replaced 98 per cent of the affected assets from its own funds, and that about $2.85bn has been frozen or intercepted. Tallies of what the attackers still hold vary, and no single source stands behind any of them; the figure most often repeated is around $1.8bn. Withdrawals reopened in phases from 04:00 GMT on Tuesday across nine blockchains. A zero-knowledge proof-of-reserves snapshot covering ten tokens went out on Monday, though co-chief executive Yi He said the proof itself needs 40 to 50 hours of computing after the snapshot is taken. Richard Teng, her co-chief executive, put it plainly: "There's still work to do."

An independent cybersecurity review has been commissioned and has not reported. Bitcoin rose 3.5 per cent on Wednesday, to about $80,500.

Sources: The Block on the incident report · proof of reserves · running totals · OpenAI recorder case, 15 October

Security · 3 November

WordPress leaves a door open while it updates itself

America's cyber agency added a WordPress flaw to its list of vulnerabilities being exploited right now, and gave federal agencies until today to fix it.

The flaw is a timing problem in the way WordPress upgrades itself. During a full update the software unpacks the new version into a folder beneath wp-content/upgrade. On affected servers that folder can be reached from the web while the update is still running, and it briefly holds a working copy of the WordPress installer. An attacker who arrives inside that window can point the staged installer at a database of their own, which is a route to running code on the server. No password, no click from anyone. It scores 8.1 out of 10 for severity and is catalogued as CVE-2026-119977.

Versions 4.7 through 7.1.3 are affected. Version 7.1.4, released on 28 October, closes it. Umut Bayram is credited with the report.

The uncomfortable part is that automatic updates are what open the window. Wordfence, which sells a WordPress firewall, says its first blocked attempt arrived nine minutes after it shipped the rule, and counted more than 18,500 requests aimed at staged installer paths in 21 hours. Other tallies differ — 12,632 blocked attacks in a day in one account, 60,471 attempts in 24 hours in another — and every one of them counts attempts, not successes. Nobody has published a number for sites actually taken over.

If you run one: open the dashboard and check that the update finished, and until it has, block public access to that upgrade folder.
