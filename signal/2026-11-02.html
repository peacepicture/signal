Six point three billion dollars left Binance on Thursday afternoon. The exchange locked the door four hours later.

What saved a third of the money was not the blockchain. It was two private companies switching off dollars they had issued. That was the week's clearest lesson in who actually holds the keys, and it was not the only one.

Traders spent the same four days marking down the people building artificial intelligence. Intel had its worst day since 1974. Reddit lost 29 per cent. Amazon's accounts showed it has spent $42bn more cash than it took in over the past year. And while private money flinched, public money stepped forward. Canada bought equity in its largest AI company. Britain's sovereign fund is reported to be buying into a British chip designer. South Korea asked parliament for 21.3 trillion won. Three governments, four days, one bet.

Since last time

The European Central Bank held its deposit rate at 2.5 per cent on Thursday, and Christine Lagarde resigned the same day. The Bank of Japan held at about 1.25 per cent on Friday, 8–1, with Hajime Takata voting for 1.5. Mistral published the Large 4 weights two days before its own deadline. Amazon reported. All of it is below.

Out the door

Four items
Crypto · 29 October

The biggest theft on record took twenty minutes

The money left Binance's hot wallets from 20:05 GMT on Thursday, across ten blockchains, in about twenty minutes. Roughly $3.8bn of it was ether. Another $1.5bn was bitcoin — 18,270 coins. One single transfer carried more than $2.5bn.

TRM Labs, which traces stolen crypto for a living, attributes the theft with high confidence to TraderTraitor, a North Korean unit within the Lazarus Group. The FBI named that same group for the $1.5bn Bybit theft last year. This one is four times larger. Elliptic's chief scientist called it almost certainly the largest known theft of any kind.

The method was new. On Ethereum the attacker used EIP-7702, a feature that lets a wallet temporarily delegate control to a contract, to turn one of Binance's own wallets into a sweeper that emptied itself. Fifty signed authorisations had been prepared in advance; one of them matched the wallet's transaction count at the moment it fired. On the other nine chains the funds simply left by ordinary signed transfer, which suggests the exchange's signing machinery itself was compromised. Binance first pointed at an engineer's infected laptop. It has published no root cause.

What happened next matters more. At 21:01 GMT Tether began freezing the wallets holding its own stablecoin, and blocked Binance's hot wallet an hour later. Circle followed three minutes after that. With Ripple, Arbitrum's security council and World Liberty Financial, about $2.7bn was frozen inside a few hours — $1.77bn of Tether's dollars, $825m of Circle's. Binance did not revoke the attacker's permissions on its own wallet until 00:10 GMT, more than four hours after the first transfer.

A third of the largest theft in history was recovered because two companies in Switzerland and Boston can switch their own money off.

Binance says it has set aside $6bn and will absorb the loss rather than pass it to customers. It had replenished 90 per cent by Saturday, restarted withdrawals on Sunday and promised proof of reserves today. Bitcoin fell about 5 per cent, below $80,000, and Coinbase and Robinhood shares fell with it. The attacker still holds roughly $2.4bn, including more than 420,000 ether, and has been selling Tether to market makers at a discount of more than 10 per cent.

Sources: TRM Labs · timeline and totals · CNN · NBC News

Semiconductors · 30 October

Intel's worst day in fifty-two years

Intel shares fell about 29 per cent on Friday. The company has not had a day like it since 1974. The quarter it had just reported was good: revenue of $16.5bn, non-GAAP earnings of 52 cents a share, and $7.3bn from the data-centre and AI division — its fastest growth in fifteen years, carrying $3bn of operating profit.

The damage was in the forecast. Intel guided fourth-quarter revenue to $15–16bn, earnings to 25 cents and gross margin to 38 per cent. Two things dragged it there. Sales of chips for personal computers will fall more than 20 per cent in three months, because memory prices have made a cheap laptop almost impossible to build. And in early October an explosion at Fab 28 in Kiryat Gat, Israel, shut the plant for about two weeks.

David Zinsner, Intel's finance chief, told analysts the accident will cost roughly $300m of fourth-quarter sales and about two points of margin, with a similar hit in the first quarter. Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon said the margin guide would have been 40 per cent without it. Zinsner did not argue. Zinsner also said demand for data-centre chips "continues to meaningfully exceed available supply", and expects that to hold through 2027.

Two workers died in the explosion. A third was injured. Neither the results release nor the call mentioned them.

The selling spread outward: AMD fell 12 per cent, Nvidia 5, and the VanEck semiconductor fund nearly 8. Nearly 70 per cent of American shares fell on the day and the Vix rose more than 32 per cent intraday, the highest since June.

Sources: TheStreet market report · Calcalist on Kiryat Gat · Intel guidance detail

Platforms · 29 October

Reddit's visitors stopped arriving

Reddit's revenue grew 53 per cent last quarter, to $895m. Its shares fell 29.2 per cent on Friday, to $91.73. The problem was the people.

American daily visitors came to 50.7 million, down 5 per cent in three months and 2 per cent on the year — the first annual fall since the company floated in 2024. Through October the site lost more than a million Google referrals a week, on average. Steve Huffman, Reddit's chief executive, named Google's AI Mode — the version of search that answers the question instead of listing the links — as his leading hypothesis, while conceding Reddit cannot see inside Google's rankings to prove it.

Reddit's answer is to stop being a public website. It will test a mostly logged-in experience in selected markets outside the United States this quarter. More than 80 per cent of its revenue already comes from logged-in and app users, and a login wall also makes the site harder to scrape for training data.

The warning in the guidance is the part to keep. If referrals keep falling, Reddit says its 130 million daily visitors worldwide could drop by a low double-digit percentage within three months. Reddit's posts helped teach the machine that now answers instead of pointing.

Sources: Q3 figures and guidance · Huffman on AI Mode · Quartz

Follow-up · Hyperscaler cash

Amazon spent $42bn more cash than it took in

Amazon reported on Thursday, and it answers the question we left open on 29 October: Alphabet is not alone. Over the twelve months to 30 September, Amazon's operations produced $168.3bn of cash. Its buildings and chips consumed more than that. Free cash flow was minus $42.4bn. Alphabet had reported minus $9.1bn the day before.

The quarter itself was strong. Sales of $201.9bn, up 12 per cent. Amazon Web Services at $46bn, up 39 per cent, now running at $184bn a year. Operating profit of $28.1bn. Earnings of $1.89 a share looked like a decline only because last year's quarter contained $9.5bn of paper gains on the Anthropic stake.

The figure to hold is the capital spending. Amazon put $175.7bn into property and equipment in nine months and still guides to about $220bn of cash capital spending for the year, unchanged. Guidance for the fourth quarter is $240–247bn of sales.

Investors treated the whole thing as a bill rather than an investment. Over the week Alphabet fell 12.6 per cent and SpaceX 8.7. Amazon's own shares slid on the Friday that followed.

Sources: Amazon Q3 release · CNBC

On the share register

Three items
Canada · 29 October

Ottawa bought a piece of its biggest AI company

Canada is now a shareholder in Cohere. On Thursday, opening the Canada–EU summit in Montreal, Mark Carney said the government will put up to C$1.6bn into the Toronto company's new funding round through the Canada Development Investment Corporation. Not a grant. Not a loan. Equity.

The round is about C$7bn and values Cohere at close to C$36bn, making it the most valuable private technology company in the country. Germany's Schwarz Group, the owner of Lidl, is leading it. Nvidia and Bell Canada are in as well.

The conditions are the interesting part. Cohere must keep its headquarters and its incorporation in Canada, create a thousand well-paid jobs, spend at least C$1.7bn on Canadian computing by 2030, and report its progress to Ottawa. Carney framed it as control rather than return: "Canadians decide how it works and who it answers to." Aidan Gomez, Cohere's chief executive, said the company would always be based in Canada. Ottawa had already pledged up to C$240m to a Cohere data-centre project in 2024.

Britain appears to be doing a quieter version of the same thing. The Telegraph reported on Sunday that the UK Sovereign AI Fund is putting hundreds of millions of pounds into Fractile, a London company designing chips that run trained AI models rather than train them. The accounts conflict on size — the Telegraph says about $700m, earlier reports said $600m — but both put the valuation at $6.5bn, and both say Anthropic has agreed to buy around $250m of Fractile's chips. Nothing has been announced. Treat it as reported, not done.

Sources: Carney announcement · Bloomberg · Telegraph report on Fractile · earlier Fractile figure

South Korea · 2 November

Seoul will tax the chip boom to pay for the next one

President Lee Jae Myung asked the National Assembly this morning for a 2027 budget of 820.9 trillion won, 12.8 per cent larger than this year's. Of that, 21.3 trillion won — about $16bn — goes to artificial intelligence and three projects the government calls megaprojects: semiconductors, physical AI, and AI data centres. The AI line is close to double this year's.

Where the money comes from is the striking part. Lee is setting up a 162 trillion won fund, around $120bn, financed out of the windfall tax revenue the semiconductor boom is throwing off. He described it as "a mechanism to keep public finances stable as tax revenue fluctuates more widely". Another 13.8 trillion won goes to ten strategic technologies meant to come after chips.

Korea is doing something few governments can: taxing one cycle to buy a position in the next. It is also betting that the cycle lasts. The opposition People Power Party, which protested in the Assembly lobby, called the budget "a declaration of war on the people" and pointed at the national debt. The legal deadline for passage is 2 December.

Sources: Korea Times · CNBC

Indo-Pacific · 31 October

The Quad went looking for chokepoints

The foreign ministers of America, India, Japan and Australia met in Atlanta on Saturday, alongside the G20, and pledged more than $85m to widen access to what they call trusted AI across the Indo-Pacific. The money is small. The second announcement is not.

The four said they will start identifying chokepoints in semiconductor and AI hardware supply chains "before they cause disruptions". No lead agency was named, no method, no date. But an allied effort to work out which single factory or chemical the whole industry rests on is a different sort of project from a grant programme — and a tacit admission that nobody currently knows.

The ministers also recorded "grave concerns" about economic coercion, arbitrary export restrictions and non-market overcapacity, and named no country at all. Washington added $10m to recover critical minerals from electronic waste in India and south-east Asia. The Quad's export credit agencies meet in Tokyo on 18 November.

Source: Quad joint press release, reported

The order book

Three items
South Korea · 1 November

Korea sold $115bn of things in a single month

South Korea's October exports came to $115.3bn, up 93.7 per cent on a year earlier. Semiconductors alone were $56.4bn, up 259 per cent — roughly half of everything the country sold abroad.

The figures are not adjusted for working days, but no calendar explains a near-doubling. A year ago the same month was worth about $59bn. Imports rose 36 per cent to $72.8bn, leaving a monthly trade surplus of $42.5bn. Exports to China rose 134 per cent, to America 186, to south-east Asia 128. In ten months Korea has sold more than $900bn abroad for the first time, and the trade ministry thinks a trillion is reachable as early as this month.

Put plainly: about half of Korea's export earnings now depend on memory chips going into other people's data centres. That is a national economy pointed at one customer base. Kim Jung-kwan, the industry minister, was careful anyway — "The global trade environment remains uncertain" — and named protectionism, supply-chain shifts and the Middle East.

Sources: Korea Times / Yonhap · Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources

China · 31 October

China's factories went back below fifty

China's official manufacturing index fell to 49.3 in October, from 50.1 in September. Fifty is the line between factories reporting more business than last month and fewer, so China's are reporting fewer. Economists polled beforehand had expected 50.

The non-manufacturing index, which covers services and construction, also slipped below the line, to 49.7. That is the more unusual of the two readings; services rarely contract in China. The National Bureau of Statistics published both on Saturday and directed attention to the sub-indices that are still expanding, which it groups as new momentum industries.

Hold the two Asian numbers side by side. Korea is selling memory into other countries' data centres faster than it has ever sold anything. China, which makes most of everything else, is shrinking. The build-out is not lifting a region. It is lifting the handful of firms that make the parts.

Sources: NBS October PMI · Global Times · consensus

Euro area · 29–30 October

France has not grown all year, and Lagarde is leaving

The French economy shrank 0.1 per cent in the third quarter, Insee said on Friday. It was flat in the second and shrank 0.2 in the first. France has not grown at all in 2026. The euro area as a whole managed 0.4 per cent in the quarter, and the EU 0.5.

Households kept spending, up 0.4 per cent. Factories did not. Transport equipment output fell 3.5 per cent, aerospace 4.5, agri-food 1.5. French inflation reached 3.4 per cent in October, the highest since January 2024. Roland Lescure, the economy minister, said the country is "at a standstill" and will "hit the wall" without a 2027 budget — which the government has no majority to pass, leaving it the choice of forcing it through under article 49.3 or by ordinance, a thing no French government has done with a budget since 1958.

The borrowing cost says the same. France pays about 4.9 per cent to borrow for ten years, roughly 1.55 points more than Germany. That is down from the 1.75-point peak of 22 October and still double the average of the past year.

Into that, on Thursday, Christine Lagarde dropped her resignation. She leaves the European Central Bank on 7 February, nine months early, having first held the deposit rate at 2.5 per cent with inflation still rising on energy. Her stated reason was unusually plain. "We have an obligation of neutrality," she said, and "I don't think that I can be comfortable in that neutrality." France elects a president in the spring.

Klaas Knot and Pablo Hernández de Cos are the names being mentioned. Isabel Schnabel leaves the board in January for the IMF; Philip Lane's term as chief economist ends in May. Three of the bank's most senior people will be new within a year — the year France decides. Markets barely moved on the news, which is either reassuring or a failure of imagination.

Sources: Insee via Euronews · Eurostat flash estimate · Irish Times on Lagarde · Reuters

Weights and limits

Three items
Follow-up · Mistral Large 4

A trillion-parameter model, free to download

Mistral published the weights for Large 4 on Thursday, two days before the 31 October deadline it had set itself. We flagged the promise on 29 October; the files are now on Hugging Face and the model is on Mistral's own API.

It has about a trillion parameters and handles text and images. Mistral says it matches GLM-5.3, the strongest open Chinese model, while using 47 per cent fewer tokens for the same task, and that it beats every other open model on Artificial Analysis's ranking for coding agents. The pitch is defensive security work: find and fix flaws in your own code and infrastructure, on your own machines, with nothing leaving the building. Several secondary accounts describe the licence as a modified MIT; we could not confirm what the modification is, so take that as reported.

Pierre Stock, Mistral's vice-president of science, made the case for open weights in one line — access "that can't be cut off by definition because the model is open-weighted". Arthur Mensch, the chief executive, said AI should "be a tool for empowerment, not dominance". Both are sales pitches. They are also the one pitch a European lab has that the American labs cannot copy, which is why Ottawa, London and Seoul were writing cheques the same week.

Sources: TechCrunch · licence, as reported

Benchmarks · 2 November

The best model anyone can buy scores twelve per cent

SemiAnalysis published a benchmark this morning called GDPx, and the top score is 12 per cent.

It tests models on the work that pays: eleven fields including chip design, cybersecurity, biology, finance, law, quantitative trading, hardware design, materials science and software engineering. The tasks are not prompts with the context handed over. One finance task drops the model into a deal room of roughly 2,000 files. Each model gets eight hours and runs in its own tooling. The headline number is the share of tasks passed on the first of three attempts.

Claude Opus 5.5 leads overall at 12.0 per cent, Gemini 4 Carbon at 10.9 and GPT-6 Astra at 9.6. In finance and law, under pass-or-fail grading, every model scores essentially zero — so the authors fall back to partial credit for those two. Max Kan, Andrew Megalaa and Dylan Patel write that strong models misjudge which source is authoritative and miss the second thing that follows from the first.

Twelve per cent is a score on a test built to be hard. It is also the most honest number published this week.

Set it beside the rest of the edition. Amazon will spend about $220bn this year. Half of Korea's exports go into the build-out. Canada has bought equity. None of that is wrong because a hard benchmark is hard — but anyone selling a chart of exponential capability should be asked to put this number on the same page.

Source: SemiAnalysis, GDPx

China · 1 November

Apple will ship Chinese AI on a Chinese model

Alibaba said on Sunday that its Qwen model will power Apple Intelligence in China. The agreement was struck the day before. Chinese iPhone owners will be able to reach Qwen from Siri, from the writing tools and from visual intelligence.

Developers can test it in the iOS beta from Wednesday. The public release comes with iOS 27.3 in December. Users choose whether to turn Qwen on, and Alibaba says queries and answers will not be used to train its models. Eligibility runs on where the phone was bought, or on the device location and Apple account country both being set to China.

Apple had no local AI in China at all until the Cyberspace Administration approved Apple Intelligence in July. Wang Xiaojie of Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications made the point to Xinhua: this is the first time a leading consumer hardware maker has built its Chinese AI on a Chinese large model. Wu Yongming, Alibaba's chief executive, said Qwen "will be deeply woven into a range of experiences across the Apple ecosystem". Apple itself said nothing, which tells you how carefully this was negotiated.

Sources: Seoul Economic Daily · July approval
