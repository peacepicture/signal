A barrel of oil has done what three years of central banking could not. It has turned the Federal Reserve around.

Petrol in America now costs $4.27 a gallon, a dollar more than a year ago, and the sea lane that sets that price is under attack. On Wednesday the Fed is expected to raise interest rates for the first time in this cycle. Not cut. Raise.

Money is about to get dearer, and nobody is borrowing like the people building artificial intelligence. Over the weekend three of their chief executives asked the industry to slow down. Their finance departments did not. Anthropic picked an exchange for a listing that could be the largest in history, and Oracle promised to spend $90 billion on data centres while burning cash.

The two stories are the same story. One of them is about to charge the other interest.

One

The oil is in the price now

A shipping lane in the Gulf has become the most important input in American monetary policy.

Inflation · 11 September

A dollar more for a gallon of petrol

The national average for regular petrol reached $4.27 a gallon on Thursday. A year ago it was $3.19. Most of that dollar arrived in the past five weeks.

AAA blames the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel at the mouth of the Gulf. Iranian forces have been attacking tankers there since February and the United States has been striking back. About a fifth of the world's seaborne oil normally passes through it. Crude went back above $100 a barrel for the first time since July.

Friday's inflation figures show where that lands. Consumer prices rose 0.4% in August and 3.4% over the year. Petrol alone accounted for more than a third of the monthly rise.

Strip out food and energy and inflation runs at 2.4%, close to the Fed's target. That is the reassuring number and it is beside the point. Nobody fills a tank with core CPI.

Monetary policy · Wednesday

The Fed is about to raise rates, not cut them

The Federal Reserve meets on Tuesday and Wednesday. Futures traders put the odds of an increase somewhere between 83% and 87%, depending on whose arithmetic you trust. The spread is wide because nobody has done this calculation for a while.

It would be the first rise of this cycle. The Fed has been cutting since last year and its benchmark sits at 3.50% to 3.75%. Kevin Warsh, who took the chair this year, told the Jackson Hole conference in August there was still "work to do" on inflation. Markets took him at his word and have not stopped since.

A rise would land in an odd place. Higher rates are meant to cool an economy running hot. This one is not running hot. Households are miserable and hiring is soft. The heat is coming from a shipping lane no central bank can reach.

Bonds · 10 September

The Treasury tried to calm the bond market. The bond market ignored it.

On Thursday the Treasury bought back some of its own long-dated debt, hoping to steady prices. It spent about $5.2 billion. Traders had expected more. Yields rose anyway, through a $22 billion auction of thirty-year bonds and out the other side.

By Friday's close the two-year Treasury yielded 4.63%, the ten-year 4.96% and the thirty-year 5.35%. All three sat at 52-week highs. "Everybody is selling everything," one strategist told Yahoo Finance.

That matters well beyond a trading floor. The thirty-year yield is what sets an American mortgage, now priced at 6.76%. It is also what companies pay to borrow for a decade. Oracle, whose debt has reached $125 billion, will be refinancing into exactly this.

Households · 11 September

Americans have stopped believing the forecast

The University of Michigan's consumer survey fell to 47.8 in September, down 7.5% in a month and 13% in a year. It is the second monthly drop in a row.

The index itself is abstract. What sits behind it is not. Asked what prices would do over the coming year, households said 4.6%, up from 4.0% in August. Over the long run they said 3.4%.

Central bankers read that second figure nervously. People who expect high inflation ask for bigger pay rises, and the expectation starts making itself true. Joanne Hsu, who runs the survey, said expectations for personal finances and business conditions "plunged", and pointed at fuel prices and trade tension.

Sentiment has fallen 16% since February. February is when the tankers started burning.

Equities · Week to 11 September

A good Friday at the end of a bad week

The S&P 500 rose 0.9% on Friday to 7,656.98. The Dow added 1% to 52,573.29 and the Nasdaq 1% to 26,333.04. Oil fell nearly 4% that day, and that was enough.

The week was less kind. The S&P lost 0.8%, the Dow 1.6%, and the Russell 2000 — smaller companies, more debt, less room to absorb a rate rise — dropped 2.4%. The S&P is still up 11.9% for the year.

Brent settled around $104 a barrel on Friday, after trading above $107 on Thursday. Reports of the exact settlement differ by about fifty cents depending on the contract quoted. The direction does not.

Two

Slow down, say the people going fastest

Three chief executives, one weekend, and a report showing the danger has already arrived.

Artificial intelligence · 12 September

Anthropic's chief executive asks the industry to stop running

Dario Amodei published an essay on Saturday arguing that AI laboratories should deliberately slow how quickly they make their models more capable.

His fear is specific. Within six months, he wrote, swarms of AI agents could seize control of large parts of the internet and do hundreds of billions of dollars of damage.

"We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models. Progress will still seem fast, and we must make wise use of the time we gain." Dario Amodei, 12 September

He offered something first, which is unusual. Anthropic will give outside evaluators the access of an employee: a desk, a badge, a company laptop, and the right to publish what they find. He asked rivals to match it, and called for laws requiring frontier models to be tested before release.

Two rivals answered within a day. Sam Altman of OpenAI said he agreed "that we need to pace the frontier" and promised the same evaluator access. Elon Musk of xAI was briefer: "Dario is right."

Not everyone was moved. The investor Chamath Palihapitiya said the plan would concentrate power in Anthropic's hands rather than make anyone safer.

Then on Monday OpenAI published a customer story. Perplexity now lets its newest model draft communications, change its software and watch its production systems, checking in far less often than before. "We're able to trust it with full end-to-end systems," its co-founder said. The essay was two days old.

Governance · 13—14 September

Microsoft writes rules for its own models, and the rivals discuss a shared examiner

Satya Nadella said on Sunday that Microsoft would publish a code of conduct for its seven in-house MAI models the next day and open it to public comment. Microsoft will also embed outside evaluators with employee-level access, matching Anthropic's offer.

"If the AI we build is not helping humanity and under human control, it's not worth pursuing," he wrote, adding that alignment has to be a design goal rather than a later repair.

Separately, The Information reported that Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind have been meeting since July about a shared standards body. It would write the safety tests their own models are then judged against.

The objection fits in a sentence. The companies would be paying for the examiners who mark their papers. Credit rating agencies were financed that way before 2008, and the ratings they sold were worth roughly what you would expect.

Washington · 13 September

Washington's answer is no

Donald Trump was asked about the slowdown on Sunday at his golf resort in Doonbeg, Ireland. He was not persuaded. "Whoever wins AI, wins," he said, and suggested that "a lot of negative forces" were raising the alarm. He allowed that "we can put guardrails".

Speaker Mike Johnson said much the same on CNN. "If Congress just races in and does some sort of emergency session to try to regulate AI, we will lose the race to China." He would rather the companies led on safety themselves.

So Washington's position is that the industry should police itself, and the industry's position is that somebody really ought to police the industry. No one has volunteered to be the police.

Security · 11 September

Criminals were already using the models, at scale

Anthropic published a second document on Friday, quieter than the essay that followed it. It sets out what people had actually been doing with its models between December and August.

One member of the hacking group ShinyHunters downloaded and took apart 1.8 million Android apps, then had the model comb the code for passwords and keys developers had left inside. Confirmed finds went straight to a Telegram channel, sorted into more than a hundred categories. In one thirty-four-hour stretch the group harvested over 2,100 sets of Microsoft login tokens across more than forty corporate accounts. A breached software supplier exposed the data of about 200 of its own customers. A technology provider lost a terabyte.

Russia's Midnight Blizzard used the model against more than twenty government, defence and diplomatic bodies. A Chinese-speaking group hit around fifty organisations and turned up flaws nobody knew about in security products.

Anthropic cut off the accounts, tightened its guardrails and told the victims. Read it beside Saturday's essay and the timing does something odd to the argument. The swarm Amodei expects in six months is already working, in a smaller hat.

Three

The money is not slowing

The same weekend, the same industry, an entirely different set of numbers.

Markets · 13 September

Anthropic picks an exchange for the largest listing ever attempted

Anthropic has chosen Nasdaq for its stock market debut, Bloomberg and Business Insider reported on Sunday. Reuters puts the timing at October, with a roadshow in the middle of the month at the earliest.

The figures under discussion are not ordinary. The company is said to be seeking about $100 billion at a valuation near $2 trillion. Nvidia, which sells it chips, is reported to be weighing $10 billion as an anchor investor.

Handle those numbers with tongs. Nothing is confirmed, and in May the company raised money at a valuation of $965 billion. Doubling in four months is possible in this market. It is also the kind of number that leaks before it is true.

For scale: SpaceX floated in June and raised more than $85 billion at roughly $1.75 trillion, the largest American listing on record. Anthropic would be larger.

What it would be selling is growth of a sort nobody has seen. Revenue was running at an annualised $65 billion in July, against roughly $9 billion a year earlier — and a loss reported near $42 billion for 2025, most of it computing. The company spends far more on machines than it collects from customers. Investors have not minded yet.

Note the week it chose. Its chief executive spent Saturday asking the industry to go slower, and Sunday reading about criminals using his product.

Earnings · 10 September

Oracle promised to spend $90 billion. Its suppliers' shares rose. Its own did not.

Oracle reported on Thursday evening. Revenue rose 30% to $19.3 billion. The cloud infrastructure business more than doubled, up 121% to $7.4 billion. The order book — work sold but not yet delivered — reached $664 billion, up $209 billion in a year.

Then the finance chief repeated the spending plan: $90 to $95 billion this year on data centres.

On Friday, Hewlett Packard Enterprise rose 11%, Dell 11%, HP 8% and Super Micro 7%. None of them had announced anything. They simply sell the racks, cooling and cabling Oracle is about to buy.

Oracle itself slipped. The reason sits on the cash flow statement. Free cash flow was minus $5.4 billion for the quarter, against minus $362 million a year earlier, and the company sold $20 billion of shares during it. Debt stands at $125 billion. S&P cut Oracle to BBB− in July, one notch above junk, and observed that roughly half the order book belongs to a single customer: OpenAI.

So: an enormous order book, one dominant customer, and a construction bill coming due in a market where the thirty-year yield has just hit 5.35%.

Geopolitics · 13 September

China offers the version you can download

Xi Jinping told the BRICS summit in New Delhi on Sunday that China would build an "open-source zone" for artificial intelligence across the bloc, with shared work on large language models, research centres and training programmes.

The pitch is aimed at countries that cannot buy the best American chips and would rather not rent American models. Chinese laboratories publish the weights of their models so anyone can run them. American ones mostly do not. Xi is offering the version you can keep.

He also called for a "broad, consensus-based global AI governance framework". He is expected at the White House on 24 September.

Four

Where it touches the ground

The data centre build-out, arriving in a shop.

Hardware · 12 September

Apple's folding phone tops out at $3,199, and memory is the reason

Apple set prices and dates this weekend for the iPhone Duo, the folding handset it showed on 9 September. It starts at $1,999 for 256GB of storage and reaches $3,199 for two terabytes. Pre-orders open on 16 October and it ships on 23 October.

The gap between those two prices is $1,200, and it buys memory.

Tim Cook has called the memory market a "hundred-year flood" and said he had seen nothing like it in four decades. Contract prices for ordinary DRAM rose 90 to 95% in a single quarter earlier this year. Phones did not cause that. Data centres did, buying every high-bandwidth memory chip the factories can produce and leaving consumer electronics to bid for what is left.

This is where the AI build-out reaches a shop counter. Someone queuing on 23 October will pay an extra $1,200 for storage because a server farm outbid them for it.
