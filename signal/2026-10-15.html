A Houthi missile started a fire at Saudi Aramco's head office on Tuesday night. By Wednesday morning Americans could read the result on a petrol pump.

September consumer prices in the United States rose 3.7 per cent over twelve months. Strip out food and energy and the rise was 2.6 per cent. Almost the whole acceleration came from fuel. Petrol costs a third more than a year ago. Heating oil costs nearly three quarters more. The Gulf war has stopped being a foreign story and turned into a household bill.

Markets took the point and sold. The Nasdaq fell 1.9 per cent, and traders put a Federal Reserve rate rise back in the diary for this month. Yet in the same three days the companies that make the world's chips posted the best numbers in their history. Taiwan Semiconductor turned half its sales straight into profit. ASML said it is sold out until 2028. Japan agreed to spend $21 billion building American factories. One part of the economy is paying more for everything; another is being handed money faster than it can spend it. This edition is about where those two meet.

The fire reaches the pump

Follow-up · Energy

Aramco's head office caught fire, and oil went to $106

A Houthi ballistic missile struck an administrative building at Saudi Aramco's headquarters in Dhahran on Tuesday night. Saudi Arabia's energy ministry described the fire as limited. No casualties were reported. Riyadh's international airport has now been hit four times; a strike on Saturday killed thirteen people.

Brent crude rose 87 cents to $106.56 a barrel on Wednesday afternoon. American crude touched a three-week high. Iranian officials told Reuters they are planning new tactics against tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, and would not say what they are. A container ship sank south of the strait after hitting a naval mine and catching fire.

On 28 September we reported that Iran had offered to reopen the strait within seven days and that Washington had rejected the terms. Since then the fighting has moved from the shipping lane onto the Saudi mainland. Gulf exports excluding Iran have crept back to just over 81 per cent of their pre-war level, almost entirely because Saudi Arabia is pumping harder. Iran now ships nothing at all, because an American blockade stops it.

"We're seeing a lot of fear trade today," said Phil Flynn of Price Futures Group. Ole Hansen of Saxo Bank made the sharper point: the recovery in flows is "a redistribution of available barrels". More oil is reaching buyers. Nobody is producing more of it.

Sources: Reuters via Business Recorder, OilPrice · earlier: Signal, 28 September

United States · Prices

American prices rose 3.7 per cent, and the pump did nearly all of it

Consumer prices rose 0.6 per cent in September, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Wednesday. Over twelve months they rose 3.7 per cent.

Take fuel and food out and the picture is almost calm. Core prices rose 2.6 per cent over the year, and 0.3 per cent in the month. So the jump was energy, and energy alone. Energy prices rose 5.2 per cent in September and 20.4 per cent over the year. Petrol is up 34.2 per cent. Heating oil is up 74.2 per cent. Airline fares, which are mostly jet fuel and staff, are up 22.4 per cent.

This is the Dhahran fire arriving in a Cincinnati household budget, with a lag of a few weeks. A family that drives to work and heats with oil is paying several hundred dollars a year more because of a war it has no part in.

Investors read the number as a rate rise. The S&P 500 fell 1.1 per cent, the Nasdaq 1.9 and the Russell 2000 1.9. Technology shares lost 3 per cent while health care gained 2.4, which is what a market does when it stops paying for growth. Bitcoin dropped below $82,000. "A hotter core at 0.3 per cent puts an October hike back on the table after a soft jobs report," said Thomas Wu of the lender Ledn. The Federal Reserve meets on 27 and 28 October.

Sources: BLS, TheStreet, The Block

Global · Forecasts

The IMF nudged its forecast up, then said who it is for

The International Monetary Fund now expects the world economy to grow 3.1 per cent this year, a shade above the 3.0 per cent it forecast in July. Next year it expects 3.4 per cent. America grows 2.3 per cent, China 4.5, India 7, the euro area 1.1.

The number worth stopping on is further down the page. Thirty-two countries will produce 95 per cent of the world's growth this year. In April it took fifty-four countries to make the same share. Growth is pooling into fewer places, and most of the pool is two of them: America and China together account for more than three quarters of the world's net demand for AI chips and equipment.

The forecast assumes oil averages $96 a barrel this year, and that tankers start moving through the Strait of Hormuz again by March. Brent closed Wednesday at $106.56.

Silvana Tenreyro, the Fund's research director, put it plainly: "The outlook is highly uneven." Kristalina Georgieva, its managing director, said artificial intelligence could touch about 40 per cent of jobs, augmenting, downgrading or removing them — "a slow moving tsunami, but a tsunami nonetheless". Among the Fund's listed risks was a correction in AI shares. Asia provided one on the day of publication, which is the next item but one.

Sources: IMF World Economic Outlook, October 2026, AP via Fortune

The machines that make the machines

Taiwan · Earnings

Half of what TSMC charges is profit

Taiwan Semiconductor reported a net profit of NT$763.1 billion for July to September, about $23.6 billion. That is 68.7 per cent more than a year earlier, and a record for the sixth quarter running. Revenue was NT$1.49 trillion, up 50.9 per cent. Analysts had expected less on both counts.

The margins are the real news. Of every dollar the company charged, 66.4 cents was left after paying to make the chip, 58.7 cents after paying to run the company, and 51.1 cents after tax. A foundry is supposed to be a capital-eating commodity business. This one earns like a toll bridge, because for the most advanced chips there is no second bridge.

The company has raised this year's capital spending to between $60 billion and $64 billion, and has committed about $265 billion to its fabs in Arizona. Its customers include Nvidia and Apple, which is another way of saying that most of what the technology industry sold this year was made in one company's cleanrooms.

Netherlands · Earnings

ASML has sold 2028, and buyers are asking about 2030

ASML of Veldhoven makes the lithography machines that print the patterns on a chip. For the most advanced chips, nobody else makes them at all. On Wednesday it reported third-quarter sales of €11.7 billion and net income of €5.0 billion, with a gross margin of 57.5 per cent. It shipped 113 new machines, against 86 the quarter before, and raised its full-year forecast to between €44 billion and €45 billion.

Then the chief executive said the sentence that matters. "We now expect that our supply as currently planned for both 2027 and 2028 will not fully meet the demand of our customers," Christophe Fouquet said. The company is studying whether it can lift 2028 output beyond the 30 per cent increase it had already promised. Some customers want to place orders for 2029 and 2030.

Read that as a schedule, not a boast. Every data centre announced this year, every sovereign AI programme, every chip plant breaking ground in Arizona or Malta or Kumamoto, is standing in the same queue at the same Dutch factory. China, which was about 30 per cent of ASML's business last year, will be about 20 per cent this year, as export controls bite.

Sources: ASML, earnings call coverage

Japan · United States · Industrial policy

Japan will pay $21 billion for two chip factories in New York

Japan's trade minister, Ryosei Akazawa, told reporters in Tokyo on Thursday that Japan and the United States had agreed their first semiconductor project together. Japan will put up to $21 billion into two factories in Malta, New York, which GlobalFoundries will build and run. Roughly $10 billion speeds up a plant due to open in 2029; the other $10 billion pays for a second one.

This is the opening item on a much longer bill. Under last year's trade agreement, Japan committed to a $550 billion investment programme in the United States. Akazawa said chip supply co-operation was essential to economic security in the age of artificial intelligence. GlobalFoundries said the deal was "made possible by the Trump administration's trade and investment agreement with the Japanese government". President Trump posted that Washington will take shares in GlobalFoundries, as it did with Intel; no terms were given.

Strip away the language and the structure is unusual. One government is paying to build factories in another country, the host government takes equity for free, and the company gets a plant it was not otherwise going to afford. Japan's earlier federal money under the Biden-era chip programme now goes elsewhere, a GlobalFoundries spokesman said. Nobody has published the terms on which Japan gets its money back.

Sources: Reuters via Investing.com, AP, Nikkei Asia

What China sold, and what it threatened

China · Trade

Chinese exports grew 36 per cent in a month, and chips led

China's customs administration reported on Wednesday that exports in September were 36.3 per cent higher than a year earlier. In August the figure was 25 per cent. Imports rose 31 per cent. The month's trade surplus was $133.5 billion, and the surplus for the first nine months of the year $938 billion. Last year's full-year record was $1.2 trillion. China will pass it.

The composition matters more than the total. Over the first nine months, the value of semiconductor exports rose 108.1 per cent, data-processing equipment 54.6 per cent and cars 54.3 per cent. A weaker yuan helped; Beijing says it has "no need or intent" to seek advantage by devaluing.

Trading partners are running out of patience. The European Union's deficit with China now runs at more than $1.1 billion a day, and the EU trade chief, Maroš Šefčovič, says a deal with Beijing will halve Chinese hybrid car imports into Europe over the next few years. Washington is investigating excess industrial capacity even while extending a tariff truce on $60 billion of goods.

The most interesting warning came from Max Zenglein of The Conference Board, who argued that the biggest threat to Chinese exports is not tariffs at all. It is a slowdown in the world's AI spending. China's export boom and America's data-centre boom turn out to be the same boom, seen from either end.

Source: AP

South Korea · Semiconductors

A speech in China knocked five per cent off the Korean market

On Saturday the president of ChangXin Memory Technologies, Cao Kanyu, told a chip conference that his company's first 4F² memory would be a server module, out before the end of the year.

A short translation. Memory cells are measured in multiples of F², where F is the smallest feature a factory can print. Everyone ships 6F² cells today. A 4F² cell is about a third smaller, so the same silicon wafer yields a third more memory, which means cheaper memory. Samsung and SK hynix have shown 4F² designs in research papers. Neither sells one.

By Tuesday the Kospi had closed at 6,035.35, down 5.46 per cent, after briefly falling through 6,000 and triggering a sell-side circuit breaker for the first time in two months. Samsung Electronics lost 7.39 per cent and SK hynix 10.09 per cent. Foreign investors sold 2.2 trillion won of Korean shares in the day and nearly 8 trillion in the month.

An unverified claim at a Chinese conference moved more money in Seoul than most central bank decisions do.

The caveats are substantial. CXMT has published no speeds, no capacities, no customers and no press release. Nobody outside the company has tested anything. Korean reports suggest the first products may use the new cell design only in part. CXMT had 9.5 per cent of global memory revenue in the second quarter, fourth place, up from 7.6 per cent in the first.

Fund managers are not sure the claim is why they sold. "The outflows are too large to be attributed solely to portfolio rebalancing after the rally," said Heo Jae-hwan of Eugene Investment & Securities, who noted that selling pressure in Korea was more than three times as heavy as in Taiwan relative to market size. Lee Jae-won of Yuanta Securities said flows worry him more than chip earnings do.

Sources: The Korea Herald, Tom's Hardware

Found out

United States · Enforcement

Bank of America will pay $1.2 billion for not asking questions

The Treasury fined Bank of America $1.2 billion on Tuesday. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network assessed the penalty; the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency takes $750 million of it, the largest fine that regulator has ever imposed. A separate settlement with the sanctions office added $11.6 million.

The bank admitted that it willfully broke the Bank Secrecy Act of 1970 — the law that requires a bank to notice suspicious money and tell the government about it. Treasury said the failures let Chinese money-laundering networks and illicit finance along the south-west border move through the second-largest bank in America, and touched sanctions on Iran, Russia, narcotics and terrorism.

"Today's actions send a clear message that even the biggest financial institutions are not above the law," said the Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent. The bank said: "Financial crime compliance is serious, and we are responsible for getting it right."

It is the third-largest penalty in Treasury history, after roughly $4 billion from Binance in 2023 and about $3 billion from TD Bank in 2024. Two of those three are banks that were told to fix this and did not.

Sources: CNBC, AP

United States · Security

Someone hid a recorder behind the television in OpenAI's boardroom

Federal prosecutors have charged an OpenAI engineer with bugging his employer's executive conference room in San Francisco. He appeared in court on Tuesday.

The complaint reads like a short thriller. On 28 September, prosecutors say, Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, plugged a recording device into a USB port on the back of a television in a room he had no right to enter. The device was wrapped in toilet paper. Its contents were encrypted. Staff found it two days later.

On 5 October, they say, he came back and looked behind the television. The same day he left a bag in another room. Security opened it and found a second recorder, along with his wallet and his identity card. He was arrested on 9 October.

Prosecutors also say a chatbot account sharing identifiers with his own asked questions about the device on 27 and 28 September, and that he used a workplace tool to look up office addresses, meeting rooms, their capacities, where executives sat and which partners were visiting. The room is where OpenAI discusses its technical and commercial plans.

"For us it is a matter of national security," said the US attorney, Craig Missakian. The charge carries up to five years. Alfozan has not entered a plea, and nothing in the complaint has been tested in court.

Sources: US Department of Justice, The San Francisco Standard

Security · Software

Microsoft closed 1,413 holes in a single day

Microsoft's monthly patch on Tuesday fixed 1,413 flaws across 176 updates. That is the largest single release the company has ever shipped, and it takes this year's total past 4,000. About seventy were rated critical.

One was already being used. CVE-2026-96951 is a flaw in Windows Shell that strips away the warning Windows shows on a file downloaded from the internet. Send someone a crafted file by email or chat; if they open it, the usual caution never appears. America's cyber agency gave federal departments until Friday to fix it.

Microsoft credited researchers at Fujitsu and at Anthropic among those who found this month's bugs. Analysts attribute the record count partly to machines: software that reads code is finding flaws faster than people ever did. No one has said what share of the 1,413 came that way. Earlier this month Google shut part of its bug-reporting programme for the opposite reason — too many machine-written reports to read.

Sources: Threat Landscape, HackRead

United Kingdom · Policy

London asked four AI labs to accept binding rules and got two answers

The Commons business, innovation, science and trade committee questioned Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta and OpenAI on Tuesday. The committee wanted to know whether Britain's voluntary approach to AI safety is still adequate.

OpenAI and Anthropic said they would accept legally binding safety rules. Google DeepMind and Meta preferred the frameworks that already exist. The specialist outlet MLex, which covered the session, put it more carefully: all four agreed on the need for outside scrutiny and testing, and differed on how binding any rules should be.

All four also said they would give Britain's AI Security Institute access to models before release, "provided they are legally allowed to". That clause is doing a great deal of work, and nobody at the table was asked to define it.

It is a familiar shape. Last week in New York, a council committee asked the same companies for a probability of catastrophic harm and did not get one. Labs asking to be regulated is now common; labs agreeing on what the regulation should say is not.

Sources: MLex, City AM
