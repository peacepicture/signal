A private key is meant to be a number nobody can guess. One of eMudhra's was four.

Things built in a hurry came due this week. An Indian company that vouches for the identity of websites had been doing it with keys a laptop can work out in under a second. Two audits had passed them. A power fault in one London data centre took Lloyds, HSBC and Royal Mail offline for eight hours. Four men died raising the steel frame of a chip factory in Kaohsiung, and a worn bolt is the leading suspect.

Section one

One bolt, one key, one power cut

Web security · India

An Indian company guarded the web with the number four

Eight of eMudhra's intermediate certificates had private keys that anyone could work out. The private key is the secret half of the pair that proves a website is who it claims to be. A proper one is a random 384-bit number, which is to say one of roughly 10115 possibilities. One of eMudhra's was 4.

The researcher Scott Helme pulled it off the public certificate on his laptop in under a second. “Not a number that starts with 4, or the number 4 followed by a few hundred random digits,” he wrote. “Just, 4.” He also found 56 and 115. Other researchers reported 66, 155, 156, 230 and 237.

Mozilla's Dennis Jackson published the problem on Tuesday, three days after a volunteer reported it on a Saturday evening. Firefox, Chrome and Apple's browsers have all shipped blocks, and the root certificate sitting above those eight was distrusted as a precaution. Readers who have not updated a browser this week should.

eMudhra says it takes full responsibility. It traces the fault to a key-wrapping step in a ceremony held in April 2025, which quietly produced a weak set of keys alongside the strong set. The weak ones went to the browsers. Nobody noticed for about eighteen months, through two independent audits.

The company and the researchers do not agree about how bad this is. eMudhra says seven of the eight were never used in production, the eighth issued nothing, and the exposure is confined to “one customer ecosystem in the United States.” Researchers describe a web-wide emergency. Both accounts can hold: the harm may turn out small and the near-miss enormous. eMudhra has issued more than 60 million certificates.

Sources: eMudhra statement · Mozilla, Dennis Jackson · The Register · Help Net Security

Software · Disclosure

Two holes in PHP, and a machine found one of them

PHP runs a large share of the world's websites. On Wednesday its maintainers patched two flaws that let a stranger run code on a web server without logging in. Both scored 9.2 out of 10 for severity. The worst case is a common Apache configuration, where a single unauthenticated request triggers the bug before a site's own code runs at all.

One of the two was found by a machine. OpenAI says a security agent of its own, built on its GPT-5.6 Sol model, turned it up; the security firm Wiz found both independently and reported them. OpenAI briefed the White House and America's cyber agency during the days between the fix and its release, and has put $1bn behind subsidised access to its tools for defenders.

The patched versions are 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35. There are backports for versions nobody should still be running, which is itself a statement about how this will go.

Sources: php.net release notes · OpenAI

Cloud · Britain and Ireland

One London data centre took the banks down with it

On Tuesday morning a power fault hit a single zone of Google's London region, the one it calls europe-west2-b. One zone of one region, in a system sold on the premise that losing one should not matter.

Customers lost eight hours. Lloyds Bank, HSBC, Royal Mail, Revolut, Rightmove, DPD, BT and Screwfix all went down or slowed to a crawl. Revolut's fault reports spiked between 7am and 8am; it has 3.4 million customers in Ireland alone, and told them their money and data were safe. Allied Irish Banks and Bank of Ireland, who do not sit in that zone, carried on as usual.

Google's own timeline runs like this: failures from 06:08 UTC, a tentative all-clear by 13:00, most customers back by 14:03, a “very small number” still broken at 17:37. Seven of its products were hit, among them Cloud Run, App Engine and its Kubernetes service. It has promised an analysis once the internal investigation finishes.

Every bank that went dark had moved to the cloud partly for resilience. The thing that failed was not software.

Sources: The Register · The Irish Times · ThousandEyes

Semiconductors · Taiwan

Four men died building a chip factory

On Tuesday four workers fell while hoisting steel at TSMC's Fab 22 construction site in Kaohsiung. The fourth died in hospital on Wednesday. All four worked for a subcontractor, Shang Hao Machinery.

The science park's bureau believes aged or broken bolts on a steel connection plate may have given way. That finding is preliminary. Inspectors had been to the site three times in 2025 and eighteen times this year, including one steel-structure inspection in July. Two other contractors there had already been fined a combined NT$210,000, about $6,500.

TSMC has suspended steel work at Kaohsiung and at every new fab it is building anywhere in Taiwan. It resumes only once Shang Hao's improvement plan passes review. Nobody has put a figure on the delay. Fab 22 is being built to make 2-nanometre chips, the leading edge, much of it destined for data centres. Tom's Hardware counts at least nine deaths across TSMC construction sites since 2024.

The industry measures this build-out in gigawatts and in hundreds of billions of dollars. In Kaohsiung on Tuesday the unit was a bolt.

Sources: Focus Taiwan (CNA) · Taipei Times · Tom's Hardware

Section two

Ahead of the cash

Earnings · United States

SpaceX spent nearly four dollars for every one it earned

SpaceX made its first quarterly profit as a public company. Net income was $4.4bn on revenue of $14.6bn, against a $1.1bn loss a year earlier. Revenue rose 161 per cent. Then look at the cash.

In the nine months to September the company spent $48.1bn on capital projects. Its operations generated $13.1bn. That is $3.67 going out for every dollar coming in. Of the third quarter's $19.6bn, $16.4bn went into one division: the AI business SpaceX calls Super Intelligence, which has grown from $813m of quarterly revenue a year ago to $7.56bn, and has now overtaken Starlink.

Starlink is the quieter story in the same filing. Consumer subscribers nearly doubled to 13.6 million. Average revenue per subscriber fell from $83 a month to $69. More customers, each worth less.

On the call Musk said SpaceX and Tesla have each set up committees of independent directors, at shareholders' request, to discuss merging the two. He said he cannot take part, being conflicted. “Do I think it would be a good idea? Yes,” he said, and then: “This is definitely kind of a question where I feel like I'm tap dancing through a minefield.”

Tesla reported the same day. Revenue was flat at $28.1bn, gross margin improved to 19.6 per cent, and net profit fell 43 per cent to $787m. The figure that moved the shares was smaller and pointed the other way. Tesla cut its capital spending plan for the year to about $20bn, from more than $25bn. One Musk company is spending everything it has. The other has just decided not to.

Sources: SpaceX third-quarter results · Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance · Electrek on Tesla

Capital markets · Korea

Korean investors asked for 388 times the shares, then sold

Elice, a Korean data-centre operator, priced its listing at the very top of its range. Institutions bid for 389 times the stock on offer. Retail investors bid for 408 times. More than 10 trillion won sat in deposits waiting for an allocation.

It opened on Tuesday 16 per cent below the issue price and kept falling, touching 61,000 won against the 90,500 it had been sold at. Outlets put the first day's loss anywhere between 28 and 33 per cent, depending on whether they used the intraday low or the last trade. Either way, the people who queued hardest lost the most.

This is the second data-centre float to break this month, after Firmus pulled its Australian listing on 9 October. American data-centre shares fell on the read-across, and by Thursday morning Asian chipmakers were being sold hard: SK Hynix down 4 per cent, Samsung Electronics 3.6, the Kospi more than 2.

Demand is extraordinary. Capacity is the constraint. Jensen Huang, Nvidia, in Berlin on Wednesday

The people selling the equipment see none of this doubt. Lam Research reported its first quarter above a 40 per cent operating margin, and said spending on wafer-fab equipment — about $150bn this year — will pass $200bn in 2027. Its shares fell 7 per cent anyway, on a warning that its own suppliers could delay shipments. Nvidia, meanwhile, has started paying for capacity years ahead, including $1.1bn handed to a Taiwanese chip-testing firm to build new plants in America, and a commitment to roughly $10bn across Europe.

Two markets are now telling opposite stories. The machines are sold out to 2028. The companies that buy them cannot reliably raise money for a week.

Sources: Reuters via Yahoo Finance · CNBC · Nvidia · Nvidia supply-chain release

Section three

Rulings and orders

Courts · United States

Meta's bill for one week came to thirty-five billion dollars

On Wednesday a judge in New Mexico ordered Meta to pay the state $25bn. Judge Francis Mathew found that the statute warranted more than $219bn, and reduced it.

The arithmetic is the remarkable part. A Santa Fe jury decided on 25 September that 26 of 29 statements by Meta and its leadership about data privacy were misleading. That came to more than 43 million separate violations of New Mexico law, each carrying up to $5,000. The state filed the case in 2021. It goes back to the personality quiz that harvested roughly 87 million Facebook profiles for Cambridge Analytica.

Attorney General Raúl Torrez called it a warning “to those in this space and beyond that we intend to protect our people.” Meta's chief legal officer, C.J. Mahoney, said the ruling “makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico.” Meta says it will win on appeal, and analysts expect it to keep appealing for years. No money moves soon.

The same week's results carried the second bill, and that one is already booked. Meta took a $9.89bn charge for an August settlement with 52 state attorneys general over teenagers' mental health, an agreement providing for payments of up to $18.6bn. The charge halved the operating margin, from 40 per cent to 20. Strip it out and the quarter beat expectations: revenue up 26 per cent to $64.6bn, and earnings of $7.40 a share rather than $3.83.

Meta also raised the floor of this year's capital spending, to a range of $135bn to $145bn, and spent $34.9bn in the quarter alone. Free cash flow was negative $2.1bn. Both of the week's bills are survivable. Spending more than you take in, at that scale, is the thing to watch.

Sources: Reuters via Yahoo Finance · CBS News · Meta third-quarter results

Procurement · United States and China

Washington ordered DeepSeek out, and anything built on it

The Department of Homeland Security published an order on 20 October removing DeepSeek from every federal civilian agency. Agencies may not buy its products, and must take out what they already run. Exceptions can be requested.

The reach is the new part. The order covers “third-party products and services developed using DeepSeek implementations” — not only DeepSeek's own software, but anything built on its models. A good deal of software now has an open-weight Chinese model somewhere inside it, and very few buyers could tell you which. Compliance means auditing a supply chain that nobody has mapped. The order was signed for the department by its general counsel, James H. Percival II, under procurement security powers passed in 2018.

China's foreign ministry objected. Its spokeswoman Mao Ning criticised “overstretching the concept of national security and politicizing technology and trade issues,” and said Beijing would defend its companies' interests.

DeepSeek's own answer, the same day, was to publish the inference engine that runs its production service as open source. It supports Nvidia's newest chips and Huawei's Ascend 950 alike. Washington can bar the company from government systems. It cannot easily bar the arithmetic.

Sources: Federal Register, 91 FR 66346 · TechCrunch · DeepSeek, DeepInfer

Policy · United States

Anthropic has drafted the law that could stop its own models

Anthropic is circulating a draft federal bill. It would let the government block the release of a model — but only through a judge, and only at a very high bar.

Independent evaluators would register with the Commerce Department and test frontier models. Labs could pay them, but could not make the payment depend on the result. If an evaluator finds an imminent catastrophic risk, it has 72 hours to tell Commerce, and Commerce must then petition a judge in Washington to halt the release. No federal agency could stop a model by itself.

The bar is a “foreseeable, imminent risk” of more than 50 deaths or more than $1bn in property damage, arising from a biological weapon, a cyberattack, or a model slipping its developer's controls. Those are the same two numbers as California's SB 53. A spokesperson said the bill “represents the proposals outlined in Anthropic's Advanced AI Framework.”

Fifty is an odd figure to legislate. It is also the first figure a frontier lab has been willing to write down. Asked in New York three weeks ago to put a number on catastrophic risk, none of them would.

Sources: TechCrunch · Mother Jones

Section four

Nobody is cutting

Inflation · Britain

A tank of petrol is now setting Britain's interest rate

UK inflation jumped to 3.5 per cent in September, from 3.1 in August. The Office for National Statistics published it on Wednesday. It is the highest rate in almost a year, and higher than forecast.

Transport did most of the work, and fuel did most of the transport. Petrol averaged 169.5 pence a litre, up 8.2 pence in a single month. Diesel averaged 192.5 pence, up 10.7. Over the year fuel prices rose 29.8 per cent, and transport alone added 0.94 percentage points to the headline rate. Core inflation, which strips out energy and food, rose to 2.8 per cent, and services to 3.7. Hotels and restaurants were the only real drag.

The Bank of England meets on 5 November with its rate at 3.75 per cent, and has not moved it since 2023. Danni Hewson of AJ Bell put it bluntly: “Market expectation of a November increase is now nearing 100 per cent,” with better than even odds of a second rise before the year ends.

Britain is not unusual. Reuters polled 100 economists between 16 and 20 October. Ninety-four expect the Federal Reserve to hold next week. All 100 expect a rise in December. Last month fewer than half did. The Bank of Korea held at 3 per cent on Thursday after raising twice in the summer; Hungary held at 5.5; China held for the seventeenth month. Across the rich world the question is no longer whether to tighten. It is how much more.

Follow-up · Indonesia

Jakarta defended the rupiah without touching its main rate

Five days after the worst session on the Jakarta exchange since 1998, Bank Indonesia held its policy rate at 5.75 per cent. Then it changed nearly everything else. We reported the crash on 19 October, when MSCI opened a consultation on dropping Indonesia from its emerging-market indices and the market fell 11.57 per cent in a day.

What moved: the bank raised its deposit facility rate by a quarter point to 5 per cent and left its lending rate at 6.5. That pays banks more to park rupiah overnight without making loans dearer — a currency defence, not a growth decision. It also cut the share of deposits banks must hold in reserve from 9 per cent to 8 on 1 November, and to 7 on 1 December, which frees cash to lend. And it raised the discount on hedging rupiah forwards for foreign money from 25 to 40 per cent at six months. The bank says that incentive scheme has pulled in $9.9bn since June.

Half of it worked. Shares recovered; the currency did not.

The rupiah closed at 18,022 to the dollar on Wednesday, back through 18,000. Foreign investors have pulled a record 88.19 trillion rupiah out of Indonesian shares this year, more than in 2008 and 2020 combined. The index is down about 40 per cent since January.

MSCI does not decide until June 2027. Indonesia has to hold the line for twenty months.

Sources: Bank Indonesia · Antara · Reuters via The Star

Capital markets · India

Ambani's $3.2bn was covered in under two hours

Jio Platforms opened India's largest share sale on Wednesday and had it covered before lunch. The institutional tranche filled in about ten minutes.

The terms: ₹30,213 crore, roughly $3.2bn, all of it new shares rather than existing holders cashing out. A price band of ₹1,065 to ₹1,119 values Reliance's telecoms and digital arm at up to $108bn, against Bharti Airtel's $123bn, on 33 times earnings. Abu Dhabi's sovereign fund and BlackRock were among anchor investors who took $853m; anchor bids came to more than $25bn. By Thursday the book was 3.47 times covered overall, and institutions 9.42 times.

Most of the proceeds repay borrowings at the operating subsidiary. The book closes on Friday and the shares are expected to list on 28 October. It is the first flotation by a Reliance company since 2006.

Set it against the week's other listing and the lesson is narrow. Elice was 388 times oversubscribed and lost a third of its value on day one. How many people want the shares tells you nothing about what happens after they get them.

Sources: Reuters via Investing.com · Issue terms · Onmanorama
