A forestry regulator in northern Finland stopped Google this week. An industry that can borrow forty billion dollars in an afternoon still cannot buy a permit.

Money is no longer the binding constraint. SpaceX is arranging $40bn of debt to buy Nvidia chips. Emera is paying $14.3bn for a Canadian utility partly to feed the grid those chips will drain. What the industry cannot arrange as easily is consent. Finland has ordered work halted on two Google sites. Eighty-four per cent of American voters call artificial intelligence a threat to workers, a higher share than blame foreign competition. Four laboratories sat under oath in New York and would not say how likely a catastrophe is. Permission is becoming the scarce input.

Section one

Who gets to say no

Finland

Finland told Google to stop clearing forest

Finland's supervisory agency has ordered work halted at two Google data-centre sites. Tuike Finland, the company acting for Google, logged forest at Muhos and Kajaani before finishing a mandatory environmental assessment. A conservation group counted more than 300 hectares cut — about 420 football pitches. Tuike must explain how it will proceed by 14 October and suspend the groundwork by 23 October.

Google announced the Finnish investment only last month: €13bn over two years, its largest single commitment in Europe. A spokesman acknowledged "shortcomings" and said the company would follow the agency's guidance. Google also says it will plant trees across 130 hectares at Muhos. That is less than half of what came down.

Sources: Al Jazeera · Tom's Hardware

United States · polling

Voters fear the machines more than they fear foreign competition

Eighty-four per cent of American voters see artificial intelligence as a threat to American workers. That comes from a Reuters/Ipsos poll of 4,506 adults, 3,526 of them registered voters, which closed on Monday and was published on Wednesday. Its margin of error is two points.

The same survey asked about the older villains. Foreign workers drew 74 per cent, large corporations 73, illegal immigration 60. Fifty-six per cent of voters now want limits on building data centres, including 69 per cent of Democrats and 43 per cent of Republicans. Eighty per cent of Democrats and 61 per cent of Republicans want stricter rules on AI. Fifty-seven per cent say the administration is not taking the risks seriously, and about a third of Republicans agree. Very little in American politics commands those margins on both sides at once.

Source: Reuters/Ipsos, via Inside AI

Follow-up · New York

The labs came to New York and could not name a number

Speaker Julie Menin asked the four frontier labs to put a figure on the chance of a catastrophe. None of them would. We flagged the hearing in our 5 October edition, when Google, Meta, Anthropic and OpenAI had just agreed to testify under oath for the first time.

The session ran to fourteen panels. Morgan Dwyer, OpenAI's head of policy development and operations, answered "I don't know", and said the exact figure does not matter because no level of catastrophic risk is acceptable. Menin, a regulatory lawyer before she was a legislator, called that "flippant at best" and said she would treat the non-answers as equivocation. Asked whether they would ship a model that failed a safety test, the companies described their internal review processes.

Jacob Coxon, who resigned from Anthropic in September, opened the day on the whistleblowers' panel. Elon Musk's SpaceXAI ignored its subpoena entirely. The council is now going to court to enforce it.

I think it is more likely than not that humanity loses control of these AIs. Jacob Coxon, formerly of Anthropic, to the council

One bill before the council would make it unlawful to deploy a model in the city without third-party validation or a human shut-off, with penalties of $25,000 for each unvalidated deployment. The validator would be liable too.

Sources: Route Fifty · Unite.AI · NYC Council

Section two

The rest of the world builds its own

France

Europe's best model is one you can download

Mistral released a preview of Large 4 on Tuesday. It carries a trillion parameters, 49 billion of them active for any given token, and it was trained from scratch on 3,800 Nvidia Grace Blackwell chips in Mistral's own European data centres. The training data spans more than 160 languages, including every official language of the European Union. The weights follow by the end of October.

On a blind human comparison run by Surge AI it scored 3.74 out of five, second among five models. Claude Opus 5 scored 4.22. So this is not the strongest model in the world. It is the strongest one a European hospital, ministry or bank can run inside its own building, on its own terms, without sending a single query to California.

Source: AI News

Taiwan

Every dollar of American AI goes through a Taiwanese assembly line

Foxconn's third-quarter revenue rose 47 per cent to $95.4bn. Analysts had expected $89.1bn. September alone brought $36.5bn, the firm's best month on record and its first above NT$1 trillion.

Foxconn builds more Nvidia servers than anyone else, and it publishes sales every month. That makes its figures the earliest honest read on whether the AI orders are real, because they land weeks before anyone's earnings call and leave no room for narrative. Full third-quarter results come on 12 November.

Sources: Reuters · DigiTimes

China · United States

Huawei and Qualcomm decided to pay each other instead

The two companies signed a multi-year patent licence in Shenzhen on Monday. Each licenses the other's portfolio across 5G, computing, AI and networking. Qualcomm is also buying certain Huawei patents held in the United States, once regulators clear it. Neither side disclosed what money changes hands.

Washington has spent six years trying to cut Huawei out of Western technology. Its engineers kept filing patents anyway, including on the polar codes inside every 5G handset. Two firms their governments treat as adversaries have now concluded that litigation costs more than a licence fee.

Sources: Huawei · Seeking Alpha

Section three

Forty billion, borrowed

United States

SpaceX wants to borrow $40bn to buy chips

SpaceX is arranging about $40bn of debt to buy Nvidia processors, the Financial Times reported on Tuesday. Roughly $10bn would be bank loans and $30bn investment-grade bonds. Apollo is expected to lead the deal and place the paper; Pimco is among the lenders in talks. It should close in 2027. Musk said last month that xAI's Colossus 2 site could more than double its Nvidia chip count by December.

Investment-grade means the safe tier — the paper pension funds and insurers buy precisely because it is meant to be dull. Morgan Stanley reckons AI infrastructure will need $1.5 trillion from outside investors by 2028. A good deal of that will sit in retirement accounts belonging to people who have never heard of Colossus 2.

Sources: Reuters, citing the FT · CNBC

Canada

A utility sells itself to meet the demand

Emera agreed on Tuesday to buy Canadian Utilities for $14.3bn in shares. The combined company is worth about $72bn and will serve six million customers through twelve regulated utilities, with Florida and Alberta the two largest markets. Emera plans $32bn of capital spending by 2030 and expects its rate base to grow 7 to 8 per cent a year.

Rate base is the pile of assets a regulator permits a utility to earn a return on. It grows when the utility builds things, and the return arrives through monthly bills. NextEra and Dominion agreed a $66.8bn merger in May on the same reasoning. The grid is consolidating because the data centres are coming, and the people who will pay for the new wire are already on the meter.

Source: BNN Bloomberg

Singapore

Dalio says the bubble is near its pricking point

Ray Dalio told the Forbes Global CEO Conference in Singapore on Wednesday that AI is a classic bubble approaching its end. "We're in the part of the cycle that is before that but approaching that," the Bridgewater founder said. "I think we're close to that."

His mechanism is specific, and it is the one this newspaper has been tracking. The building is funded by borrowing, and borrowing keeps getting dearer. He added a second trigger: "Everybody says 'I'm worth a billion dollars' but OK, try to spend that." To spend a paper fortune you have to sell it, and the selling is what breaks the price. He spoke on a day when the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq had just set records, then gave a little back.

Source: Bloomberg

Section four

Somebody else's bill

Japan

Japan's bond market is negotiating with its new prime minister

Sanae Takaichi promised a proactive fiscal policy in her speech to the autumn parliament on Monday. Japan's thirty-year bond yield had already reached an all-time high of 4.235 per cent. She told investors to rest assured. They were not reassured.

By Wednesday she was saying Japan may review its spending plans if yields move sharply. Washington has been pressing Tokyo to spend less, because a weak yen is partly Tokyo's own making. The thirty-year eased to 4.18 per cent on Thursday, a fifth of a point below its peak. That sounds small. Japan owes more than twice what it produces in a year. On a debt that size, a fraction of a point is real money. It comes out of the same budget as the pensions.

Sources: Reuters · Finimize · Trading Economics

Follow-up · United States

The Fed's own minutes put the rise back on the table

Most members of the Federal Reserve's rate-setting committee expected another increase this year, according to minutes of the September meeting published on Wednesday. Two deputies had talked that rise out of the market last week, as we reported on 5 October. The written record puts it back.

The ten-year Treasury yield touched 5.36 per cent on Wednesday, its highest since April 2002, before closing at 5.28. The thirty-year reached 5.73, a level last seen in May 2002. The target range is 3.75 to 4 per cent and the effective funds rate was 3.88 on Tuesday. The committee meets again on 27 and 28 October.

Sources: Federal Reserve H.15 · Yahoo Finance · The Hill

Denmark

One company's access cost 8.8 million Danes their details

Attackers used a private Danish company's legitimate credentials to search the Central Person Register, and took the names, addresses and personal numbers of 8.8 million people. Denmark has about six million residents. The register holds roughly eleven million records, counting the dead and the departed, so the theft reached about four fifths of it. The intruders never asked for more than companies are allowed to see.

The register's administrators learned of the irregular queries on the evening of Friday 2 October, spent the weekend measuring the damage, notified the data protection agency on Sunday and told the public on Monday. Christina Egelund, the minister responsible, called it a deeply serious incident and ordered a security review.

The advice to Danes is the part that stings. Do not give passwords to a caller, even one who knows your name, address and personal number. That ten-digit number opens tax records, medical files and bank accounts. Nobody gets a new one.

Sources: Help Net Security · DataBreaches.net
