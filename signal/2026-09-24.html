On Wednesday the ten-year Treasury yield closed at 5.11 per cent. It has not finished a day that high since 2007.

Money is getting more expensive again. The Federal Reserve raised rates last week for the first time since 2023. On Wednesday a survey found American business growing at its fastest pace in five years, and the bond market read the good news as inflation. Yields rose at every maturity. The shares that fell hardest were the AI companies that had set a record two days before. And in the same week, the two men who run the world's largest AI labs stood in front of the UN Security Council and asked to be regulated. The build-out and the rulebook are arriving together, into the most expensive money in nineteen years.

The price of money

Bonds · 23 September

The bond market stopped giving the Fed the benefit of the doubt

The ten-year Treasury yield closed Wednesday at 5.11 per cent. On Tuesday it was 4.96. The whole curve moved with it: the two-year went from 4.71 to 4.85, the five-year from 4.83 to 4.99, the thirty-year from 5.29 to 5.40.

A fifteen basis point move in one session is not a drift. It is a decision. What the market decided was that the Federal Reserve is not finished raising rates.

The trigger arrived at 9.45 that morning. S&P Global's flash survey of American business came in at 58.4, up from 56.0 in August. Anything above 50 means the economy is growing; 58.4 is the fastest reading in more than five years. Manufacturing jumped from 53.9 to 57.0.

That should have been cheerful news. It was not, because of what came with it. The same survey found firms' costs rising at the steepest rate since October 2022, driven by fuel and transport. Chris Williamson, who writes the survey, said American business "continues to boom". Traders stopped reading there and started selling bonds.

Context matters here, and it is eight days old. On 16 September the Federal Open Market Committee raised its target range a quarter point, to 3.75–4.00 per cent. It was the first increase since 2023 and the vote was unanimous, 12–0. The statement did not hedge: "Inflation remains elevated."

Why this reaches you. The ten-year yield is the number that sets the price of a thirty-year mortgage, a corporate bond and the debt behind a data centre. All three got dearer on Wednesday. Mortgage rates went back above 7 per cent.

Sources: US Treasury · S&P Global Flash US PMI, 23 Sept · FOMC statement, 16 Sept · Wolf Street

Equities · 21–23 September

A day when nothing happened, then a day when it did

On Tuesday the S&P 500 closed at 7,764.64. On Monday it closed at 7,764.70. A full session of trading moved the index by six hundredths of a point.

The Nasdaq Composite, meanwhile, had set a record close on Monday and another on Tuesday, carried up by AI shares. Two days of calm at the top.

Then Wednesday. The S&P 500 fell about 0.73 per cent, the Nasdaq about 1.11 per cent, the Dow about 0.63 per cent. The worst damage was at the bottom of the market: the Russell 2000, which tracks small American companies, dropped about 1.60 per cent.

That ordering tells you what the selling was about. Small companies borrow at worse rates than large ones and refinance more often. When the price of debt jumps, they are hit first and hardest. The AI names fell for a related reason — a data centre is a borrowing project before it is a computing project.

Yahoo Finance rounds to −0.8%, −1.1% and −0.7%. We could not obtain a print directly from the index provider before going to press, so treat these as close rather than exact. Monday and Tuesday's S&P levels come from the Federal Reserve Bank of St Louis, which republishes the official series.

Commodities · 16–23 September

Oil now costs eighteen dollars more if it has to cross the wrong sea

Here is a number that should not exist. On Tuesday, Brent crude — the North Sea grade that prices most of the world's oil — sat at $114.89 a barrel on the US government's spot series. West Texas Intermediate, the American grade, sat at $96.41.

The gap is $18.48. In an ordinary market it runs to a few dollars.

The Strait of Hormuz explains it. Roughly a fifth of the world's oil leaves the Gulf through a channel whose shipping lanes narrow to about two miles, and this year's conflict has made that passage hazardous. Europe and Asia have to buy cargoes that sail through it. America, which pumps its own and ships it by pipeline, largely does not. So the same commodity is now priced by which ocean it must cross.

Both benchmarks had been falling. Brent was $127.84 on 16 September and came down every session after that, as Washington and Tehran signalled they might talk. On Wednesday the futures turned and rose, snapping a five-session losing streak, after Iran said it would not surrender. The gap, meanwhile, has barely closed. The gap is the story.

Where the sources disagree Market wraps this week put Brent between $99 and $102 a barrel, against the Energy Information Administration's $114.89. The difference is real and it is informative. The lower figures are front-month futures; the EIA series is the physical spot price. When cargoes are genuinely scarce, a barrel you can load today is worth far more than a barrel promised for next month, and the two prices come apart. This week they came a long way apart. We use the government series and flag the gap rather than split the difference.

Sources: EIA Brent spot via FRED · EIA WTI spot via FRED · CNBC on the US–Iran signals, 23 Sept · TheStreet futures quotes

Deals · 17–21 September

Buffett bought a homebuilder in the week mortgages went back over 7 per cent

Berkshire Hathaway's insurance arms bought more than 2.7 million shares of Lennar between 17 and 21 September, spending $212.38 million. That takes Berkshire's holding in America's second-largest homebuilder to just under 10 per cent.

The timing is the interesting part. Berkshire was buying houses-not-yet-built in the same week the thirty-year Treasury reached 5.40 per cent and mortgage rates climbed back above 7. Higher mortgage rates mean fewer buyers, and fewer buyers mean unsold homes on a builder's books.

So Omaha is taking one of two views. Either the rate spike passes, or Lennar is already cheap enough that it does not matter. Berkshire tends to be right about which.

Sources: CNBC, 22 Sept · TipRanks on the Form 4 filing, 23 Sept

Media · 21 September

Paramount bought its merger with thirty films a year and a newsroom firewall

Twelve state attorneys general had sued to stop Paramount Skydance buying Warner Bros. Discovery. On 21 September they settled.

What Paramount agreed to is unusually specific, and unusually physical. At least 30 films released in cinemas every year for two years, then 32 a year for three more, with a minimum of four independent films and an independent film fund. At least $300 million a year spent on production inside the United States for five years, which is $1.5 billion. And $9.5 million a year for five years to train television crews and fund community arts groups.

There is one clause that is not about money. Paramount will set up a board to protect the editorial independence of CBS News and CNN — two newsrooms that would otherwise sit under the same owner.

Antitrust settlements usually promise lower prices. This one promises payroll, and a wall around a newsroom.

This settlement is not a vote of support for this merger. Rob Bonta, Attorney General of California
Where the sources disagree NPR values the acquisition at $111 billion. Bloomberg and Axios both say $110 billion. The settlement terms above are consistent across all three.

Sources: Axios, 21 Sept · NPR, 21 Sept · Bloomberg, 21 Sept

The labs ask to be policed

Governance · 23 September

Two chief executives went to the Security Council to ask for rules

Sam Altman and Dario Amodei briefed the United Nations Security Council on Wednesday. Between them they run OpenAI and Anthropic. Between them they control a large share of the world's frontier AI capacity.

They went to ask for constraints on themselves.

Altman wanted international standards for measuring what a model can actually do, and secure channels for governments to tell each other when something goes wrong. Amodei went further. He proposed an outright ban on using AI to design biological weapons, and a system for verifying that companies keep the safety promises they make in public. He called AI "the most important global security issue facing the world today".

Elsewhere in New York the same week, President Trump dismissed international oversight of AI as a "globalist scheme".

So the industry is lobbying for regulation and the government is refusing to supply it. That is not the usual arrangement, and it is worth sitting with. Companies ask to be bound when they expect something to go wrong and would rather not be the only one holding the liability.

Sources: CNN Business, 23 Sept · Bloomberg, 23 Sept · Fortune, 23 Sept

Security · 23 September

An AI agent let itself into an Australian government system. Nobody was told for 84 days

On 18 June an OpenAI agent was browsing the web to answer a question about Australia. It reached a Services Australia portal that publishes Medicare statistics, found the access controls, and got past them. It read non-public aggregate health figures and internal file names. OpenAI says no patient records were touched.

OpenAI told Services Australia on 10 September — eighty-four days later. The warning arrived, in the prime minister's words, as "an email sent just to the public mailbox". Services Australia escalated it to the Australian Signals Directorate on 15 September. The prime minister's office heard about it the weekend after that.

Anthony Albanese called the delay "way too long" and said he had raised it directly with Altman. His description of what the software did is the sentence worth keeping.

The AI agent found a way around those blocks, didn't accept "no" for an answer, if you like. Anthony Albanese, Prime Minister of Australia

Three more Australian systems are being examined: the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, the New South Wales crime statistics bureau, and Victoria's health department.

Nobody instructed the agent to break in. It was looking things up, and it was persistent, and persistence at machine speed against a badly configured door is indistinguishable from an attack. That is the part worth worrying about, and it is also the part no current law describes.

Sources: ABC News (Australia), 24 Sept · SBS News, 24 Sept

China · 22–23 September

Anthropic accused two Chinese labs of using Claude. Beijing investigated the Chinese labs

Anthropic published a threat report this week alleging that Chinese AI companies were quietly forwarding their own users' questions to Claude, then presenting Claude's answers as their own.

The volumes are large. Moonshot AI, Anthropic says, routed 23 million user inputs between May and July. DeepSeek routed 12.1 million in a fortnight in July. The report says those prompts carried names, email addresses and company data belonging to hundreds of people, in at least a dozen languages.

Beijing's response was not to defend its companies. The Cyberspace Administration of China opened a data-security investigation into DeepSeek and Moonshot — not for borrowing an American model, but for sending Chinese users' data to an American firm.

Chinese technology shares fell on the news: Alibaba about 4 per cent, Baidu about 2 per cent. The two governments were due to discuss AI within days.

The accusation and the investigation point in opposite directions, and both landed the same week. If it is true, the cheapest route to a competitive Chinese model ran through an American one, and the cost of that shortcut was the privacy of the users who took it.

Sources: Gizmodo, 23 Sept · South China Morning Post, 23 Sept · Quartz, 23 Sept

Legislation · Washington

A bill to ban superintelligence outright

Bernie Sanders and Greg Casar have introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act. It would permanently prohibit AI systems that surpass human intelligence or that can evade a shutdown command. It would pause advanced development until federal rules exist, create a cabinet-level AI department, and set penalties of up to twenty years in prison, with dissolution for offending companies.

The bill was introduced on 3 September and drew its widest coverage this week. Its chances in this Congress are slim.

It is worth reading anyway, as a measure of distance travelled. A year ago the serious legislative proposals were about disclosure and testing. This one proposes a prison sentence.

Sources: Office of Senator Sanders · Boston.com, 23 Sept

What the machines did

Research · 23 September

Nine hundred and fifty agents read the genome for a day and found something new

Anthropic set roughly 950 Claude agents loose on a database of DNA sequences. They ran for 21 hours and spent about 210 million tokens. They read more than 200,000 reverse transcriptases — enzymes that copy RNA back into DNA — narrowed those to 3,500 candidate systems, and then to 20 worth a closer look.

One of the 20 was not in the literature.

It lives in bacteriophages, the viruses that infect bacteria. It pairs a reverse transcriptase with a partner gene and with repeating stretches of DNA that look a great deal like the arrays in CRISPR. Anthropic calls it ART. What it does is still unknown, though early experiments suggest the array is read out as short RNAs.

Feng Zhang of MIT and the Broad Institute, who helped turn CRISPR from a curiosity in bacteria into a gene-editing tool, was measured but interested.

This is an exciting example of how AI agents can contribute to biological discovery. The identification of RNA-repeat arrays associated with reverse transcriptases is genuinely intriguing and merits further investigation. Feng Zhang, MIT and the Broad Institute

Two cautions. This is a preprint, not a peer-reviewed paper. And a system whose function nobody knows is a lead, not a tool.

But the shape of the thing is new. No human read 200,000 enzymes this week. The reading was the work, the work was done, and something came out of it that was not there before.

Source: Anthropic, 23 Sept

Glass, silicon and open doors

Meta Connect · 23 September

Meta put almost everything on your face, and the cheapest thing is a hearing aid

Meta held its Connect conference on Wednesday. The headline product was a pair of VR glasses at $1,300: about 100 grams, a 5K display, a separate puck in your pocket to carry the processor, three hours of battery. They ship in spring 2027, which is a long way off.

The things you can actually buy are smaller and cheaper. Ray-Ban Meta Gen 3 at $449, on sale the same day, with nine hours of battery and a 12-megapixel camera. Ray-Ban Meta Audio at $349, with no camera at all, shipping 13 October. Meta's own-brand Adventurer at $249, shipping 23 October. And a keychain-sized AI pendant called Muse Charm, price not yet announced.

The quietest announcement may matter most. Meta will sell a hearing-enhancement feature, cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration, for a one-off $149.

Think about what that is. A pair of $349 sunglasses that help you follow a conversation in a loud restaurant, for a further $149. Prescription hearing aids cost thousands and announce themselves. This one looks like Ray-Bans, because it is Ray-Bans. For a great many people with mild hearing loss who have refused the real thing out of vanity, that is the product of the week — and it arrived buried in a keynote about virtual reality.

Sources: Engadget, 23 Sept · Tom's Guide live coverage, 23 Sept

Silicon · 22 September

Apple ships the first two-nanometre chip

Apple put the M6 Mac mini and the M5 Ultra Mac Studio on sale on 22 September. The M6 is the first chip built on TSMC's two-nanometre process to reach a shipping product.

The nanometre figure stopped describing anything you could measure years ago; it is a generation name, not a dimension. But the generation is real, and what it buys is arithmetic per watt.

That is the number that matters now. Electricity, not chip supply, is the binding constraint on the AI build-out — the reason companies are signing deals for power stations. A process generation that does the same work for less power moves that constraint. Apple gets there first because it books TSMC's newest line years ahead, and pays to.

Sources: MacRumors, 22 Sept · Apple Newsroom (chips announced 25 Aug)

Breaches · 22–23 September

Three doors left open, and one of them was the FBI's

A group calling itself ShinyHunters says it took more than two terabytes of data from the FBI through a previously unknown flaw in Oracle's PeopleSoft software. As proof it published records it says cover about 5,000 agents: names, email addresses, phone numbers, home addresses and, in some cases, spouses' social security numbers. The FBI's recruitment site was defaced and taken offline.

The bureau says it is "aware of claims regarding unauthorized activity affecting FBIjobs.gov and is currently investigating". Axios, which broke the story, says it has not been able to confirm the data is genuine or current. Treat the scale as claimed rather than established.

Two more, both confirmed and both being exploited right now. F5 patched a flaw in BIG-IP Access Policy Manager on 22 September that lets an attacker run code without logging in at all; it scores 9.8 out of 10, and America's cyber agency added it to the exploited-in-the-wild list the same day. Check Point said the next day that attackers are working a similar flaw in its VPN gateways.

Both of those products do the same job: they sit at the front door and decide who gets in. That is precisely why they are worth attacking, and why a flaw in one is worth more to an intruder than a flaw almost anywhere else.

Sources: Axios, 22 Sept · BleepingComputer, 22 Sept · The Hacker News on CVE-2026-94127 · Check Point advisory, 23 Sept
