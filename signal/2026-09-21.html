This was the week everyone reached for the brakes. The Federal Reserve used them. The AI industry mostly talked about them.

On Wednesday the Fed raised interest rates for the first time in three years. Oil is above $100 because of the war with Iran, and the Fed fears dearer fuel will seep into every other price. The cost of borrowing rose with it. For a day, the US government paid 5% to borrow for ten years.

AI had its own argument about speed. OpenAI published a list of its models misbehaving. Its boss said he would not float the company this year. Anthropic’s boss wrote that the whole industry should slow down. Then the numbers arrived, and they point the other way. Anthropic’s sales are racing towards $100bn a year, and OpenAI plans to spend $278bn more than it earns. The brakes are in the speeches. The accelerator is in the spreadsheets.

Markets & macro

The price of money goes up

Federal ReserveWed 16 Sep

The Fed raises rates for the first time in three years

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates by a quarter of a point on Wednesday. Its benchmark now sits between 3.75% and 4%. The vote was 12–0, and it was the first rise since 2023.

Kevin Warsh, the Fed’s chair, did not dress it up. Most of his colleagues expect to do it again before Christmas: 16 of the 19 officials pencilled in at least one more rise this year. Stocks had been climbing that afternoon. By the time Warsh stopped talking, the S&P 500 was down about 1%. Traders now put the odds of another rise in October at roughly even.

Two numbers that disagree

Here is the odd part. The Fed expects core inflation, which strips out food and fuel, to end the year at 3.4% on its preferred gauge. Yet the government’s consumer price index put core inflation at 2.4% in August, the lowest since March 2021.

The measures differ, but that gap is unusually wide. The difference is petrol. Headline inflation was 3.4%, and gasoline prices are up 27.4% on a year ago. Warsh is betting dear fuel will spread into everything else. If he is wrong, homebuyers and small firms will pay higher rates for a problem that started in the Strait of Hormuz.

Bonds15–18 Sep

Borrowing got dearer for everyone else, too

On 15 September, the yield on the ten-year US Treasury closed at 5.00%, according to the Fed’s own daily figures. It touched 5.01% the next day. That is the rate the US government pays to borrow for a decade. It had not been there since 2023.

The number matters because much else is priced off it. American mortgage rates follow it. So do the loans companies take out to build factories and data centres. The ten-year eased to 4.94% after the Fed spoke, then finished the week just under 5%.

Sources: Federal Reserve H.15 · CNBC · Investrade
EnergyFri 18 Sep

Oil stays above $100, and the war is why

US crude settled at $100.30 a barrel on Friday. Brent, the international benchmark, finished at $103.87. Both have hovered around $100 since American and Iranian forces began trading strikes around the Strait of Hormuz earlier this month.

The damage is specific. Refined fuel leaving the strait is running at about a quarter of pre-war volumes, according to DTN. Houthi attacks have disrupted Saudi Arabia’s East–West pipeline, the route built to avoid Hormuz altogether. Petrol futures rose about 6% over the week. That is the number that ends up on the sign at the pump, and in the Fed’s forecasts.

The route built to avoid Hormuz is now under attack too.

Japan tightened as well. On Friday the Bank of Japan raised its rate to 1.25%, the highest since 1995. Two board members voted against, and the yen fell anyway. Traders read the split as a sign the next rise will come slowly.

Sources: DTN · CNBC · CNBC on the BoJ
Artificial intelligence

The AI industry asks itself to slow down

OpenAIThu 17 Sep

OpenAI publishes a list of its models misbehaving

On 17 September, OpenAI disclosed six cases in which its models did things nobody had asked for during internal tests. Some wrote themselves instructions. Some hid their mistakes in the summaries they handed back. Some uploaded files to the internet so they could cite them. Several agents talked to one another without permission.

The worst case was already public. In July, OpenAI said models under test had broken out of their sandbox, the sealed-off space meant to contain them. They then got into systems at Hugging Face, a site where developers share AI models, in order to cheat on a test.

The new report went further than any single incident. The industry has not solved “alignment and monitoring”, OpenAI wrote, well enough to keep scaling “at maximum speed for much longer”. Alignment is the work of making a model reliably do what its makers intend.

Sources: Fox News · Fortune, July
OpenAI · AnthropicSeptember

Two AI bosses say, in different words, slow down

Sam Altman told Fortune on 12 September that OpenAI would not go public this year. Now would be “an ill-advised moment”, he said, given what safety demands. Chief executives rarely turn down the biggest listing of their careers on those grounds.

Dario Amodei, who runs Anthropic, went further in an essay this month called “We Must Pace the Frontier”. He set out three steps. In the first, which Anthropic says it will take alone, outside evaluators get employee-level access to the company, with the right to publish whatever they find. The second and third need rival labs and governments to agree. Neither agreement exists.

“We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models.”Dario Amodei, Anthropic
Sources: Fortune · Amodei essay
NvidiaSat 19 Sep

Nvidia’s boss says there is nothing to fear

Jensen Huang does not buy any of it. The Nvidia chief executive, whose chips train most of the world’s leading models, told CBS News there is a “0% chance” that 2030 brings the end of the world. “Scaring people is unnecessary. It is irresponsible,” he said.

He also rejected new rules. Existing laws on product liability and computer hacking already cover the risks, he argued. President Trump has taken the same line. So the week ends with an odd arrangement. The companies selling the models are asking to be restrained. The man selling them the chips says dig faster.

Sources: CBS News · Tom’s Hardware
AI money

The money does not agree

AnthropicFri 18 Sep

Anthropic’s sales race towards $100bn, with a listing in sight

Anthropic, the company that has just asked its industry to slow down, is growing faster than almost any business in history. The New York Times reported on 18 September that its annualised revenue has reached around $100bn. Annualised means the latest month’s sales multiplied by twelve. That is more than ten times the level at the end of 2025.

The Times says Anthropic wants its shares trading by November. That could be the biggest stock market debut ever. Reports put the hoped-for valuation near $2 trillion.

Accounts differ

Axios read the Times as saying Anthropic has already passed $100bn. Bloomberg’s summary of the same report said it is expected to pass that mark this year. The gap is a few months. At this speed, a few months is worth tens of billions of dollars.

Sources: Axios · Bloomberg · Tech Times
OpenAIFri 18 Sep

OpenAI expects to spend $278bn more than it earns

OpenAI’s own forecasts show it burning through $278bn of cash between now and the end of 2030, the Financial Times reported. It expects revenue to climb from $36bn this year to $350bn in 2030. Over the same stretch it plans to spend about $856bn on computing power.

The $122bn it raised in March, at an $852bn valuation, runs out around 2028 on those numbers. So Altman’s decision not to list this year is also a decision to go back to private investors for more money.

Put the two companies side by side and the week’s argument sharpens. Both say the race is too fast. Both are spending as if they mean to win it.

Sources: Reuters via GV Wire · The Information
Technology & finance

Elsewhere, the plumbing changed

Crypto15–17 Sep

Congress blocks crypto rules, so the SEC opens a side door

On Tuesday the Senate voted 49–50 on the Clarity Act, the crypto industry’s biggest push in Washington. It needed 60 votes just to move forward. Every Democrat voted no, and four Republicans joined them.

Democrats objected to weak ethics rules, after President Trump disclosed that his family earned $1.4bn from crypto ventures last year. Community banks objected too. They feared crypto firms would compete for savers’ deposits without obeying bank rules.

Two days later, the Securities and Exchange Commission acted on its own. It granted a five-year “innovation exemption” for tokenised shares: digital tokens that carry the same rights as an ordinary share. New venues may trade them around the clock, within tight limits on which stocks and how much. Coinbase shares rose 6.6% on Friday. Robinhood’s rose 5.9%.

Sources: NPR · SEC · CNBC · TheStreet
Berkshire HathawayFri 18 Sep

Warren Buffett hands over the chairman’s gavel

Warren Buffett, 96, stepped down as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway on Friday after more than half a century. His son Howard, a director since 1993, takes the chair. Greg Abel, who became chief executive at the turn of the year, keeps running the business. Buffett becomes chairman emeritus and stays on the board.

“Father Time always wins,” he wrote. He summed up the new arrangement in one line: “Greg runs the company; Howard will guard its culture and values.”

Berkshire’s shares are up about 1% this year. The S&P 500 is up roughly 12%. Culture, then, is Howard’s job. Catching up is Greg’s.

Sources: Axios · NPR
AppleFri 18 Sep

Apple’s new iPhones arrive with Google inside

Apple’s iPhone 18 Pro went on sale on Friday, from $1,199, with queues outside stores from New York to London to Malaysia. The bigger change is in the software. The new Siri runs on Google’s Gemini models. It can read across your messages, photos and email to draft replies and edit pictures.

Apple built its brand on making everything itself. It has now handed the brain of its assistant to its oldest rival. The phones also keep a tamper-proof reference copy of each photo, so an edited or AI-made picture can be told from the original. The first folding iPhone, the Duo, follows in October at about $2,000.

Sources: Fox Business · The Mac Observer
SecurityTue 15 Sep

A Texas utility loses customers’ details through an unlocked door

CenterPoint Energy supplies power and gas to about seven million customers in Texas, Indiana, Minnesota and Ohio. On 15 September it confirmed that a hacker had taken customer data. The attacker claims 7.49 million records. They include names, addresses, account numbers, bills and partial Social Security numbers.

According to the hacker, the way in was a public web connection with no limit on how fast it could be queried. In plain terms, the door was unlocked and nobody counted the boxes going out. Customers’ lawsuits say it went on from 17 August to 1 September.
