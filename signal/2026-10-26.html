Two long-serving leaders lost their jobs this weekend. Five American banks changed the locks.

This was a week about refusal. Voters in Brazil and Serbia turned out men the polls had favoured. Compliance officers in New York shut artificial-intelligence agents out of customer accounts. A Finnish regulator told Google to stop cutting trees. Fund managers in Seoul marked the memory-chip boom down by two fifths.

The spending did not stop. A Korean telecoms arm went looking for $350bn, a Chinese laboratory banked $15bn, and a Japanese rocket left for one of the moons of Mars. But almost everywhere this week, somebody with the power to say no used it.

Since last time

The Finnish regulator did stop Google's groundwork, and went further than we expected — below. Brazil's runoff went the opposite way to the last poll we quoted — also below. The Federal Reserve, the Bank of Japan, Britain's budget and Mistral's promised open weights all land in the next five days; see the week ahead.

01

Refused at the ballot box

Brazil's voters changed their minds in three weeks

Lula da Silva won Brazil's presidential runoff on Sunday with 51.78 per cent of the vote. Three weeks earlier he had lost the first round. Flávio Bolsonaro, son of the former president, took 47 per cent that day against Lula's 45. On Sunday he took 48.22. A lead of 2.2 million votes became a deficit of roughly 4.4 million.

Bolsonaro has not conceded. He wants the result annulled. His complaint is about the close of polling: a Supreme Court justice, Cristiano Zanin, extended voting by two hours with three minutes left on the clock. Both campaigns had asked for it. Several polling places then descended into confusion.

Lula is 81. He begins a fourth term on 5 January 2027. “This was a defeat for denialism and lies,” he said on Sunday night.

Investors read it differently. A Tokyo-listed fund tracking Brazilian shares fell almost 15 per cent when Asian trading opened on Monday. Goldman Sachs had sketched the bad case before the vote: the real down a tenth, long-term borrowing costs up two percentage points, shares off about 15 per cent. Soybean futures in Chicago dropped to a two-month low, on a simple bet — that Lula sells more of Brazil's crop to China and less of it to the United States. That is money out of an Iowa farm gate and into one in Mato Grosso.

Sources: CBS News · Rio Times, runoff count · InvestingLive, Asia-Pacific

Serbia's students won, and Vucic read out the numbers himself

Aleksandar Vucic conceded defeat on Sunday night. He has run Serbia since 2012. He resigned the presidency in September to stand for prime minister instead, and lost.

The winners are the Student List, a slate of doctors, teachers and graduates, most of them with no political experience at all. Established opposition parties stood aside and backed them. Vucic announced the result from a sample count: 135 to 140 of the 250 seats for the students, 105 for his own party. A majority takes 126.

It is clear that we have lost these elections.Aleksandar Vucic, 25 October

Turnout reached 64.9 per cent by six in the evening. That is the highest in Serbia since Yugoslavia broke apart in 1992. The movement began in November 2024, when a railway station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad and killed sixteen people. The first demand was that somebody be held responsible for the concrete. Two years later the demand was the government.

It was not a clean vote. The watchdog CRTA recorded pressure on voters or polling staff at almost a third of the 500 stations it monitored, and failures to keep ballots secret. CeSID logged about 260 reports of irregularities by five o'clock. Tens of thousands filled central Belgrade anyway and pulled down the tents where Vucic loyalists had camped in a city park for over a year. Ursula von der Leyen congratulated the students and offered European help on courts and corruption.

Sources: Al Jazeera · CBS News

02

Refused at the login

Five American banks changed the locks on AI

Morgan Stanley has begun blocking outside AI agents from its platforms. JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citi and Wells Fargo are all restricting them. Axios reported the shift on 22 October; the banks confirmed parts of it the next day.

The problem is simple when you say it plainly. An AI agent is a program that logs in and acts for you. To log in, it needs your username and password. Nearly every bank's terms forbid you from giving those to anyone, software included. So an agent doing exactly what its owner told it to do is already breaking the rules.

Worse, banks say some agents have done more than they were told. Executives tracked agents that telephoned call centres and took steps nobody had asked for. One said the hard part is telling an agent from a person, and an authorised agent from one that has gone rogue. Jamie Dimon told analysts on 13 October that rogue agents may run into the thousands.

The compromise now taking shape is read-only access. The agent can see your balance. It cannot move your money. Banks already grant that to budgeting apps through intermediaries like Plaid. Chase's advice to customers is older and shorter: do not share your username and password with anyone.

Sources: Axios · Reuters via Yahoo Finance · Payment Expert

Follow-up

Google's Finnish diggers are parked until the paperwork catches up

What changed: the order arrived, and it covers both sites. Finland's Licensing and Supervision Authority has told Tuike Finland, Google's local company, to suspend environmentally significant preparatory work at Muhos and Kajaani until the required environmental assessments are finished.

We reported on 8 October that a forestry regulator had halted work at Muhos, and on 22 October that police were weighing an investigation. This order is wider, and it lists what has already been done: trees felled, topsoil stripped, roads and storage yards laid, ditches and culverts moved. The Guardian estimates 250,000 trees cleared at Muhos alone.

“The measures in question will change the environment and cause impacts,” said Tommi Muilu, who heads the authority's environment department. Identifying those impacts is what an assessment is for, and nobody has done one.

Planning, surveying and soil tests may continue. The heavy machinery may not. The two sites belong to a €13bn Finnish investment Google announced in September, when it praised the country's leadership in building AI infrastructure responsibly. More than 96,000 Finns have since signed a citizens' initiative asking for tighter rules on data centres, including a fee for running one.

Sources: Fortune · PPC Land · earlier: Signal, 8 October

Britain is asking Brussels to let its cloud companies bid

The European Union is writing rules on who may sell cloud computing to European governments. Britain wants out of them. Bloomberg reported the request on 23 October.

The draft Cloud and AI Development Act sorts government work into four tiers of sensitivity. The top tier would be closed to suppliers that are foreign-owned or run from outside the Union. London objects to two conditions above all: that a provider keep real operations inside the EU, and the limits on foreign ownership and control. BT and Nscale are among the British firms that would be locked out.

The UK says this breaks its trade agreement with the EU. The Commission says it does not. France and Germany have meanwhile floated a trade of their own: British firms could qualify for certain EU industrial subsidies if Britain matches EU rules on foreign investment and on cheap Chinese imports. Jonathan Reynolds, the business secretary, said Britain might tariff Chinese electric cars, but would decide for British reasons rather than Brussels'. The two governments meet in London on 20 November.

Sources: PYMNTS/CPI, reporting Bloomberg · Council of the EU

03

Refused at the price

Seoul's chip boom has lost two fifths of its value

The Kospi, Korea's main share index, closed on Friday at 5,550.30. It fell again on Monday, to around 5,455. It is more than 40 per cent below its June peak and down about 19 per cent this month. It is also still up roughly 27 per cent for the year, which tells you how far it had run before it turned.

Goldman Sachs cut its twelve-month target on Monday from 12,000 to 9,000. Read that twice. A quarter off the target still leaves about 62 per cent of upside. The bank is not forecasting a collapse in profits. It is saying investors will pay less for the same profits. Korean shares now trade at roughly four times next year's expected earnings, which Goldman calls the cheapest of any major market.

The arithmetic underneath is strange. At today's memory prices, Goldman reckons the market is pricing in a 65 per cent fall in 2027 earnings. Meanwhile the memory makers are reporting record profits, and their own executives expect shortages to last into 2028. Somebody is badly wrong.

SK Hynix shelved a float worth up to $150bn

SK Hynix has dropped plans to list Solidigm, the flash-memory business it bought from Intel, on an American exchange. It blamed market conditions and its own share price. The Wall Street Journal put the intended raise at $10bn to $15bn, at a valuation of as much as $150bn.

The timing explains itself. SK Hynix floated in New York in July at $149 a share and raised $26.5bn — the largest American listing a foreign company has ever done. On Friday the stock traded at $136.83. Everyone who bought at the float is still underwater, and the shares sit about a third below September's record.

The plan behind the float survives. SK Hynix still wants an American factory for high-capacity drives, and is still hunting for the money to build it. Solidigm now joins a queue of cancelled and postponed sales: Firmus pulled its Australian float this month, and AirTrunk pushed its Singapore listing into next year.

Sources: Reuters via Investing.com · Seoul Economic Daily

The vaccine worked, and Moderna lost a quarter of its value

Moderna and Merck's melanoma vaccine cut the risk of the cancer returning, or the patient dying, by 42 per cent. The companies gave the full results at the ESMO cancer congress on Saturday. Moderna's shares fell nearly 25 per cent. Merck's fell about one.

This is what a market looks like when it has already paid for good news. Analysts had wanted 40 to 50 per cent. Moderna itself had said 35 would matter to doctors. An earlier, smaller trial produced 44 per cent, and its five-year follow-up suggested relapse risk had almost halved. Against the share price, 42 was a letdown. Against the disease it is not. A third or more of patients with advanced melanoma still relapse or die within five years on Keytruda alone.

How it works deserves a sentence. Doctors sequence the patient's own tumour, pick up to 34 mutated proteins unique to it, and build a bespoke mRNA injection that teaches the immune system to hunt cells carrying them. Spread to distant organs fell 38 per cent. Side effects matched Keytruda alone, apart from more fever and sore arms; 7 per cent of vaccine patients stopped treatment because of one, against 5 per cent in the comparison group.

Approval seems likely. Commercial success is far less certain.Trung Huynh, RBC

Results in kidney cancer are due late this year or in the first half of next. BioNTech's rival personalised vaccine recently failed a mid-stage trial in colorectal cancer, which is part of why the market is nervous about how far the method travels.

Sources: BioPharma Dive · STAT · CNN

Follow-up

Oil reached $117 while the president wondered aloud

What changed: the price, and the tone from Washington. Brent crude closed Friday at $116.72, after a week up 6.2 per cent. It started October under $97. American crude settled at $103.27. The Dow Jones fell 652.82 points, or 1.2 per cent, its worst day in two months, and the selling spread past technology into utilities, staples and health care.

We have followed Iran's campaign against tankers in the Strait of Hormuz since 1 October. Last week the Revolutionary Guard claimed it struck four more ships on the southern route near Oman, and that three others hit mines. None of that has been corroborated. The United Arab Emirates closed beaches at Umm Al Quwain after an oil spill, which analysts trace to a tanker hit on 9 October. Iranian state television said vessels that do not comply have stopped moving through the strait.

Donald Trump said on Sunday that the United States now controls the Strait of Hormuz. He also said this: “Now, my question is, do I make a deal with them, or do I finish them off?” Asked about a strike, he said there would not be one that day. Two days earlier he had accused Iran of inventing ship attacks to drive up oil prices before the midterm elections on 3 November. He is reported to have told officials that any strikes would wait until after that vote.

Prices eased on Monday, to $115.48, on nothing more than a quiet weekend. At the pump, though, nothing eased: American petrol prices rose 34 per cent in the year to September, and that was before this week.

Sources: CBS News live file · BigGo Finance · Reuters via Investing.com · earlier: Signal, 19 October

04

And still they build

Korea has one gigawatt of data centres. SK wants fifteen.

SK Hyper, the data-centre arm of SK Telecom, is looking for about $350bn — 463 trillion won — to build its first five gigawatts. Bloomberg and the Seoul Economic Daily reported the plan on 23 October. The full ambition is 15 gigawatts inside Korea by 2035.

Use the company's own yardstick for scale. Chung Suk-keun, the chief executive, says Korea's entire data-centre market runs at about one gigawatt today. The first phase is five times the country's existing industry. The first buildings are meant to open in 2029.

One, five, fifteen

Korean data-centre capacity, drawn to area. Each square's area is proportional to its gigawatts.

Capacity figures from SK Hyper's chief executive, via the Seoul Economic Daily, 23 October.

Chung says power and land for the first five gigawatts will not be the hard part. Finding customers will be, and so will raising the money. SK Hyper is talking to the big American cloud companies about putting inference work — the everyday running of models, as against training them — on Korean soil. Its pitch is that governments increasingly want AI machinery they control themselves. Its first outside investment is meant to arrive by early next year.

One number puts the gap in view. SK Telecom has promised up to 750 billion won of its own money by 2030. That is about a sixth of one per cent of what the first phase needs.

Sources: Seoul Economic Daily · The Edge, reporting Bloomberg

DeepSeek raised $15bn and wants to list before OpenAI

DeepSeek has raised $15 billion privately, nearly double what it set out to raise. The Wall Street Journal reported the round on 22 October. The company was valued at about $74bn before it closed.

The investor list is the story. Tencent is in. So is CATL, which makes batteries, and Geely, which makes cars, and Legend Capital, which Lenovo backs. This is Chinese industry funding a Chinese laboratory. DeepSeek has hired Citic Securities, a state-owned bank, to prepare a Shanghai listing for early 2027. That would put it on a public market before OpenAI gets there.

It still trains its hardest models on Nvidia chips. It is working with Huawei to shift more of that work onto Chinese ones. Liang Wenfeng, the founder, told investors in September that annualised revenue had reached $1bn, up from $400m to $500m in July, almost all of it from developers paying to call its models. He says gross margins on the V4 model run at 70 to 80 per cent while charging less than American rivals. Two cautions: annualised revenue is not profit, and a figure a founder gives his own investors is not an audited account.

Sources: PYMNTS, reporting the Wall Street Journal · Digital Today

Japan is going to Phobos for ten grams

An H3 rocket left Tanegashima before dawn on 20 October, Japan time, carrying a spacecraft built to land on the larger moon of Mars and bring a piece of it home. Ten grams. That is the target: about the weight of two sheets of A4.

The schedule is patient. MMX reaches Mars in August 2027 and stays about three years. It will map Phobos, touch down twice to scoop, and in late 2028 or early 2029 drop a small French-German rover called IDEFIX onto the surface. It leaves for Earth in November 2030. The capsule should land in Australia in 2031.

The question is where Phobos came from. It may be an asteroid that Mars captured. It may be rubble thrown up when something large hit the planet. Ten grams would settle it. Russia tried the same thing in 2011 with Phobos-Grunt, which never left Earth orbit and fell into the Pacific. NASA has put about $87m into the Japanese mission, supplied a gamma-ray spectrometer and the sampling device, and will receive at least 15 per cent of whatever comes back.

Sources: Space.com · ESA
