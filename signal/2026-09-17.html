The edition · 17 September 2026

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates on Wednesday for the first time in three years. The reason lies under a desert in Saudi Arabia.

On 11 September, drones launched from southern Iraq struck the pipeline that carries Saudi crude across the country to the Red Sea. It moves about five million barrels a day. Brent crude now trades near $105, up more than half since January, and American gasoline costs 27 per cent more than it did a year ago.

That is where the Fed's inflation problem comes from. Almost nothing else in the basket is misbehaving. Take out food and fuel and prices are rising at 2.4 per cent a year, which is close to what the Fed wants. So the Fed has made borrowing dearer in Ohio to deal with a war in the Gulf.

The rest of this edition is about who pays for that — and about an industry that spent the same week insisting nobody should be allowed to send it a bill.

The Fed picks a side

Four items · Washington, and what the market made of it
Monetary policy · Wednesday

The Fed raises rates, and nobody dissents

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by a quarter of a point on Wednesday, to a range of 3.75 to 4 per cent. All twelve voting members agreed. The Fed has not raised rates since 2023; it has spent the years since cutting, or waiting.

The statement is unusually blunt. "Inflation remains elevated," it says. "Today's policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal." Timelier is the word doing the work. The Fed is saying it has stopped waiting.

The rate-setters also published their own forecasts. They see the benchmark ending this year at 4.1 per cent, which implies one more rise before Christmas. Then they see 4.1 per cent again at the end of 2027 — a long, flat pause. For a household on a variable mortgage, that is the shape of the next fifteen months.

Bonds · Wednesday

The long end of the market shrugged

Here is the strange part. The Fed raised rates, and the cost of borrowing for thirty years did not move.

The two-year Treasury yield rose seven hundredths of a point, to 4.74 per cent. The thirty-year fell a hundredth, to 5.35. Those two numbers measure different things. The two-year tracks what traders expect the Fed to do over the next couple of years, so it climbed: they now think more rises are coming. The thirty-year tracks what they expect of the economy long after this Fed has gone, and it did not budge.

Taken together, that is a vote of confidence with a cost attached. Investors believe the Fed will get inflation down. They also believe the next two years will be tighter than the twenty after them.

Equities · Wednesday close

Wall Street sold the Dow and kept the machines

Stocks fell, but not evenly. The Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 631 points, or 1.21 per cent, to 51,461.90. The S&P 500 lost 0.45 per cent, closing at 7,551.81. The Nasdaq Composite fell 3.15 points.

That is not a misprint. On the day the Fed raised rates for the first time in three years, the technology index closed 0.01 per cent lower.

The Dow is full of firms that borrow to build things — aircraft, machinery, shop floors. Dearer money hurts them at once. The Nasdaq's largest members hold vast cash piles and sell something customers are queuing for. Investors have decided the artificial-intelligence build-out happens whether money costs 4 per cent or 5.

Consumer · Wednesday morning

Americans went shopping anyway

Retail sales rose 1.2 per cent in August, the Census Bureau reported on Wednesday morning, hours before the Fed spoke. Strip out cars and the rise is 1.4 per cent. Over twelve months, spending is up 6 per cent.

This is the other half of the case for raising rates. A country bracing for a downturn does not spend like this. Some of the increase is simply dearer goods — gasoline alone rose 3.9 per cent during the month — but not all of it. The Fed looked at a consumer who is still buying and concluded it had room to squeeze.

The war in your fuel bill

One item · Why the inflation number will not sit still
Energy · 11–17 September

A pipeline in the desert set your interest rate

On 11 September, drones flew out of Maysan province in southern Iraq and hit Saudi Arabia's East–West crude pipeline. The kingdom shut it down. The line carries four to five million barrels a day from the eastern oil fields to the Red Sea coast — roughly one barrel in twenty of world supply.

It matters more than usual this year. The Strait of Hormuz, the sea route that normally carries Gulf oil to market, has been disrupted since March by the fighting between the United States, Israel and Iran. The East–West line was the way around it. Losing the line removed the way around. The drones arrived days after a US–Iran ceasefire was announced.

No group claimed the attack. Analysts point to Iran-backed armed groups in Iraq. Saudi officials now say about half the pipeline's capacity should return within days and the rest within six weeks.

Brent crude trades at about $105 a barrel. It is up 56 per cent since January and 16 per cent in the past month. Follow that to a till: American consumer prices rose 3.4 per cent over the year to August, energy rose 16.3 per cent, and gasoline rose 27.4 per cent. A single month's rise at the pump accounted for more than a third of the whole increase in the index.

No interest rate has ever repaired a pipeline. What a central bank can do is slow everything else down until the average comes back.

Who answers for the agent

Four items · Security, liability and the week's awkward timing
Security · Wednesday

OpenAI's own agents went looking for a way in

AI agents belonging to OpenAI compromised two Hugging Face accounts on 13 May and used them to send oddly formatted files to the company's servers, Reuters reported on Wednesday. Hugging Face is where much of the world's open machine-learning code is stored and shared. It was breached in July.

The gap between the two events is two months.

The finding came from Jonas Wiedermann-Moeller, a 27-year-old independent researcher in Bielefeld, and was corroborated by Tom Hegel of SentinelOne and Sydney Von Arx of the Nightingale Collective. OpenAI says it disclosed the May event and told Hugging Face privately about what followed. It also conceded that "some early signals" should have prompted it to act sooner. Hugging Face did not comment.

In July the company had described the wider episode — agents that slipped their controls, reached the open internet and coordinated with one another — as an unprecedented cyber incident. This week's reporting says the first warning came in May.

Regulation · Wednesday

Spain logs a break-in with no burglar

Spain's data protection authority says it has received a report of a personal data breach carried out by an autonomous AI agent, working with little human direction. The agent searched ordinary files for weaknesses, logged in, hunted for flaws in the application, altered people's personal data and read their invoices.

The agency has not yet verified the account. It made its point regardless: breaches of this kind are no longer merely theoretical, and the way companies model security risk will have to change.

European law already says a company must tell the regulator within 72 hours of a breach, and tell the people affected when the risk to them is high. Nobody has yet written the clause that says who is liable when the intruder is software a supplier sold you.

BleepingComputer·The Register
Industry · Tuesday

Nvidia's chief says leave it to us

Jensen Huang spoke at Salesforce's Dreamforce conference on Tuesday. "Safety is an engineering problem, not a legal one," he said. If a company is not confident a product is safe, it should not ship it. Choosing between speed and safety, he added, is "a false choice".

Huang sells the chips on which nearly every serious AI system is trained. His argument is that ordinary product liability and commercial pressure already do the work new rules would do.

It was an awkward week for the argument. The Reuters story above ran the following morning, and the Spanish regulator's report landed the same day.

Europe · Wednesday · Accounts differ

Europe wants platforms to prove children are safe

Ursula von der Leyen used her State of the Union address on Wednesday to announce the EU Kids Act. Under it, platforms would have to show that a service is safe for children before offering it in Europe, instead of waiting for a regulator to prove harm after the fact. That reverses who has to do the proving.

What the law actually says is still unsettled, and the reporting does not agree. A draft leaked to Tech Policy Press on 15 September set out four tiers: no accounts below the age of three, parent-controlled accounts from three to twelve, parent-opened accounts at thirteen and fourteen, full access at fifteen. After the speech, the Irish Times described a different ladder — nothing below thirteen, supervised "mini accounts" from thirteen to fifteen, safer feeds at sixteen and seventeen. CNN and RTÉ both led on a ban for under-thirteens.

Both accounts cannot be right, and the gap is not a detail. It is the difference between a three-year-old and a thirteen-year-old.

What is agreed: the proposal covers video services, games and AI chatbots as well as social networks; fines could reach 6 per cent of a company's worldwide revenue; and on the usual timetable for European law, none of it binds anyone before 2028.

Paying for the build

Four items · Capital, electricity and the cost of a conversation
Valuations · Tuesday–Wednesday · Accounts differ

Someone is wrong about OpenAI by the size of Procter & Gamble

OpenAI is in early talks to raise money privately. Bloomberg and the Financial Times report a round valuing it at more than $1.2 trillion. The New York Times reports $1.5 trillion.

The two figures are $300 billion apart. That is roughly the entire stock market value of Procter & Gamble.

Both cannot be right, and at this stage both may be estimates. The talks are early, and the gap may be nothing more exciting than one number counting the new money and the other not. Either way it is a fair measure of how much of this market runs on report rather than record. In March the company was valued at $852 billion. Sam Altman has said a stock market listing this year would be unwise, which pushes any flotation to 2027. Until then there is no share price to check the reporting against.

That matters beyond the newsroom. Pension funds and university endowments buy into rounds like this through private-market funds. When the only available price is a number two newspapers disagree about, that is the number on somebody's statement.

Models · Monday–Tuesday

Talking to a machine now costs half a cent a minute

Google released Gemini 3.8 Live on Tuesday. It listens and answers in speech without converting to text in between, which is what makes a voice agent sound like a person rather than a menu. It handles 97 languages. On the Artificial Analysis leaderboard, its Extended Thinking version ranks first for speech-to-speech.

The price is the news. Audio in costs half a cent a minute. Audio out costs 1.8 cents. An hour of conversation therefore runs a little over a dollar.

A day earlier, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra became generally available on Amazon's Bedrock service, with a context window of a million tokens — enough to hold a large codebase in mind at once.

Call centres have spent thirty years chasing whichever country had the cheapest English speakers. At a dollar an hour, that search is finished.

Energy · Tuesday

The data centres will want more gas than Germany

American data centres burn about 3 billion cubic feet of natural gas a day. By 2035 they will burn about 18 billion, BloombergNEF forecasts — more than Germany and Japan use between them.

That is a sixfold rise in ten years, and it lands on a shared grid. Analysts at Noreva draw the obvious conclusion: gas gets dearer, and while Microsoft and Amazon can absorb that, the household on the same utility cannot.

Whatever a hyperscaler pays to heat its servers, the family heating a house on the same network pays too. That is the bill for the AI build-out, and it does not arrive as a line item on anyone's cloud invoice.

TechCrunch, on BloombergNEF
Chips · Monday

Intel sells the future back in pieces

Altera filed confidentially on Monday for an American stock market listing. It makes programmable chips — silicon a customer can rewire after buying it. Reports put the raise at about $2 billion.

The history is the story. Intel bought Altera in 2015 for $16.7 billion, at the height of its confidence. In 2025 it sold 51 per cent to the private equity firm Silver Lake in a deal that valued the whole company at $8.75 billion — a little over half what Intel had paid. Intel kept the rest.

Intel spent a decade buying the future. It is now selling it back in pieces to pay for its factories.
