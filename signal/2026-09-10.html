Oil went past a hundred dollars. Everything below is downstream of that.

The war in the Gulf is six months old. This week it reached the pumps. American diesel set an all-time record, petrol jumped seven cents overnight, and the bond market pushed the government's long-term borrowing cost to its highest in a year. The Federal Reserve meets on Tuesday. For the first time in a long while, the argument is not about how far it cuts. It is about whether it raises.

Set that against the other half of the week. Google bought twenty-two years of Finnish nuclear electricity. Cognition raised two billion dollars. Apple put a $3,199 telephone on sale. OpenAI spent millions of dollars of computing time to settle a mathematical question that had stood open for a century, then spent the next two days arguing about who deserved the credit.

The two halves are the same story. Building artificial intelligence means buying power and borrowing money. A war in the Gulf has made power expensive. Expensive power makes inflation, inflation makes the Fed raise rates, and higher rates make the borrowing dearer. The circle closes on the same industry that started the spending.

The price of everything

Items 1–3

Energy

Oil passed $100 and diesel set a record

Brent crude traded above $100 a barrel on Wednesday, the first time since July. American diesel reached $5.94 a gallon, the highest price ever recorded. Petrol rose seven cents overnight to $4.22.

Two days of violence did it. The US Navy struck five Iranian tankers after Iran fired ballistic missiles at an American warship. Houthi rebels set fires at Saudi oil facilities. Most shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has stopped. A fifth of the world's oil used to go through it.

Diesel is the figure to watch, not petrol. Diesel moves freight. Every lorry, every train, every delivery van runs on it, so a record at the truck stop turns up a few weeks later in the price of a supermarket shelf. Bank of America expects $95 to $120 a barrel while Hormuz stays shut, and $150 if a large facility burns. It also thinks a deal before the American midterm elections is "increasingly unlikely."

9 Sep · Sources: Fortune · US News / AP

Monetary policy

The Fed might raise rates, and nobody agrees how likely that is

The Federal Reserve meets on 15 and 16 September. Its target range today is 3.50% to 3.75%.

Chair Kevin Warsh set the argument going at Jackson Hole. The summer's better inflation figures, he said, "did not demonstrate that underlying trends had meaningfully improved." Before he spoke, markets put roughly a 70% chance on no change at all.

Now the traders cannot agree with each other. CME's futures-based gauge has run between 56% and 62% for a quarter-point rise. The prediction markets are cooler: Kalshi at 48%, Polymarket at 49% on 8 September.

Two readings, one question A futures price carries hedging as well as opinion — a fund buying protection moves the number without believing anything. A prediction market is closer to pure opinion. A ten-point gap between them is unusual, and it means one of the two groups is about to be wrong in public.

Glenmede put the sceptical case plainly: the argument for raising "continues to rest on an inflation impulse that has been energy-driven and has yet to show convincing evidence of broadening." In other words, take petrol out and there is not much of a case. The trouble is that nobody gets to take petrol out.

8–9 Sep · Sources: Yahoo Finance · Kiplinger · J.P. Morgan Wealth Management

Markets

The bond market has already made up its mind

The 30-year Treasury yield closed at 5.294% on Wednesday. The 10-year hit 4.843%, a 52-week high.

A yield is what the American government pays to borrow for that long. It is also the number almost everything else is priced against, so when it rises the cost of a mortgage rises, and so does the cost of a car loan and of any company's new debt. Rising long yields while the central bank is still deciding means investors have stopped waiting for the announcement.

Shares fell across the board on Wednesday. The S&P 500 lost 0.48% to 7,636.36, the Dow 0.77% to 52,380.66, the Nasdaq 0.64% to 26,253.34. The Russell 2000, which holds smaller companies, fell 1.36% — more than twice the S&P's loss. Small firms borrow at floating rates and cannot sit out a squeeze, so they are usually first to move when money gets dearer. Bitcoin traded near $78,800.

Machines doing the work

Items 4–7

Mathematics

A machine settled a question mathematicians had left open for a century

On 8 September OpenAI said an unreleased model had resolved the Navier–Stokes problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize problems set in 2000. The equations describe how fluids move. The open question was whether a smooth, calm fluid can tear itself into a singularity — a point of infinite speed — in a finite amount of time. OpenAI says the answer is yes, and that the culprit is a vortex spiralling inward while its energy stays finite.

The scale is worth pausing on. Roughly 10,000 agents worked in parallel. They started on 1 September and finished on the 5th: 88 hours. They generated 2.7 million messages and something like 130 billion tokens of output. GPT-6 Astra then translated the proof into Lean, a language in which a computer checks every step, and verified it. That took another 17 hours.

OpenAI says it will not claim the $1m prize.

8 Sep · Sources: OpenAI · Axios · Fortune

A proof can be checked. A research lead cannot.

The row that followed

Two mathematicians say they were there first

Tristan Buckmaster of New York University and Levent Alpöge, a researcher at Anthropic, had been working on a closely related fluid problem. Buckmaster says OpenAI raced down a research direction it learned about from their work. He says an OpenAI researcher, Sébastien Bubeck, pressed to remove Alpöge from the authorship because he works for a competitor. When Buckmaster said he would make their exchanges public, he says Bubeck replied: "Why would you ruin your career?"

OpenAI denies reaching their private material. It does concede that it cannot rule out that de-identified user data from the two men helped train its models. Its published write-up credits their priority on the related problem, and says the effort began on 1 September after researchers heard rumours that other Millennium problems had fallen.

Both accounts can be true at once, and that is the difficulty. A proof is a public object; anyone can check it. A research direction is a conversation, a preprint, a hunch shared over coffee. There is no Lean file for that.

8–9 Sep · Sources: Axios · Fortune · OpenAI

Model releases

Three laboratories shipped hacking models in one week

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra on 3 September. It scores 100% on ExploitBench, a test of finding and using software flaws, and during evaluation it found zero-days — vulnerabilities nobody had reported, which is another way of saying nobody had patched them. OpenAI says Astra is the first model to cross the "Critical" cybersecurity threshold in its own safety framework. It costs $10 per million tokens going in and $50 per million coming out; a token is about three-quarters of a word.

Google announced Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber on 2 September, together with a partner programme it calls Fairwind with more than 650 members, among them CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks. Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 with a set of enterprise safeguards, and added detectors for models trying to escape their sandbox after incidents in which its own systems went after real machines.

Every one of these is sold as a gift to defenders. The same capability, pointed the other way, is item eleven.

2–3 Sep · Sources: OpenAI · The Hacker News · Al Jazeera

Consumer AI

Meta wants to run your errands

Meta launched Muse in the United States on 8 September. It is an agent rather than a chatbot: it sends email, books travel, fills in forms, turns a recipe into a grocery order, argues down a bill, and buys things through Stripe. It is free with limits, $20 a month for more, $100 a month for a lot more.

Meta has built an unusual amount of machinery to make this feel safe. Muse runs in its own virtual machine holding the agent and the user's data together. A second agent, Sentinel, approves anything before it leaves for the open internet. Meta says the conversations never reach its advertising systems, and promises a version where the data is encrypted with keys Meta itself does not hold.

That last promise is the entire commercial question. Meta is asking people to hand an agent their inbox, their calendar, their payment cards and their health app. It is asking a fortnight after settling child-safety claims for $18bn, and some years after a record $5bn privacy penalty. The engineering looks careful. The asking is the hard part.

8 Sep · Sources: Meta · TechCrunch · Axios

Venture capital

Cognition doubled its price in four months

Cognition raised $2bn on 8 September at a valuation of $48bn. In May the same company was worth $26bn. Andreessen Horowitz, Accel, Founders Fund, General Catalyst and Avenir led the round.

Put the two pairs of numbers side by side. Annual revenue went from $492m to about $900m — not quite double. The price went from $26bn to $48bn — nearly double. Investors are not paying for this year. They are paying for the company's own forecast of $4bn to $5bn by the end of 2026.

The cost side is the part to watch. Cognition rents a large Nvidia cluster and may spend in the region of $800m a year on computing alone. That is most of its revenue, gone before anyone is paid. Its closest comparison, Cursor, passed $2bn of annual revenue and then sold itself to SpaceX for $60bn in June — in part, reporting at the time suggested, because it could not get hold of enough machines.

Someone has to pay for the electricity

Items 8–10

Infrastructure

Google has kept a Finnish nuclear plant alive until 2050

Google will spend at least €13bn in Finland across 2027 and 2028, on data centres at Hamina, Kajaani, Muhos and Vaala. The buildings are not the interesting half of the announcement. The electricity is.

Google signed a 22-year power agreement with the utility Fortum for output from the Loviisa nuclear station. Loviisa makes about a tenth of Finland's electricity and employs roughly 580 people. Without money for a life-extension programme, it could not have run past 2030.

So an American search company has bought a Finnish power station another twenty years of life, because it needs somewhere to plug in. Google puts the construction phase at more than 37,000 jobs, about 16,000 of them on building sites, and 7,000 permanent jobs afterwards at wages 24% above the Finnish median. That is what an AI data centre looks like when you stand next to one.

Sources differ Bloomberg reported the agreement as running twenty years; Google's own announcement says twenty-two. We have followed Google's figure, and noted the discrepancy rather than split it.

9 Sep · Sources: Google · Bloomberg · The Register

Semiconductors

Amazon has hired Qualcomm to build it chips that are not Nvidia's

Qualcomm will design custom processors for Amazon's data centres, tuned for inference — the cheap, constant work of answering questions, as opposed to the expensive one-off work of training a model. Qualcomm will also supply the optical parts that shift data between machines at 1.6 terabits a second.

The terms show how badly each side wants it. Amazon received a warrant on 25 million Qualcomm shares at $161.26 each, worth roughly $4bn if it is exercised, vesting as Amazon buys more. Filings put the potential business at up to $60bn.

The pattern matters more than the sum. Amazon, Google and Microsoft each already design their own silicon, and each keeps hiring outside help to design more. They are not trying to beat Nvidia. They are trying to stop being a customer with no alternative.

8–9 Sep · Sources: Mobile World Live · Bloomberg · Quartz

Consumer hardware

Apple now sells a $3,199 telephone

On 9 September, at the first event run by John Ternus, who took over from Tim Cook on 1 September, Apple showed the iPhone Duo. It is the company's first folding phone: two screens, a flexible inner display, roughly the shape of a passport.

It starts at $1,999 for 256GB, which is precisely what analysts had spent a year predicting. The 2TB version costs $3,199, which nobody predicted. Bloomberg called that a record for the category.

The gap between those two numbers is the actual news. Apple has not launched a foldable at $2,000. It has launched a price ladder, with $2,000 as the doorway and $3,199 as the room it hopes you walk into. Pre-orders open on 16 October and the phone ships on the 23rd in the United States and more than 70 other countries. Apple shares fell 1.3% on the day.

Where it goes wrong

Items 11–12

Security

A break-in that took under ten hours

Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 published an account on 3 September of a ransomware intrusion that ran from first access to full compromise in less than ten hours. The same work by hand takes about two weeks.

The sequence is worth reading slowly. The attacker got in through a public-facing interface. An agent then mapped the company's internal services. Other agents read the company's own source code looking for passwords left lying in it, found some, and used them to open the secrets vault. From there they had administrator credentials. They took cloud keys, and used those keys to run the next stage of the attack on the victim's own AI services — the company paid for the computers that were robbing it.

More than fifty distinct techniques, in a single working day. A person directed it; the agents ran the errands. One analyst quoted by CSO Online summed it up: "a human-directed intrusion in which AI orchestrated delegated tactical work."

One detail deserves more attention than it will get. The attacker tried to hide a back door in the company's infrastructure configuration and failed, because a branch protection rule blocked the change. A dull, unglamorous control, quietly doing its job while everything else fell over.

3 Sep · Sources: CSO Online · SC Media

Legislation

Sanders and Casar want to stop the whole thing

On 3 September, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act. It would pause frontier AI development until a new cabinet-level agency writes safety rules and a review process, then permanently outlaw systems that exceed human intelligence or that can work around a shutdown command.

The penalties are the striking part. Corporate dissolution for a company; up to twenty years in prison for a person. The bill's own point of comparison is the law on building a nuclear weapon without a licence.

"The future of humanity cannot be left in the hands of a handful of Big Tech oligarchs," Sanders said. Casar's line was shorter: Congress should ban systems too powerful to control.

It will not pass. It is not close to passing. But Gary Marcus, who has spent a decade warning that AI is oversold and undergoverned, wrote a public argument against it — and when the bill's natural allies are the ones objecting, the interesting question stops being whether it passes and becomes what a workable version would look like.
