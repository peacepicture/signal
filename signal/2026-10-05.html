The machines are being bought on credit now. This was the week the lenders began setting the terms.

Broadcom spent Friday assembling $60 billion of debt so that Anthropic can have chips. Amazon is in talks to sell $8 billion of chips it already owns and rent them straight back. An Australian company opens its share sale tomorrow on the strength of sheds full of Nvidia cards. None of this is money raised from shareholders. It is borrowed, and borrowed money has a price.

The price moved this week, the wrong way for the borrowers. The extra yield demanded to hold risky American company debt rose by 31 hundredths of a percentage point in five sessions. The largest new deal of the cycle — $52 billion for the Paramount–Warner merger — lost its buyers money on its first day of trading.

Then Friday morning arrived. America added 29,000 jobs in September and took back 60,000 it had claimed for July and August. Traders scrapped the Federal Reserve's October rate rise within the hour. Cheaper money is coming back, in other words, just as the people building the machines work out how much of it they need. Outside America the strain is plainer. France pays a full point more than Germany to borrow, British long bonds are at a 1998 high, and Indian shares have now fallen eight weeks running.

I

The chips arrive with a loan attached

Three of the world's largest technology companies went to the debt market this week. None of them went to the stock market.

Broadcom · 2 October

Broadcom will borrow $60 billion so Anthropic can have chips

Broadcom began raising $60 billion of debt on Friday. The money buys computer chips for Anthropic.

Borrow it — not raise it from shareholders, borrow it. Banks are syndicating $42 billion of the senior portion, the slice that gets repaid first. Blackstone leads the other $18 billion, the slice that gets repaid last, and is putting up $9 billion of its own. Broadcom has separately agreed to lend Anthropic as much as $42 billion directly. If Anthropic cannot pay in cash, that loan can convert into shares in Anthropic.

Blackstone's $9 billion is not, in the main, Blackstone's own. The firm invests money gathered from pension funds, insurers and endowments. Those are the people lending Anthropic its processors, whether or not they have heard of Claude.

Anthropic has committed $125.2 billion over five years to rent Broadcom's chips. The $42 billion loan covers roughly a third of that bill. The machines start arriving in 2027.

Read the shape of it once more. The chipmaker is lending its customer the money to buy the chips, and becomes a part-owner if the customer cannot pay. Broadcom is now both Anthropic's supplier and its creditor. Anthropic is expected to be Broadcom's largest compute customer next year.

Sources Reuters via Yahoo Finance · Quartz · Bloomberg

Amazon · 2 October

Amazon wants to sell its chips and rent them back

Amazon is in talks to move about $8 billion of Nvidia chips off its own books, the Financial Times reported on Friday.

The chips are already installed and running. Amazon would sell them to a company set up for the purpose, lease them back, and carry on using them exactly as before. It has discussed selling outside investors as much as a tenth of that company.

Nothing changes in the data centre. Everything changes on the balance sheet. Money Amazon spent becomes money somebody else spent, and Amazon pays rent instead. Airlines have done this with aircraft for fifty years. Retailers do it with shops. It is new for computers, because computers used to be cheap enough not to bother.

Amazon has not commented. The question worth holding on to is who owns the chips in 2031, when the models that run on them are four generations old and the rent is still due.

Sources Financial Times, via aggregated coverage · TechStartups

Australia · 6–22 October

Nobody can agree what Australia's biggest AI float is worth

Firmus Technologies opens its share sale tomorrow and lists in Sydney on 22 October. It would be the second-largest flotation in Australian history, behind the sale of Telstra in 1997.

What the company owns is concrete enough. Seven sites, two of them already running. More than 900 megawatts of contracted computing capacity. A 360-megawatt campus on Batam, an Indonesian island a ferry ride from Singapore, built around a $30 billion Nvidia agreement signed in June and planned to hold up to 170,000 Nvidia accelerators. OpenAI signed up for two Malaysian sites last month. Nvidia and Blackstone are already shareholders, alongside Coatue, Jane Street and the Pratt family.

What it is worth is not concrete at all.

Where the reporting conflicts

Cryptopolitan puts the raise at A$7 billion and the valuation near A$50 billion. Bloomberg, citing the Australian Financial Review, reported a target of about US$30 billion, and other accounts put the raise nearer A$5.5 billion. A private round in July valued Firmus at A$15.5 billion. We cannot reconcile these, so we are printing them side by side rather than choosing.

One thing every version agrees on. A Blackstone-led facility is lending the company US$10 billion, and Australian fund managers have spent the fortnight asking out loud whether that debt and that valuation can both be right. Bloomberg reported on Sunday that roughly half the offer has been set aside for Nvidia and Blackstone, who already own shares.

This is the retail end of the story in Section I. A pensioner in Perth will shortly be able to buy, through an ordinary brokerage account, a stake in graphics cards that OpenAI rents.

Sources Cryptopolitan · Bloomberg · Startup Fortune

II

Twenty-nine thousand

The American labour market stopped growing. The bond market only half believed it.

Labour Department · 2 October

America added 29,000 jobs, and lost 10,000 it thought it had

The United States added 29,000 jobs in September. Forecasters had expected around three times as many.

The revisions are the real story. July was first reported as a gain of 21,000 jobs. It is now a loss of 10,000. August came down from 162,000 to 133,000. Between them, 60,000 jobs the government said existed in August do not exist today.

Unemployment rose to 4.2 per cent from 4.1. Average pay went up five cents an hour, to $37.81, which is 3.0 per cent more than a year ago. Prices are rising faster than that. So the typical American worker got a pay rise and can buy less with it than last September.

Hospitals and clinics added 17,000 jobs. Construction added 11,000, factories 9,000. Finance lost 7,000. Take out health care and the private economy barely moved at all.

Source Bureau of Labor Statistics, Employment Situation, 2 October 2026

Follow-up · Federal Reserve

Two Fed deputies talked the October rise out of the market

On Thursday this letter reported that traders put the odds of another Federal Reserve rate rise in October at 37 per cent. By Friday afternoon they were somewhere in the high teens.

Two things moved them. First the officials spoke. On Thursday the Fed's vice chair, Philip Jefferson, told an audience in Charlottesville that the central bank "has time to assess the economy before considering another increase." Borrowing costs had already risen across the board since September, he said: "a sign that investors are reassessing the evolving macroeconomic landscape." John Williams, who runs the New York Fed, had said something similar two days earlier. Governor Michelle Bowman saw "little urgency to move rates again."

Then Friday's jobs number arrived and settled the argument.

Recall why the Fed raised rates on 16 September, its first increase since 2023, to a range of 3.75 to 4.00 per cent. Oil was pushing prices up and the economy looked strong enough to take the medicine. Six weeks later the economy is adding 29,000 jobs a month. The committee meets again on 27 and 28 October.

Where the reporting conflicts

CME's FedWatch tool was quoted at 21.59 per cent on Friday afternoon. Yahoo Finance's market desk put it at 16 per cent, down from 64 per cent a week earlier. CNBC declined to give a number and wrote that traders now saw little chance of a rise. All three agree on the direction. None agrees on the figure, which is why this item gives a range.

Sources Jefferson's remarks, 1 October · CNBC · FOMC press conference, 16 September · Signal, 1 October

Follow-up · Credit markets

The $52 billion of merger bonds went on sale, and buyers lost money by Thursday lunchtime

Last Thursday this letter described $52 billion of borrowing the market could barely swallow. It has now swallowed it, and been sick.

The money pays for Paramount's purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery. The bonds began trading on Thursday 1 October. By the close, the eight-year investment-grade slice had slipped to around 96 cents on the dollar, handing its buyers over $100 million of paper losses in a day. A $6 billion tranche paying 8.25 per cent, due in 2031, fell to $97.50. An unsecured bond due in 2036 dropped six points, from $85 to $79; the yield it offers rose from 9.25 to 10.4 per cent. A euro-denominated piece was pulled before it reached anyone.

Fund managers rang Bank of America and Citigroup to complain.

One-day choppiness in the market.Dennis Cinelli, chief financial officer, Skydance

The wider signal is cleaner than any single bond price. The extra yield investors demand to hold risky American corporate debt went from 2.93 per cent above Treasuries on 25 September to 3.24 per cent on 1 October. Thirty-one hundredths of a percentage point in five sessions. That is what it looks like when the bond market is asked to fund too much at once.

And it was asked. SoftBank's record $11.1 billion of junk bonds, raised to pay for the last slice of its OpenAI stake, settled on the very same day — the seven-and-a-half-year piece at 9.75 per cent. Broadcom turned up the next morning asking for $60 billion more.

Sources Quartz on the first day of trading · ICE BofA US High Yield spread, via FRED · Japan Times on SoftBank's pricing · Signal, 1 October

III

The trouble is not only American

Europe's problem is a French budget. The euro's problem is a pipeline. India's problem is eight weeks old.

France · 2 October

France now pays a full point more than Germany to borrow

The gap between French and German ten-year borrowing costs went past one percentage point this week. It has not been that wide since the euro-zone debt crisis.

A budget caused it. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu's draft for 2027 proposes €54 billion of savings and promises to bring the deficit down to 5 per cent of national income. This year's will be around 5.4 per cent, and the national debt is close to 120 per cent of the whole economy. Investors read the plan and asked for more to lend. French two-year borrowing costs rose to 3.73 per cent while Germany's sat still at 3.05.

What that gap means in a living room: France's government pays about €10 million more a year in interest for every billion euros it borrows for a decade, compared with its neighbour, for no reason other than that lenders trust it less. That money comes out of the same budget Lecornu is trying to cut.

France is not alone. British thirty-year government debt yielded 6 per cent on Thursday, the highest since February 1998. Euro-area inflation jumped to 3.8 per cent in September from 3.2, the fastest since 2023, driven by energy prices rising 18.8 per cent in a year. Strip energy out and it is 2.3 per cent. The European Central Bank's difficulty is a pipeline, not a wage spiral — but it has to set one interest rate for both.

Christine Lagarde said in late September that "measured" increases remain appropriate. Markets now price a December rise at about 82 per cent. European bank shares had their worst week since April.

Sources Eurostat flash inflation estimate, 2 October · Reuters on the French–German spread · Reuters on 30-year gilts

Energy · 2 October

Europe opened the diesel tanks

European governments agreed on Friday to release 50 million barrels of diesel from emergency stockpiles over twenty days, alongside 50 million barrels of crude oil.

The proposal was French. Emmanuel Macron chaired the G7 videoconference that preceded it. It was not immediately clear that the other G7 members had signed up to France's numbers.

Note what is actually short. Not oil — diesel. The Strait of Hormuz is still closed, refineries in the Middle East are damaged, and China has suspended exports of refined petroleum products for October. Crude can be found. The fuel that moves lorries, trains, combine harvesters and container ships cannot. That is why a European agriculture minister cares more about this release than about the price of a barrel.

Prices behaved accordingly. Brent settled at $102.25, barely moved on the day. American WTI crude fell 1.9 per cent to $91.11, because the release lands squarely on the grade that American refiners buy. Brent costs 56.7 per cent more than it did a year ago.

Europe has just agreed to release a massive amount of their heavily stocked Diesel Oil.President Trump, Friday

Sources Reuters · EnergyNow on the week's settlements

India · week to 1 October

Indian shares have fallen for eight weeks, which last happened in 2001

The Nifty 50 closed at 22,421.95 on Thursday, down 3.1 per cent for the week. The Sensex closed at 71,909.70, down 2.69 per cent. For both, it was an eighth consecutive weekly fall.

India has not had a losing run that long since 2001. The Nifty now sits 8.7 per cent below its August high. Foreign investors have pulled a reported $27.8 billion out of Indian shares this year, a record — though that figure appears in only one report and should be read as an estimate.

The causes are all imported. Brent above $100 is a tax on a country that buys most of its oil abroad. Rising American yields pull money out of Mumbai and back into Treasuries. A weak monsoon did the rest.

Indian markets were shut on Friday, so the eight-week streak has not yet met the news that America added 29,000 jobs. On its face that number helps India: lower dollar yields make emerging-market shares worth more. Whether it lands that way is this week's question, and it is a bigger one than it looks. Rising American interest rates have been quietly taxing savers in Mumbai, Jakarta and São Paulo all year.

Sources Business Standard · Reuters via Business Recorder

IV

The institutions push back

A city council, a medical-records company, a search engine and a physics archive all changed their rules this week because of what the models are doing.

Follow-up · New York

Four AI labs answer questions under oath in New York today

At eleven this morning all 51 members of the New York City Council sit as a single committee and take evidence from the companies building frontier artificial intelligence. OpenAI, Google, Anthropic and Meta have agreed to testify under oath. Elon Musk's xAI did not answer the invitation and has been subpoenaed.

Getting them there took three weeks. Speaker Julie Menin wrote to five chief executives in mid-September, with a deadline of 25 September. Meta agreed at once. Google and Anthropic refused. Both changed their minds on Sunday 27 September — Anthropic late that night, hours before its subpoena was due to issue. None of the five chief executives is expected to appear in person.

The bills they will be asked about have teeth. The main one requires an outside validator to check an AI system for data quality, bias, privacy and security before anyone deploys it, and requires a human override: a switch a person can throw. The fine is $25,000 for each breach. Menin has been precise about what "each" means.

If there's a swarm of agents, the penalty would apply per agent.Julie Menin, Speaker of the New York City Council

A thousand agents misbehaving is therefore a $25 million bill. Other bills in the package pay whistleblowers a share of the fines recovered, protect city employees who report AI safety problems, and let a New Yorker sue an AI company directly when a jailbroken tool causes foreseeable harm.

A city council cannot regulate a frontier laboratory. It can make one sit down and answer questions, which no American legislature had managed before. Gary Marcus, the NYU professor emeritus who is testifying, will propose something closer to a medicines regulator: outside validation before release, and legal protection for the people inside who raise the alarm.

Sources Fortune · City & State New York · Council calendar · Gary Marcus on his testimony · Signal, 1 October

OpenAI · 30 September

OpenAI says people connected to a Chinese lab were stealing its model's reasoning

OpenAI published an unusual document on 30 September. It accuses a named Chinese competitor of industrial theft.

The claim is specific. From 1 July a group of accounts worked to make OpenAI's models reveal their hidden reasoning — the working-out a model does before it answers, which OpenAI encrypts and does not show. On 24 and 25 July the effort spiked: 16,000 requests using one extraction technique, from more than 4,000 accounts. The wider network ran to more than 15,000 accounts. OpenAI says it had shut the whole thing down by 28 July.

The method was ingenious. The accounts copied encrypted reasoning out of one conversation and pasted it into another, coaxing the model into decrypting its own hidden workings.

OpenAI attributes "a core cluster of the activity to individuals associated with Moonshot AI, the developer of Kimi." Read that wording twice. Individuals associated with the company, not the company. OpenAI says it cannot attribute the rest of the network at all.

Moonshot AI has not responded publicly. Silence is not a denial, and it is not an admission either.

Why this matters beyond two companies: a hidden reasoning trace is close to the only thing a frontier lab holds that cannot be read off a published paper. This is the first time one has formally accused a named rival of trying to take it. The first session of the new United States–China dialogue on advanced AI is due before the end of November, and this will be in the room.

Sources OpenAI's own report · CNBC · Quartz

Health records · 2 October

An AI model read Epic's software and found a way into patient records

Epic Systems has halted most of its product development for six weeks to fix security holes. Its founder, Judy Faulkner, told customers so last week.

Epic runs MyChart, the site and app through which Americans read their test results and message their doctor. It holds more than 320 million patient records. Stirling Martin, Epic's chief security officer, said that in some hospitals' configurations an outsider could reach patient records without the software recording that anyone had been there. He said it is not yet clear whether records could also have been altered without trace.

The holes were found by Mythos, Anthropic's model built for cybersecurity work, during a deployment at Epic. No evidence has emerged that anybody exploited them.

This is the encouraging version of the story. A machine read the code, found what people had missed, and a company holding 320 million medical histories stopped everything else for six weeks to close it. The uncomfortable half is that the same capability is for sale to people who are not Epic's customers.

Source TechCrunch

The commons · 1–4 October

Google stopped taking bug reports because machines wrote too many of them

Google closed part of its bug bounty on 1 October. The programme pays outsiders who find security holes in open-source software. It will not take new product submissions until the first quarter of 2027.

Google's stated reason is "a significant rise in automated submissions, the vast majority of which are not valid." In plain terms: people point AI models at open-source code, forward whatever the model claims to have found, and hope. Engineers and unpaid volunteers then have to read it all. Linux kernel maintainers describe something near 2,000 vulnerability reports per release. Intel has suspended a programme that paid up to $100,000 a flaw.

The same week, arXiv did something similar. The preprint server where physicists and computer scientists post work before review now accepts at most two submissions per author per month, and three live at a time. September 2016 brought 9,869 papers. September 2024 brought 20,569. September 2026 brought 40,363, and roughly 9,000 support tickets. arXiv's own term for part of what it is drowning in is "salami papers" — one result sliced thin enough to make several.

Now set this beside the item above. In one, a model read code and found real flaws nobody had seen. In the other, models buried the same kind of institution in flaws that were not there. The tool is identical. The difference is who is holding it, and whether anybody checks the output before sending it on.
