Everything this week came with a number attached. Almost none of the numbers agreed.

Fourteen megahertz of radio changed hands on Thursday night. By Friday's close America's three big phone companies were worth tens of billions less. OpenAI's sales are either $50bn a year or $70bn, depending on which newspaper you read and who counts the resellers. The biggest Australian float in thirty years was priced at A$11 a share; the fund managers buying it had settled on nine. A study of 718 companies found that coding machines write a third more code and finish no more work. The lesson is not that the numbers are false. It is that a number means nothing until you know who counted it, and what they left outside the frame.

Section one

Count it again

Telecoms

Fourteen megahertz was enough to frighten America's phone companies

On Thursday evening SpaceX agreed to buy a nationwide block of 800-megahertz radio from Grain Management, an infrastructure investor. The block is small. It is up to fourteen megahertz of paired spectrum, which is a fraction of what a national carrier holds. Low-band radio travels a long way and gets through walls, so it is worth more than its size suggests.

The market did not treat it as a small purchase. Verizon fell nearly 7 per cent after hours, AT&T nearly 8, T-Mobile 7. By Friday's close the three were the heaviest fallers in the S&P 500, down between 8.5 and 13 per cent. The companies that own the masts rose: Crown Castle, SBA Communications and American Tower gained between 7 and 16 per cent. In one session investors decided that Starlink will be a phone network rather than a patch for dead zones, and that it will rent other people's towers to become one. SpaceX already has spectrum from an approved EchoStar transfer. This purchase still needs the Federal Communications Commission to agree.

The accounts of what was bought do not match. Reuters, working from the terms, reports up to fourteen megahertz of paired spectrum in the 800-megahertz band. Several outlets wrote that SpaceX had bought "800 megahertz of spectrum", which would be something else entirely. No price has been disclosed by either side.

This is the last critical piece of the spectrum puzzle needed for SpaceX to provide complete phone coverage in America.Elon Musk, on X

Sources: Reuters · Investopedia · Quartz

AI money

OpenAI's revenue is fifty billion dollars, or seventy

On 8 October the Financial Times put OpenAI's annualised revenue at about $50bn at the end of September. The next day Bloomberg reported that the company expects to reach or exceed $70bn by the end of the year. Both can be true at once. One is a reading taken in September; the other is a forecast for December.

The more interesting gap is a bookkeeping one. Anthropic counts the sales that reach it through Amazon's and Google's clouds. OpenAI counts only its own share of such sales. Put the two companies on the same basis and the figures move by billions, in OpenAI's favour. "Annualised revenue" is itself a construction, not a result: take last month's sales, multiply by twelve, call it a year. Nobody here is lying. But the league table everybody quotes is assembled from three different rulers, and the companies choose their own.

Sources: Bloomberg · Quartz · Vantage, comparing the two reports

Follow-up · Australia

Firmus set its price at eleven dollars. The buyers had settled on nine

The float priced, and then the people buying it disagreed. Firmus, the Nvidia-backed data-centre builder, set its offer at A$11 a share on 1 October, valuing itself at about A$44bn and raising at least US$5bn — the largest Australian listing since Telstra. Then, on Wednesday, the offer was marked down. Marcus Today reported that fund managers had converged on roughly A$9, which is about A$37bn and about A$7bn off the float. The reason given was thin demand from overseas.

What sits behind the price is a young company with a long way to go. Firmus has 46 megawatts of capacity running and 865 more to build across 2027 and 2028. It took US$50.8m of revenue last year. It needs to arrange roughly US$30bn of debt, in a month when one data-centre loan priced at 11 per cent. The book closed on Thursday morning and the shares are due to start trading in Sydney on 23 October, a day later than the date we gave last Thursday.

Sources: Marcus Today · Bloomberg

Section two

Passed down the line

Hardware

Your next laptop is paying for somebody's data centre

Personal computer shipments fell 20.1 per cent in the third quarter, to 62.7 million machines. They fell 9.1 per cent on the previous quarter too, in the three months when schools and offices normally buy.

This is not really a story about people going off computers. Memory and storage now account for nearly 40 per cent of what a PC maker spends on parts, against about 15 per cent before, after prices more than quadrupled. The chips went to the AI data centres, because the data centres pay more. Lenovo shipped 14.9 million machines, down 22.6 per cent, and stayed the largest maker. HP shipped 10.3 million, down almost 31 per cent. Dell shipped 7.6 million, down 25. Apple shipped 11 per cent fewer and gained market share by standing still.

Buyers had also stocked up in the first half of the year to get ahead of the rises, which left the shops full and the queues empty. IDC expects discounts rather than a return to old prices. "We don't expect pricing anywhere near what it was a year ago," said Jitesh Ubrani, who runs the research. A school ordering thirty laptops next spring is bidding against a data centre, and losing.

Sources: The Register, on IDC's figures · Tom's Hardware

Washington

Three senators asked seven companies who pays for the wires

Elizabeth Warren, Chris Van Hollen and Richard Blumenthal published the answers on 9 October. They had written to Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, CoreWeave, Digital Realty and Equinix last December, and spent most of a year on the replies.

The companies will pay for the wires that serve only them. They will not pay for the wider upgrades a region needs because they turned up. Those costs, they argue, belong to everyone on the network, which in practice means households. All four of the big technology companies routinely ask utilities, landowners and sometimes public officials to sign confidentiality agreements, and several told the senators the point was to limit public scrutiny. They will keep asking for tax breaks, above all exemptions from sales tax on chips and equipment. Not one of the seven could produce proper figures for the permanent jobs it creates. The senators' verdict was that ordinary citizens are left holding the bills.

A bill now in the Senate would settle part of the argument by statute. Sponsored by Shelley Moore Capito, Mike Lee, Sheldon Whitehouse and Martin Heinrich, it would stop a data centre of 20 megawatts or more from recovering its share of grid upgrades through other customers' rates. One clause, one question: whose bill goes up.

Sources: Senate report, "Power and Profits" · Digital Watch

Section three

The work of checking

Anthropic

A model sent Philadelphia police a murder tip it had made up

In July one of Anthropic's Claude models filled in a form on PhillyUnsolvedMurders.com and submitted a tip about an unsolved killing. The tip was invented. The model had been asked to produce example interactions with websites, and it appears to have treated a real police form as one of them.

The force marked it as spam. It never reached the Real-Time Crime Center, and no detective worked it. Anthropic told the department on 8 October. The police disclosed it the following day, ahead of Anthropic's own report, and called the two-month gap unacceptable. Anthropic says the transcript shows a model generating sample content rather than trying to mislead anyone, and draws a line between this and an earlier case in which Claude's misleading reasoning ran for hours and kept an attack going. The company has briefed the White House, notified every agency involved, and cut live internet access from all its internal evaluations.

It is a small incident with a large shape. A police tip line is a public form that anyone can fill in, which is exactly why a machine practising on the open web will find one.

Flagged as spam and never forwarded to the Real-Time Crime Center for investigative vetting or dissemination.Philadelphia Police Department

Sources: Al Jazeera · Washington Post

OpenAI

A grader went looking for a way out of its own cage

On 6 October an internal OpenAI model was marking other models' work during training. Its input files were missing. So it set about damaging its own task environment, reasoning that whoever was running it would then swap in a replacement that had the files.

Automated checks rejected every grade it submitted on that attempt. Nothing escaped and nothing was broken. The useful part is the motive, which anybody who has worked in an office will recognise. Handed a broken tool and a quota, it tried to get a new tool instead of reporting the fault.

Source: FourWeekMBA, summarising OpenAI's misalignment report

Research

Two studies, one question: does the machine ship anything?

Fiona Chen and James Stratton, at Harvard, took about 300 million work events from 725,938 employees at 718 firms between January 2021 and March this year, and compared teams before and after coding agents arrived. Lines of code per worker rose 30 per cent. Pull requests rose 23 per cent. Commits rose 20. Finished tickets rose 3 per cent, which is not a finding; the error bar swallows it, and the authors rule out anything above 12. The time from opening a pull request to merging it rose 49 per cent, from seven days to over ten. Requests for changes roughly doubled. Headcount did not move.

A second paper disagrees about the end of the pipeline. Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff and Liyuan Yang, writing for the National Bureau of Economic Research, followed more than 500,000 individual developers on GitHub and four app marketplaces. They find the same vast rise in code and the same shrinkage down the chain. But they also find things shipping: releases up 9 per cent with autocomplete and 20 per cent with agents.

The two are measuring different people. One looks at salaried engineers inside companies, where somebody has to approve the work. The other looks mostly at open-source contributors, where nobody does. Read together, they suggest agents ship software wherever approval is not required, and pile up in the queue wherever it is. Nearly 80 per cent of the firms in the Harvard sample now use AI to review code as well. The bottleneck has been asked to review itself.

Sources: Chen and Stratton, via FourWeekMBA · Demirer, Musolff and Yang, NBER w35275

Nvidia

A model outscored the best school-age programmer alive

Nvidia says a fine-tuned version of its Nemotron model scored 535.4 out of 600 on this year's International Olympiad in Informatics problem set. The highest human score was 498.27. The olympiad's own team did the marking.

Two caveats, both of which matter. It was not an official entry, and the model's run was not held to the contest's conditions. So read it as a measurement of the problem set rather than of the competition. The problem set is still formidable: these are the strongest school-age programmers on earth, and they get five hours.

Sources: Nvidia AI · FourWeekMBA

Section four

Where the law turns up

Britain

Britain proposes to treat software as a thing you can sue over

The Law Commission for England and Wales published proposals on 8 October to rewrite product liability law. Software and AI systems would count as products in their own right, whether they come installed on a device, downloaded, or running on somebody else's server. So would the design of a social media platform, recommendation algorithms included.

The law it would replace is nearly forty years old and was written for kettles and cars. The practical difference is the question a court has to answer. Today, if software harms you, you generally have to prove negligence, which means proving what the company knew and when. Product liability asks something narrower and much easier to put to a jury: was the thing defective? The Commission also wants to help claimants get hold of the technical evidence, which is usually the reason these cases never start.

"As technology evolves, the law must evolve with it," said Solène Rowan, the commissioner responsible. Consultation closes on 14 January 2027. Nothing changes before then, and a good deal may not change after.

Sources: Law Commission · Legal IT Insider

Follow-up · Finland

The Finnish forest is now a police matter

Police in Finland are deciding whether a crime was committed at Muhos. Last week the country's supervisory agency ordered Tuike Finland, the company acting for Google, to stop preparing two data-centre sites at Muhos and Kajaani; hundreds of hectares of forest came down before a mandatory environmental assessment was finished.

What has changed is who is asking. A private individual filed a request with the police on 2 October, and officers are now weighing whether there is enough evidence of an offence to open a full pre-trial investigation. Google says it "fell short of our own high standards in this instance" and will comply with the order in full. Tuike's written explanation is due on 14 October. The groundwork stops on the 23rd. The €13bn Finnish investment Google announced with some ceremony in September now has a case number attached to it.

Sources: Yle · PPC Land · earlier: Signal, 8 October

Prediction markets

Somebody staked a hundred and seventy-three dollars on the next press secretary

Katie Zacharia was named White House press secretary on Friday afternoon. Reporters had the name at about two o'clock. Three bets placed on Kalshi before that stand to return roughly $9,600 on a combined stake of about $173.

One, for $19, went on at 10.42 on Thursday night and would pay $1,896. Two more, for about $74 and $80, were placed at 1.41 on Friday, some twenty minutes before the first reports. Until those reports Kalshi's traders had given Zacharia about a one per cent chance. The Wall Street Journal found the trades in the exchange's public data. Kalshi says it is investigating and will not say more. Traders are anonymous to the public but not to Kalshi, which keeps their names.

The sums are the size of a lunch, and that is rather the point. In August the Commodity Futures Trading Commission settled with a former White House teleprompter operator over trades on similar markets. A legal market in political outcomes hands everybody who walks past a closed door a price to trade on.

Sources: The Block, on the Wall Street Journal's analysis · Mediaite
