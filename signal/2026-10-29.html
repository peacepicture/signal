Closes of Wed 28 Oct, except the Kospi (intraday Thu 29 Oct) and the 10-year, which is the Federal Reserve's H.15 figure for Mon 26 Oct. Nothing here is investment advice.

Canada raised interest rates on Wednesday for the first time in more than three years. The reason sits in a strait that has now been shut for 241 days.

Oil is doing the tightening now. Brent is still above $115, roughly 60 per cent dearer than before the war. Canada's central bank raised rates on Wednesday to stop the price of petrol leaking into the price of everything else. Britain's budget watchdog doubled its inflation forecast for next year. Two of the Federal Reserve's own voters wanted a rise and were outvoted. The European Central Bank is expected to resume raising within weeks.

The same week, the companies that borrow the most money on earth opened their books. Alphabet earned $26.5bn in three months and still ended the quarter $9bn short of cash. Microsoft, spending nearly as hard, did not. Oracle promised investors it would stop borrowing, and its shares rose eleven per cent in a day. Dearer money and enormous building programmes do not sit together comfortably, and this was the week the market started to say so out loud.

01

Priced in oil

Follow-up

Four tankers a day, where there used to be eighty-five

Iran said on Wednesday that tankers could move through the Strait of Hormuz again within four or five days. The condition is that Washington lifts its naval blockade of Iranian ports. The strait has been closed for 241 days. When we wrote on Monday, Brent had just closed at $116.72 and the American president was asking aloud whether to make a deal with Tehran or finish it off.

Little has moved since, and that is the news. Talks in Tehran on 26 October ended with no ceasefire and no joint statement. The IMF's PortWatch counted four tanker transits on 18 October. The normal number is eighty-five. Underwriters now want between 6 and 10 per cent of a ship's value to insure a single passage; before the war they wanted a quarter of one per cent. On that arithmetic, insuring one voyage of a large tanker costs more than the ship earns from it. The International Maritime Organization said on 27 October that the route holds mines and it cannot promise anyone a safe crossing.

Brent settled at $115.32 on Wednesday, up 1.3 per cent. Betting markets put the odds of normal traffic resuming by the end of the year at about one in eight. Washington has said it will not strike Iran before the midterm elections on 3 November.

Sources: Strait of Hormuz tracker · reporting on Iran's conditions, 28 Oct

Ottawa

Canada raised rates to stop petrol becoming everything else

The Bank of Canada raised its overnight rate by a quarter point to 2.5 per cent on Wednesday. It was the first increase since July 2023. Canadian consumer prices have run near 3 per cent since April and the bank now expects about 3.5 per cent by Christmas. It named the cause: persistently high oil prices.

Strip out petrol and Canadian inflation was 2.2 per cent in September. Core measures sit close to 2 per cent. Nobody gets to strip out petrol at the pump, which is the bank's problem. Governing Council said it had considered holding, then judged that the risk of fuel costs spreading into other prices had grown. Businesses have absorbed most of their higher input costs so far. The bank expects them to stop absorbing them over the next year.

The statement warned that "there could be a need to increase the policy rate further". Governor Tiff Macklem said decisions would come "one meeting at a time". Canada now expects inflation to average 3 per cent through 2027 and to reach 2 per cent only around the middle of 2028.

Source: Bank of Canada statement, 28 October

Follow-up

Two votes at the Fed were for a rise, and they lost

The Federal Reserve left its target range at 3.75 to 4 per cent on Wednesday. The vote was ten to two. Both dissenters, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, wanted a quarter-point rise. A hawkish dissent is rare; two in the same meeting is rarer. Three weeks ago the September minutes put a rise back on the table. Now two members have tried to put one on the board.

The statement said activity is expanding strongly, that job gains are keeping pace with the workforce, and that inflation remains elevated. Chair Kevin Warsh told reporters that the "monetary medicine" was "starting to work some, but there's no firm conclusions". Asked whether next week's elections had weighed on the room, he said he had spent two days upstairs with his colleagues and "didn't hear an utterance about politics".

The committee will deliver price stability.Kevin Warsh, Federal Reserve chair, 28 October

Markets took the hint. Futures now price roughly an 85 per cent chance of a quarter-point rise on 9 December, against about 80 per cent a week ago. The ten-year Treasury yield rose back above 5 per cent, closing near 5.04 per cent; the Federal Reserve's own H.15 release put it at 5.00 per cent on Monday, with the effective funds rate at 3.88 per cent. Shares barely moved: the S&P 500 closed up 0.28 per cent at 7,743.98, a third straight gain.

Sources: FOMC statement, 28 Oct · Federal Reserve H.15 · press conference, Yahoo Finance

Westminster

Britain taxed its banks to pay for the heating

John Healey delivered his first budget on Wednesday. The headline measure doubles the bank surcharge from 3 to 6 per cent for fifteen months from January. The surcharge is an extra slice of corporation tax that only banks pay, on top of the 25 per cent everyone pays. It will raise about £1.2bn.

The money goes out again as heating. Twenty million households in council tax bands A to D get a £100 Household Energy Payment this winter, costing £2.6bn. The VAT cut on electricity runs to July, worth about £50 off the annual cap. Gas standing charges disappear next year for some 23 million homes, saving a typical account about £45. The 5p cut in fuel duty is extended to July 2027. Readers who can do arithmetic will notice that £1.2bn from the banks does not cover £2.6bn of cheques; the rest comes from borrowing, which rises by £12.4bn over three years.

The forecasts are the story. The Office for Budget Responsibility now expects consumer price inflation of 3.5 per cent next year. Its previous forecast was 2 per cent. Growth slows from about 1.25 per cent this year to 1 per cent next. Debt interest, which cost Britain £39bn in 2019-20, costs £109bn this year and is forecast to reach £160bn by 2031-32. About £14bn of this year's bill is down to the Middle East. The chancellor keeps £15bn of headroom against his own rule for 2029-30, which the OBR describes as narrow.

Gilt yields eased. Bank shares went up: NatWest and Lloyds by 1.5 per cent, Barclays by 1.1 per cent. The City had feared worse than a surcharge.

Sources: ITV News on the measures · OBR forecast analysis

Paris and Frankfurt

France borrows at 4.94 per cent. Germany pays 3.37.

A spread is simply the gap between what two governments pay to borrow. France's gap over Germany reached 174.6 basis points on 22 October, its widest since the euro crisis, and stood at 156.6 on Wednesday. A basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point. The average over the past year is 79.8, so the gap has roughly doubled.

French ten-year debt now costs 4.94 per cent, close to a twenty-four-year high. Germany's costs 3.37. The reason is domestic: no majority for a budget, a debt pile nobody has a plan for, and suggestions from parts of the left that French debt held by the central bank could simply be written off. Every French mortgage and company loan is priced off that yield, which is how a parliamentary stalemate reaches a kitchen table in Lille.

The European Central Bank meets on Thursday and is expected to hold its deposit rate at 2.5 per cent. It has already raised twice since June, and money markets price three more rises, which would take the rate to 3.25 per cent. Intervention in French bonds looks unlikely for now; the bank's untested Transmission Protection Instrument sits in the drawer. There is also a vacancy question. Reuters reported that the World Economic Forum could name Christine Lagarde its next chair as early as November, a year before her term ends. Klaas Knot and Pablo Hernández de Cos are the names mentioned to replace her.

Sources: OAT–Bund spread, 28 Oct · Reuters on the ECB and Lagarde

02

Profit, and no cash

Mountain View and Redmond

Google made twenty-six billion dollars and still ran short

Alphabet earned a net profit of $26.5bn in the three months to September. It ended the quarter $9.07bn short of cash. Both figures are from the same press release.

The gap is building work. Alphabet's operations generated $42.3bn of cash. It spent $51.4bn on property and equipment, against $24bn in the same quarter last year. Free cash flow is what is left after a company has paid for the buildings and machines it bought; it was negative $9.07bn, worse than the negative $5.9bn of the previous quarter. Profit is an accounting number. This one is closer to the bank balance.

The rest of the quarter was strong. Revenue rose 25 per cent to $127.6bn. Google Cloud more than doubled, to $30.7bn from $15.2bn, and operating income rose 30 per cent to $40.6bn. Earnings per share fell to $2.12 from $2.87, because an $8.7bn paper loss on shares Alphabet holds in other companies took 55 cents off. Shares fell as much as 10 per cent after hours.

Microsoft reported the same evening and was rewarded for the same behaviour. Revenue up 19 per cent to $92.3bn, net profit up 32 per cent to $36.6bn, Azure up 48 per cent, and future contracted work of $677bn, up 73 per cent. Microsoft also doubled its building bill, to $39.4bn from $19.4bn. It stayed cash-positive. Its shares rose 2.6 per cent after hours. The difference between a 10 per cent fall and a 2.6 per cent rise was not growth, and was not AI. It was nine billion dollars of cash.

Sources: Alphabet Q3 2026 release · Microsoft FY27 Q1 release · Fortune

Austin

Oracle promised to stop borrowing, and the shares jumped

Oracle told investors on Wednesday that it will raise no more than $20bn from capital markets between now and 2030. Half equity, half debt, with refinancing of existing borrowing excluded. From the 2029 financial year, it says, growth pays for itself.

This was an answer to a question the bond market had been asking loudly. Two weeks ago it cost a record 288 basis points a year to insure Oracle's debt against default, more than any other large technology borrower, and lenders had begun pricing it like a company in trouble. Oracle's reply was to cap the borrowing and publish a schedule.

The targets are large. Revenue of $225bn or more in the year to May 2030, against at least $90bn this year, which is growth above 35 per cent a year for four years. Operating margin above 40 per cent. Earnings per share above $21. Free cash flow turns positive in the second half of the 2028 financial year and reaches $30bn in 2029, with net debt below 3.5 times earnings. Capital spending peaks in 2027 and 2028, then falls. Customers will prepay, and will bring some of their own hardware.

Shares rose about 11 per cent. Vertiv, which sells the power and cooling equipment that goes inside these buildings, rose nearly 17 per cent the same day on its own forecasts. The market will believe a plan that ends in cash, even one that takes four years to get there.

Sources: Oracle Investor Day targets · market report, 28 Oct

Pittsburgh

America's largest gas producer was assembled for the data centres

EQT agreed on Monday to buy Range Resources for $12.8bn in shares, a premium of nearly 21 per cent. Range's owners take 27 per cent of the combined company, which closes in the first quarter of 2027. The result produces more than nine billion cubic feet of gas a day and is worth about $50bn including debt, making it the biggest gas producer in the United States.

What comes with it is 2.8 million acres in Appalachia, more than thirty years of drilling left, and 3,700 miles of pipe — further than the width of the continent. Chief executive Toby Rice called the company "America's natural gas champion" and said it could grow to seventeen and eventually twenty billion cubic feet a day.

The customers are the point. Gas-fired power stations are what data centres plug into when the grid cannot carry them, and the export terminals on the Gulf Coast are loading for a Europe and an Asia that are short of gas because the Gulf is shut. Both halves of this edition meet in one merger. The risks, as Infralogic's Patrick Harris pointed out, are the three ways that stops: peace in Ukraine, a reopened strait, or an AI build-out that slows down. Range shares rose 11 per cent on the news. EQT's fell 8 per cent.

Source: Fortune, 26 October

03

Eighty cents in the dollar

Follow-up

The memory makers are keeping four fifths of what they charge

SK hynix reported an operating margin of 80 per cent on Tuesday. For every dollar of memory chips it sold last quarter, eighty cents was profit before tax and interest. Revenue was 104.6 trillion won, the first quarter above a hundred trillion, and 328 per cent higher than a year ago. Operating profit was 83.3 trillion won, up 632 per cent.

Samsung followed on Thursday with its own records: revenue of 195.3 trillion won, up 127 per cent, and operating profit of 107.4 trillion won, up 783 per cent. Its margin was 55 per cent against 14.1 per cent a year earlier. The mechanism is simple and worth stating plainly. Samsung's memory prices rose about 20 per cent on the quarter for DRAM and by the high teens for flash, while the number of chips it shipped grew only in single digits. It sold barely more and charged a great deal more.

Someone pays those prices, and some of it is Samsung. Its phone division sold more handsets on the Galaxy Z Fold8 and watched the gain disappear into the cost of the memory inside them. The company expects mobile memory to stay short and dear.

Two threads close here. SK hynix formally abandoned the stock market listing of Solidigm, its flash-memory arm, which it shelved on Monday at a valuation of up to $150bn; chief executive Kwak Noh-Jung said the company had "discontinued our review". Instead it will hand shareholders about 100 trillion won this year — at least 30 trillion in dividends and 60 trillion in buybacks — including a special dividend out of gains on its stake in Kioxia. It is sitting on 98.7 trillion won of net cash.

The market's response to all this was to sell. The Kospi touched a record 6,100.11 on Thursday morning, then fell to about 5,920, down 1.5 per cent. Samsung dropped 5 per cent, having risen 9 per cent the day before, because its 30 trillion won dividend was exactly what analysts had already assumed and the rest of its shareholder plan waits for a January board meeting. Korea's finance ministry said it would respond immediately if volatility worsened.

Sources: SK hynix 3Q26 results · Edaily on Samsung and the Kospi · DigitalToday on Samsung's divisions

Hefei

China is making the chip anyway, and discarding most of it

Two or three of every ten advanced memory chips that ChangXin Memory makes are good enough to sell. Reuters reported the figure on Wednesday, citing people familiar with the company. CXMT is China's largest maker of DRAM, and it plans to begin limited production of HBM3E within two months. Samples are already with Chinese AI chip designers.

High-bandwidth memory is ordinary memory stacked in towers and wired to sit beside an AI processor. The stacking is the hard part, and it is where the chips fail. SK hynix, which leads the market, gets around 70 per cent of its equivalent chips out of the door. CXMT is throwing away three in four and will keep going, because American export controls have made the alternative unbuyable and Beijing wants its own answer to Nvidia. Its chips reportedly perform closer to SK hynix's previous generation than its current one.

The scale of the demand explains the stubbornness. A single project of Stargate's size needs hundreds of thousands of extra DRAM wafers a month, roughly 40 per cent of world output. SK hynix, Samsung, Kioxia and CXMT are together worth more than $2 trillion. Four companies now supply about a third of what the big technology firms spend on data centres, and one of them is deliberately running at a quarter of the yield.

Source: Gizmodo, on Reuters reporting, 28 October

04

Nothing to do with the money

Tokyo

A Japanese internet company told its customers to unplug

GMO Internet asked its customers this week to disconnect their computers from the network, shut them down and pull out the cables. It is an unusual instruction from a company whose business is keeping computers running.

The sequence was quick. On 20 October GMO found malicious software in some customer server environments. At 4am on 21 October it shut down every plan of its Onamae Desktop Cloud service, which rents virtual desktops to financial traders, and identified 26,739 servers as possibly affected. The same day an email arrived demanding about ¥8bn, roughly $50m. GMO went to the police on 22 October and refused to pay, saying payment would guarantee neither the deletion of the stolen data nor its non-use. The attackers then set a deadline: publish the customer data and trigger ransomware at noon on 27 October.

At 12.30pm material said to be GMO's and its customers' appeared online. At 1.55pm the company said no ransomware attack had been confirmed and that it was still checking whether the published files were real. Customers were told to reset passwords after reports of unauthorised activity on trading accounts. The people being told to unplug are traders, and the machines they were unplugging were not on their desks. They were in GMO's data centre, rented by the month. Japan's Financial Services Agency responded by asking firms for better "vulnerability management, defence-in-depth and incident preparedness", which is what regulators say when the basics have failed.

Sources: TechCrunch · timeline of GMO's notices

London

DeepMind's new model builds a world that answers back

Google DeepMind released Genie 4 on Tuesday to a small group of testers. Describe a place and it generates it around you as you move. Then change it by asking: shift the camera, bring in rain, add a person. The people it adds now hold a conversation in real time, and every scene comes with its own ambient sound and music.

The number that matters is two minutes. That is how long a scene holds together before it starts to drift, and it is twice what Genie 3 managed a year ago. Underneath, two models share the work: Gemini plans the steps and checks the frames, Genie draws them. Research director Tejas Kulkarni described it as a hybrid, with Gemini writing code and text while the world model handles the pictures. Access reaches Google AI Ultra subscribers through Project Genie in the coming weeks, and early-access partners through the Gemini API.

DeepMind was unusually frank about what does not work. Objects wander the longer a simulation runs. Nobody has solved keeping the audio accurate, or making text inside the world legible. Diego Rivas, an engineer on the project, said the end-to-end product experience is not yet clear. The company frames world simulation as a step towards general intelligence and names robot training as a use: a machine can practise in a world that costs nothing to break. Two minutes is not a world. It is, however, twice as much world as last year.

Sources: Google DeepMind announcement · TechCrunch
